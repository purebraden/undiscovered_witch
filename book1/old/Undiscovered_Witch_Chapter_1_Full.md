# CHAPTER 1 — Senior Year

Allison woke up before her alarm went off.

She lay still for a moment, staring at the faint crack in the ceiling above her bed—the one that looked like a sideways question mark if she tilted her head just right. The house was quiet in the way it always was at that hour, not asleep exactly, just waiting. Pipes hummed softly somewhere in the walls. The heat clicked on, then off again.

She reached for her phone, careful not to move too quickly. The screen lit up her room in pale blue.

6:02 a.m.

Her alarm wasn’t set to go off until 6:15. It never mattered. She almost always woke up early on school days, like her body had learned not to expect rest when there was something scheduled later. She told herself she liked the extra time. That it made mornings easier.

She stayed in bed anyway, scrolling without really reading—headlines she’d forget, messages she’d already answered, weather she already knew. It was supposed to rain today. The little cloud icon showed a sixty percent chance, which felt vague enough to be polite.

Eventually, she sat up and swung her feet onto the floor. The carpet was cold. She stood there for a second longer than necessary, grounding herself, then pulled on a hoodie and padded down the hall.

Emily’s door was closed. Allison paused outside it, listening out of habit. Silence. That meant Emily was still asleep, curled sideways across her bed, probably having kicked off at least one blanket during the night. Allison smiled faintly and moved on.

In the kitchen, her mother was already up, standing at the counter with a mug of coffee cradled between both hands. She looked tired in the gentle, permanent way adults often did—like tiredness had become a feature rather than a condition.

“Morning,” Allison said.

Her mother glanced over and smiled. “You’re up early.”

“I couldn’t sleep,” Allison said automatically. It wasn’t a lie. It just wasn’t the whole truth.

Her mother nodded like that made sense. “I made oatmeal. It’s still warm.”

“Thanks.”

Allison took a bowl from the cabinet and filled it, careful not to clink the spoon too loudly. Her mother went back to staring out the window over the sink, watching the sky lighten inch by inch.

“You have a quiz today, right?” her mother asked.

“Maybe,” Allison said. “In English.”

Her mother hummed sympathetically. “You’ll do fine.”

Allison nodded, even though she hadn’t studied. She hadn’t needed to. That was part of the problem, if she let herself think about it too long.

Her father came in a few minutes later, already dressed for work, jacket folded over one arm. He leaned down and kissed the top of Allison’s head as he passed her, a soft, practiced motion.

“Morning, kiddo.”

“Morning.”

He poured himself coffee, then glanced at the clock. “Emily up yet?”

“No,” Allison said. “I’ll wake her.”

“You don’t have to—”

“It’s okay,” Allison said quickly. “I’ve got time.”

Her father hesitated, then smiled. “Thanks.”

She finished her oatmeal, rinsed the bowl, and headed back down the hall. Emily’s door creaked faintly when Allison nudged it open. Emily was sprawled diagonally across her bed, hair fanned everywhere, one arm flung dramatically over her face.

“All right,” Allison said softly. “Rise and shine.”

Emily groaned. “It’s too early.”

“It’s almost seven.”

“That’s early.”

Allison laughed quietly and nudged Emily’s foot with her own. “If you don’t get up now, Mom’s going to come in here, and she won’t be nice about it.”

Emily peeled one eye open. “You’re lying.”

“I promise I’m not.”

Emily sighed with the exaggerated suffering of someone who had never known real hardship and rolled onto her side. “Five more minutes.”

“Three,” Allison said.

“Four.”

“Three,” Allison repeated, firmer this time.

Emily glared at her for a second, then sat up, hair sticking out in every direction. “You’re the worst.”

“I know.”

Emily smiled despite herself.

By the time they were both downstairs again, Emily was talking nonstop—about a test she didn’t want to take, a friend who had looked at her funny the day before, a rumor that someone in eighth grade had gotten suspended for vaping in the bathroom. Allison listened, nodding at the right places, offering occasional murmurs of agreement.

Their mother moved around them, packing lunches, checking lists, reminding Emily to grab her jacket.

“Allison,” she said, turning suddenly. “Did you remember your college forms?”

“Yes,” Allison said.

Her mother hesitated. “You’re sure?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.” Her mother smiled again, relieved. “I just don’t want you to fall behind.”

“I won’t,” Allison said. She never did.

Outside, the air was cool and damp, the sky heavy but undecided. Allison and Emily walked together as far as the footbridge before splitting off—Emily turning toward the middle school, Allison continuing on toward the high school.

“Don’t forget I have practice late,” Emily called.

“I know,” Allison said. “I’ll be there.”

Emily grinned and ran ahead, already forgetting the conversation.

The creek ran low beneath the bridge, water sliding lazily over smooth stones. Allison paused in the middle, resting her hands on the railing. The air smelled like wet leaves and earth. She breathed it in, slow and steady, until the tightness she hadn’t realized she was carrying eased.

She didn’t linger too long. Being late made people worry.

---

The hallway outside Allison’s locker was quieter than usual when she arrived. Most mornings were a rush of noise and bodies and backpacks swinging dangerously close to heads. Today, it felt like the building was holding its breath.

She stopped in front of her locker and frowned.

“That’s weird,” she murmured.

It opened on the first try.

She stared at it for a second longer than necessary, then shook her head and swapped out her books. It was nothing. Sometimes lockers behaved.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. She pulled it out, thumb hovering over the screen. No new messages. Just a notification from the weather app.

Rain expected later this afternoon.

She slid the phone back into her pocket and headed to class.

English passed quietly. The quiz her mother had mentioned never happened. Instead, the teacher spent most of the period answering questions about college essays and reminding them how fast senior year would go.

“Don’t wish it away,” she said, smiling sadly. “You’ll miss it when it’s gone.”

Allison wrote that down, even though she wasn’t sure she believed it.

In the hallway between classes, she nearly collided with Kara Bennett.

“Oh—sorry,” Allison said automatically.

Kara looked at her, startled, then smiled. “It’s okay.”

The smile lingered a second longer than necessary.

Allison walked away feeling faintly disoriented, like she’d stepped off a moving walkway she hadn’t known she was on.

At lunch, she sat at her usual table near the windows. Her friends—if that was the right word—filtered in one by one. They talked about college acceptances, about who was dating who, about a teacher who might be retiring.

Allison listened.

At one point, someone asked her a question—something about her plans after graduation. She answered honestly, vaguely. She hadn’t decided yet. There were options. There was time.

“That’s smart,” someone said. “Not rushing it.”

Allison smiled, relieved.

When the bell rang at the end of the day, the sky outside the windows was still dry.

---

She walked home instead of taking the bus, cutting down toward the creek again. Her phone felt heavy in her pocket. She pulled it out as she walked, thumb moving without conscious thought.

She opened her notes app.

She stared at the blank screen for a long moment, then typed:

> **Probably Nothing**  
>  
> Locker opened first try.  
> Quiz didn’t happen.  
> Kara smiled.  
>  
> Could be coincidence.

She hesitated, then saved it.

At home, the house was empty. Allison dropped her bag by the door and went to the kitchen, starting on homework she didn’t really need to do. She worked until the light outside shifted and the house filled again—her mother’s keys, her father’s footsteps, Emily’s loud retelling of her day.

Dinner was uneventful. Emily complained about practice. Their parents listened. Allison passed the salt.

Later, she lay on her bed, staring at the ceiling crack again. Her phone buzzed.

A text from Kara.

*Hey, do you have the English homework from today?*

Allison stared at the screen, heart doing a strange, unnecessary flutter.

*Yeah,* she typed. *I’ll send it.*

She did, then set the phone face-down on the bed.

She didn’t add a note about it.

Not yet.

Outside, the rain never came.
