# Prologue

# PROLOGUE — What She Was For

Allison learned early that crying didn’t make things better.

Emily cried like it was a language. Loud, sharp sounds that filled the house and demanded an answer. When Emily cried, doors opened. Voices rose. Someone always came running.

Allison sat on the third step from the bottom of the staircase and waited.

She was nine. Emily was four. Their mother was in the kitchen, their father somewhere down the hall. Allison could hear drawers opening too hard, the scrape of a chair leg against tile.

Emily’s crying hit a higher pitch.

“I want it,” Emily shouted. “I *want* it.”

Allison didn’t know what *it* was. That didn’t seem important.

She knew what would happen next.

“Emily,” their mother said, already tired. “We’ve talked about this.”

Emily screamed again. Their father appeared in the doorway, rubbing his face, eyes unfocused like he’d been interrupted mid-thought.

Allison stood up.

She didn’t hurry. She didn’t sigh. She didn’t say anything at all until she reached the kitchen.

“It’s okay,” Allison said quietly.

Everyone paused.

Her mother looked at her then—really looked, like she’d just noticed Allison was there. “Sweetheart, you don’t have to—”

“I can help,” Allison said.

Emily was red-faced, fists clenched, shaking with feeling. Allison crouched in front of her.

“What’s wrong?” she asked.

Emily pointed at the cabinet. “The blue cup.”

The blue cup was in the dishwasher.

Allison stood, opened the dishwasher, and handed it to her.

The crying stopped so suddenly it felt less like comfort and more like relief settling into place.

Their mother exhaled. Their father laughed softly, relieved.

“See?” their mother said. “Your sister knows how to calm you down.”

Emily ran off with the cup, already distracted.

Allison stayed where she was.

“That was very mature,” her father said. “You’re such a good kid.”

Their mother smiled at her. “You always make things easier.”

Allison nodded.

She went back to the stairs and sat down again.

From there, she could hear Emily talking to herself in the living room, happily narrating a game no one else was playing. The house settled. The tension drained away.

Allison rested her chin on her knees.

She felt something then—not pride, exactly. Something steadier.

Relief.

This was what she was for.

If she paid attention.  
If she stayed calm.  
If she didn’t need much.

The house stayed quiet, as if it had been waiting for her to decide that it would.

# Chapter 1

# Chapter 1 — The Girl Who Fits Enough

The first thing Allison did every morning was check her phone.

Not because she was popular. Not because she expected a flood of messages. It was more like… habit. A reflex. The way you touch a bruise to see if it still hurts.

Notifications: three.

A weather alert. A reminder from the school app about attendance. And one from her Notes app, flagged because she’d starred it last night.

Don’t forget: you’re allowed to want things.

Allison stared at the line for a second, then locked her phone and rolled onto her back.

The ceiling above her was smooth except for one small crack near the corner—the kind that looked like a tiny lightning bolt. She’d stared at it so much she’d started to think she could map her moods by it. Some mornings it felt like a threat. Some mornings it was just… there.

Today it was just there.

She swung her legs over the side of the bed and listened.

The house was awake.

Not loudly—just enough. The sound of the coffee maker clicking. A cabinet door closing. The faint hum of the dryer in the laundry room downstairs. And, from the hallway, Emily’s music—thin through her closed door, as if she didn’t want anyone to hear and also wanted everyone to hear.

Allison pulled on her favorite sweatshirt, the one that was soft in the sleeves from being washed too many times. It wasn’t stylish. It wasn’t ugly. It was safe.

Safe was most of her wardrobe.

She padded into the bathroom and looked at herself in the mirror.

Her hair was blonde and long, usually too long, the kind that slipped out of ponytails and tangled if you looked at it wrong. She’d never dyed it or cut it dramatically or done any of the things girls in movies did when they wanted to become a new version of themselves. Her eyes were green, a little too bright when the light hit them.

For a second—just a second—she caught herself looking like someone else.

Like a girl in a book jacket.

Then the thought slipped away and she was just Allison again.

Allison Crites.

The name looked normal on forms. The name sounded normal when teachers called roll. It didn’t carry a story.

Survive. Blend. Don’t make a scene.

It wasn’t that she wanted to be invisible.

It was that she didn’t want to be wrong.

Downstairs, her mom was at the counter with her back turned, hair clipped up, wearing the cardigan she wore when she had early meetings.

“Morning,” Allison said.

Her mom turned. “Hey, honey.”

Her smile landed soft and practiced, like she was laying something carefully on a shelf.

Allison opened the fridge. Milk. Leftovers. A box of strawberries that looked like they were on their last day.

“Did you sleep?” her mom asked.

“Yeah.”

“Good.”

Allison grabbed a yogurt and a spoon and sat at the table.

Her dad came in a minute later, already in his work jacket, keys in hand, eyes flicking to the clock.

“Morning, kiddo,” he said, kissing the top of her head as he passed.

Kiddo.

Not Allison. Not honey. Not sweetheart. Kiddo was what you called someone when you didn’t want to get too specific.

Emily came down a few minutes later like a firework.

She didn’t walk so much as appear—hair messy, pajama pants loud, phone in hand, already talking as if she’d been mid-conversation since midnight.

“Mom, I need the purple hoodie. The one that makes my eyes look good. The gray one makes me look like—like a sad Victorian child.”

Her mom laughed. Real laughter, the kind that showed teeth. “It’s in the dryer. If you’d stop leaving clothes on the floor, you’d know where things were.”

Emily spun dramatically toward Allison. “Did you hear that? She’s attacking me. In the morning.”

Allison lifted her spoon. “I’m witnessing.”

Emily grinned, slid into the chair next to her, and stole one of Allison’s strawberries without asking. Allison didn’t stop her.

Emily was fourteen and somehow made every room feel like it was built for her. She had dark hair that fell in perfect waves like it had a personal assistant. She had her mother’s confidence and her father’s ability to move like the world expected her to. She was loud in a way that read as charming instead of annoying.

If Allison had been a different kind of person, she would’ve hated her.

Sometimes she tried to.

But mostly she just watched her like someone watching a show they weren’t allowed to be in.

“Are you coming to my game Friday?” Emily asked, already chewing.

“Allison has work,” her mom said immediately.

Allison paused.

“I—” she started, but her mom kept going.

“The school store needs her. She’s responsible.”

Emily rolled her eyes. “Of course. Allison is responsible. Allison is always responsible. Allison should get a trophy for being so responsible.”

The words weren’t mean, exactly.

But they landed like a label.

Responsible: the version of Allison people could explain.

Allison swallowed. “I can probably come after.”

Emily brightened. “Really?”

Allison’s dad glanced over. “If you’re not too tired.”

Allison nodded, even though she hadn’t been asked a question.

She looked down at her yogurt, suddenly not hungry.

Something about mornings always made her feel like she was watching other people’s lives happen around her. Like everyone else had a script and she was improvising, hoping no one noticed the hesitation.

The hallway smelled like floor cleaner and too many different perfumes layered on top of each other.

Allison moved through it the way she always did—shoulders slightly tucked in, eyes forward but unfocused, aware without appearing to be.

She preferred being unnoticed.

She paused near the guidance office, leaning her shoulder against the wall where the noise dimmed just enough to breathe.

She pulled out her phone and opened Notes.

# Chapter 2

# Chapter 2 — Things That Go Smoothly

The worst part was that nothing felt dramatic.

If Allison had woken up with a headache, or a sense of dread, or even that hollow, buzzing feeling she sometimes got before exams, she would have trusted it more. Those sensations came with explanations attached—stress, nerves, lack of sleep.

But she woke up feeling… fine.

Not rested. Not energized.

Just fine, in a way that felt strangely undeserved.

She lay still for a moment, staring at the familiar crack in the ceiling. It branched near one corner like it had once considered going somewhere else and changed its mind. She’d stared at it long enough over the years that it felt like a marker for time, proof that things could stay the same even when everything else shifted around them.

The house sounded normal.

Emily’s voice floated down the hallway, fast and animated, like she’d already started living the day before anyone else was fully awake. Downstairs, the coffee maker hissed and clicked. A cabinet closed. Footsteps crossed the kitchen.

Allison waited for the unease from yesterday to rush back all at once.

It didn’t.

That bothered her more than she wanted to admit.

She dressed on autopilot, pulling on clean jeans and a soft sweater she’d worn often enough that it no longer required thought. As she brushed her hair, she checked her reflection only long enough to confirm she looked acceptable—presentable, forgettable, the kind of person teachers nodded at approvingly without remembering later.

She told herself yesterday had been a fluke.

People argued all the time. Teachers forgot students. Bells rang late. None of it meant anything unless you decided it did, and she’d always been careful not to decide things like that.

Breakfast passed the way it usually did: half conversations, overlapping schedules, Emily narrating something Allison only half-followed. Allison answered when spoken to, smiled when expected, and kept her thoughts deliberately unremarkable.

She didn’t mention the stairwell.
She didn’t mention the feeling.

There was nothing to mention.

Outside, the air had that early-morning chill that made everything feel briefly sharper and cleaner than it really was. The walk to school was uneventful. Cars passed. Someone waved at someone else. A dog barked behind a fence.

Normal.

The parking lot buzzed with engines and laughter. Groups clustered near the entrance, phones already out, faces angled toward invisible audiences. Allison walked past them with the familiar relief of not belonging to any one circle strongly enough to be missed.

She told herself she liked it that way.

First period was English.

She slid into her seat just as the bell finished ringing and opened her notebook to a blank page. The room smelled faintly of dry erase markers and too much perfume. Sunlight filtered through the tall windows, catching dust in the air.

Mrs. Harlan stood at the front, flipping through papers.

“Okay,” she said, clapping her hands once. “Cold reads today.”

A collective groan rippled through the room.

Allison felt the familiar tightening in her chest—not panic, just awareness. Cold reads weren’t hard. She read well. Teachers had told her that. But there was something about hearing her own voice fill the room without warning that made her acutely conscious of herself in a way she didn’t enjoy.

She lowered her gaze to the page and traced the margin absently with her pen.

Please don’t call on me, she thought.

The thought barely registered. It was automatic, unexamined, the same way her shoulders tensed when something loud went off nearby.

Mrs. Harlan began calling names from the front row, moving steadily down the seating chart.

Allison listened without really listening, letting the cadence of other voices wash over her. Pages turned. Someone stumbled over a sentence and laughed nervously. Mrs. Harlan corrected pronunciation gently and moved on.

It wasn’t until the reading ended that Allison looked up.

Her name was still untouched.

She frowned.

Mrs. Harlan was already collecting papers, the moment clearly over. Chairs creaked as students shifted. Conversation returned in low murmurs.

Allison flipped through her notes, searching for an explanation. Had she missed something? Had the seating chart changed? Had she spaced out at exactly the wrong moment?

She hadn’t.

The realization arrived late and cold.

That was my turn.

She waited for the correction that never came.

The moment passed.

By the time the bell rang, the relief she expected hadn’t shown up. Instead, something colder settled in her chest, like she’d stepped onto solid ground that hadn’t been there a second before.

Second period was biology.

Group lab day.

Allison slid onto a stool and scanned the room automatically, noting who was already paired off and who wasn’t. She did this every time without thinking, the way you checked traffic before crossing a street.

Her stomach dipped when she saw who was still standing alone.

He wasn’t a bad person. He just didn’t understand the invisible rules everyone else followed instinctively.

Anyone but him, Allison thought.

The thought came sharper this time.

She felt bad for it immediately.

She wasn’t cruel. She wasn’t even unkind. She would have worked with him if she had to. She always did what she was supposed to do.

Mrs. Grant clapped her hands. “Okay, looks like we’ve got one extra today. Let’s shuffle just a bit.”

A girl at the next table groaned. “Seriously? We already set up.”

“Just one swap,” Mrs. Grant said cheerfully. “You’ll live.”

She pointed across the room. “You—go join them. And you—switch places.”

The awkward boy blinked, startled, then gathered his things and shuffled toward the other table.

Someone else slid into the empty seat beside Allison instead.

The movement was quick. Efficient. Over.

Allison stared down at the lab equipment, ears ringing softly.

Relief came first.

Then guilt, hot and immediate.

She hadn’t done anything. She hadn’t said a word. It could have happened regardless of what she’d thought.

And yet the timing clung to her.

By the time the bell rang, the feeling hadn’t gone away.

Third period history blurred past in a haze of dates and names. Allison checked the clock as she rounded the corner near the science wing.

Two minutes.

She knew she was going to be late.

Not disastrously late. Just enough to draw attention.

Just this once, she thought, breathless as she took the stairs two at a time.

She pushed through the classroom door.

No bell.

Mr. Alvarez was still shuffling papers. Students were talking. Someone laughed.

Allison slid into her seat, pulse loud in her ears.

The bell rang thirty seconds later.

Relief came—and then unease followed close behind it.

Between classes, the hallway felt clogged, bodies moving in uneven clumps. Allison nearly ran into someone at the corner and apologized automatically. She reached her locker and leaned back against the cool metal, trying to breathe evenly.

Nothing is happening, she told herself.

Fourth period was math.

She noticed herself almost thinking please don’t call on me and stopped the thought before it finished forming. She stared hard at her notebook, as if concentration could pin her thoughts in place.

Mr. Jacobs scanned the room and called on students.

Not her.

She didn’t feel relieved.

She felt watched—by herself.

Lunch was louder than she expected.

She found Kara at their usual table and slid into the seat across from her. Kara talked easily, complained about a quiz, laughed at something someone else said. Allison laughed in the right places, nodded at the right times.

Normal.

Except the weight of everything she wasn’t saying pressed harder with each passing minute.

She considered telling Kara. Imagined starting with a joke. Imagined Kara laughing, or worrying.

She said nothing.

After school, Allison didn’t go straight home.

She turned toward the narrow path behind the houses and followed it to the creek. The water moved steadily over smooth stones, indifferent and constant. She sat and watched it, hugging her knees.

She didn’t wish.

She didn’t think anything on purpose.

She just let the thoughts come and go until the light shifted and the air grew colder.

Then she went home.

She went to her room.

She opened her phone.

# Chapter 3

# Chapter 3 — The Shape of Consequences

Allison was already tired when she woke up.

Not the heavy kind of tired that came from staying up late, not the one that had a clear cause you could point to and blame. This was the thin, brittle kind that lived in the edges of her day—where sounds felt sharper, where people’s voices had corners, where her own thoughts landed with more weight than they should.

Emily’s alarm went off once. Then again.

Allison stared at the ceiling and waited for Emily to hit snooze a third time, because Emily always did. It was practically a ritual. A pattern. The thought made Allison’s stomach tighten in a way that felt unfair to both of them.

She rolled onto her side and checked the time on her phone.

She didn’t open her notes.

The notes app sat there like an open door in her head, and Allison wasn’t sure she could step through it and come back out again like a normal person. She wasn’t sure she wanted to.

She put the phone down and lay still for another minute, trying to make herself breathe like she hadn’t been counting her thoughts for the last two days.

Downstairs, the coffee maker clicked and hissed. A cabinet closed with a little too much force. Someone—her dad, probably—cleared his throat like he was trying to wake up the house with sound.

Allison got up.

She dressed on autopilot, pulling on jeans and a sweater without looking too closely at herself. Her hair felt heavy, like it was still holding sleep. When she brushed it, she watched the mirror just long enough to make sure her face looked neutral. Fine. Normal. The kind of normal that didn’t ask questions.

In the hallway, Emily’s door cracked open and a bright sliver of light spilled out. Emily appeared in the doorway with her phone already in hand, hair messy in a way that somehow looked intentional anyway.

“You’re up,” Emily said, like this was surprising.

Allison nodded. “I have school.”

Emily smirked. “Oh right. That thing.”

Allison didn’t answer.

Downstairs, breakfast passed the way it usually did.

Emily talked, voice fast and certain, as if she’d already decided what kind of day it was going to be. Their mom moved around the kitchen with the quiet efficiency of someone who was always five minutes behind schedule. Their dad read something on his phone, thumb scrolling, eyes unfocused.

Allison ate her cereal slowly, listening to the clink of spoons and the hum of the refrigerator.

“You’re quiet,” her mom said, not looking up.

Allison shrugged. “Just tired.”

Her mom’s gaze flicked to her, brief and searching, then away again. “Late night?”

“No.”

“Okay,” her mom said, like she’d offered a door and Allison had chosen not to go through it.

Emily laughed at something on her screen, loud enough that Allison’s shoulders tightened.

Allison hated that her body reacted like that. She hated that she could feel herself bracing for her own family.

Her dad finally looked up. “You got everything for today?”

“Yeah,” Allison said. “Homework, binder, everything.”

“Good,” he said, and went back to his phone.

Allison stared at her bowl. The cereal had gone soggy.

She told herself she was being dramatic.

She told herself that middle-of-the-road girls didn’t get to be dramatic. They didn’t get to be interesting. They got to be functional.

She finished breakfast, rinsed her bowl, and left before anyone could ask anything else.

Outside, the air was cold enough to wake her up properly. The kind of cold that made your lungs sting for a second when you breathed in too fast. The sky was pale, washed-out blue, like the day hadn’t decided what color it wanted to be yet.

The walk to school was ordinary—cars passing, someone jogging with headphones, a dog barking behind a fence.

But Allison felt like she was walking with something invisible tucked under her coat.

At school, the parking lot buzzed with the familiar morning energy—engines, laughter, shouting, the sound of teenagers trying to outrun their own lives for a few minutes.

Allison moved through it like she always did: head up, face neutral, eyes not lingering too long on anyone. She was good at being present without being noticed.

The hallway smelled like floor cleaner and cheap body spray. Lockers slammed. Someone argued with someone else about a test they hadn’t studied for. A teacher stood near the office, already tired.

Allison went to her locker, spun the dial, and swapped books with hands that felt slightly clumsy. She told herself she was just tired.

It was fine.

It was all fine.

The hallway near the science wing always felt tighter than the others.

Not physically—there was enough space—but something about the way lockers jutted out unevenly, the way people stopped abruptly to talk, made it feel like you were always navigating around someone else’s urgency. Allison slowed instinctively, adjusting her grip on her bag.

She caught herself thinking about nothing in particular.

That scared her a little.

Lately, nothing felt too open. Too quiet. Like space that invited her thoughts to stretch out farther than she wanted them to.

She focused on the sound of her shoes against the floor. On the hum of voices. On the simple act of walking forward.

Someone brushed past her shoulder without apologizing.

Allison flinched and told herself not to take it personally.

The moment happened between classes.

She didn’t know that at first, of course. No one ever knows when the moment is happening. It always looks like nothing until it’s already past.

Allison was halfway down the hallway near the science wing when she heard the sound—sharp clatter, a hard impact, the kind that makes a dozen people react without meaning to.

A backpack hit the floor.

A binder popped open.

Papers spilled like confetti, sliding across the tile and under shoes.

Allison stopped.

Rachel was on the ground.

Not sprawled, not dramatically, but kneeling awkwardly with one hand braced against the floor and the other reaching for a sheet of paper that had already skated out of reach. Her hair was hanging in her face. Her cheeks were bright red in the way that happens instantly, like embarrassment flips a switch inside the skin.

People slowed.

Not to help. Not right away.

They slowed to look.

A couple of kids laughed—not even loudly, not the cruel movie-villain laugh Allison might have been able to categorize and hate easily. This was casual laughter. Bored laughter. The kind people made when they didn’t think it counted.

Rachel made a small sound, not quite a word. She shoved hair behind her ear and reached again for her papers, movements too fast, too frantic.

Someone stepped on a worksheet without noticing.

Someone else muttered, “Oof,” like they were watching a blooper reel.

Phones appeared.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. One student angled their screen downward, pretending to text while recording anyway. Another lifted their phone a little higher, then lowered it quickly when a teacher’s voice called from somewhere farther down the hall.

Allison stood there with her bag heavy on her shoulder.

She could step forward.

She could kneel down. She could pick up papers. She could say something—anything—that might rupture the moment and give Rachel a way out of it.

She didn’t.

It was only a second.

Maybe less.

But in that second, Allison felt everything at once: the heat in her face, the tightness in her chest, the awareness that everyone was watching, and the terrifying possibility that if she stepped in, they would watch her too.

She told herself she was frozen because she didn’t know what to do.

She told herself that later.

In the moment, it was simpler and uglier:

She didn’t want to be seen.

Rachel grabbed her binder, shoved loose papers into it without sorting them, hands shaking. She looked up once—only once—and Allison caught a glimpse of her eyes.

Not angry.

Not accusing.

Just wide. Humiliated. Trying not to cry.

Allison’s stomach dropped.

A teacher’s voice cut through the hallway like a blade. “Let’s move it, people.”

The crowd broke apart immediately.

Phones vanished. Laughter turned into murmurs. The flow of students resumed like water moving around a rock.

Rachel stood up fast, clutching her binder to her chest, and walked away without looking at anyone again.

Allison moved too.

The rest of the hallway passed in a blur.

Allison reached her classroom without remembering how. She sat down without remembering pulling the chair out. When she opened her notebook, she stared at the page for a full minute before realizing it was upside down.

Her body felt hollow.

Like everything inside her had shifted slightly out of alignment and never quite snapped back.

She kept waiting for a feeling to arrive—panic, anger, grief, something that would make sense.

Nothing did.

That was worse.

The question followed her into class.

Why didn’t I do anything?

In history, Mr. Alvarez’s voice washed over her in facts and dates Allison couldn’t hold onto. She wrote notes anyway, pen moving on habit, her page filling with neat lines that meant nothing.

Every few minutes, her mind snapped back to the hallway.

Rachel’s hands.

The paper sliding under someone’s shoe.

The sound of laughter that barely even tried to be cruel.

And her own stillness.

The bell rang and Allison flinched like she’d been caught doing something wrong.

The hallway between classes felt too narrow. Too loud. Too close. Allison kept her eyes down and moved fast, like speed could erase the last hour.

By lunch, the story had already spread.

Allison saw it in the way people clustered in groups, heads angled toward each other, phones held in hands but not always used. She caught a glimpse of a screen as she passed—Rachel’s hair in her face, the moment frozen mid-motion.

Allison looked away so quickly her neck twinged.

She found Kara at their usual table near the windows. Kara was talking animatedly, eyebrows drawn together, one hand slicing the air as she made a point.

When Kara saw Allison, she paused, then patted the seat beside her.

Allison slid into it, tray balanced carefully, like her hands needed something to do.

Kara leaned in. “Did you see what happened to Rachel?”

Allison swallowed. “Yeah.”

“That was brutal,” Kara said, voice low but intense. “Someone recorded it. It’s everywhere.”

Allison stared at her food. Her appetite had disappeared somewhere between history and the cafeteria line.

“People are awful,” Allison said, and hated how thin her voice sounded.

Kara exhaled. “I feel bad for her. Like—she didn’t do anything. She literally tripped. And now everyone gets to make it a thing.”

Allison nodded.

Kara’s anger made sense.

It had edges. Direction. A target.

Allison watched Kara talk, watched the way her hands moved when she was frustrated, and felt strangely disconnected from it all, like she was observing a reaction she’d missed the instructions for.

Kara could be mad for Rachel.

Allison couldn’t stop being mad at herself.

Part of Allison wanted Kara to stay mad longer. To push harder. To make it matter.

And then Allison hated herself for that too.

Because Rachel was the one who had been on the floor. Rachel was the one whose face people were replaying on their phones.

Allison wasn’t allowed to want anything here.

Kara kept talking, but the conversation drifted, the way lunch conversations always did. Someone mentioned a teacher. Someone complained about a homework assignment. Kara laughed at a joke and the sound felt wrong in Allison’s ears, like laughter didn’t belong in a day that had already been cut open.

Allison tried to stay present. She nodded. She smiled. She made herself swallow bites of food she couldn’t taste.

But she kept seeing Rachel’s eyes.

When lunch ended, Kara stood and bumped Allison’s shoulder lightly. “You good?”

Allison forced a smile. “Yeah. Just tired.”

Kara gave her a look that lingered half a second longer than usual, then shrugged. “Okay. See you later.”

Allison watched her disappear into the crowd and felt the absence like pressure.

The rest of the day moved strangely.

Not in a dramatic way. Not with sudden lightning strikes or teachers shouting or the universe announcing itself.

It moved in the subtle way a room feels different after someone has slammed a door.

In math, Allison stared at the board and realized she couldn’t remember what she’d just read. In biology, she copied answers from the worksheet without understanding them. In English, she wrote down a quote the teacher said would “probably be on the test” and then immediately forgot it.

Every time she looked at someone’s phone, her stomach tightened.

Every time she heard someone laugh, she wondered if they were laughing at Rachel.

Or if they were laughing at nothing at all, because the world didn’t keep score the way Allison did.

The thought came late, when Allison finally had a moment alone.

It wasn’t sharp.

It wasn’t even fully formed.

It came from guilt and helplessness and the sick feeling of replaying the hallway moment over and over without being able to change it.

I wish people would just leave her alone.

The thought slipped through her mind like breath.

Allison felt it and immediately tried to take it back—not because it was cruel, but because it felt like reaching for something she didn’t deserve.

She hadn’t helped Rachel on the floor.

She didn’t get to want anything now.

She shoved the thought down and moved on with her day, pretending it hadn’t happened.

By the end of school, the energy in the hallways had shifted.

Not louder. Not quieter.

Just different.

Allison saw it in the way people talked with less excitement, in the way phones stayed down longer, in the way the story seemed to have lost its shine.

By the time she reached her locker, someone behind her said, “It got taken down.”

Another voice replied, “Probably reported.”

Someone else scoffed. “It wasn’t even that bad anyway.”

Allison’s hands went cold.

She turned her head slightly, pretending to look for a book, and listened without looking.

They were talking about Rachel.

About the video.

About the thing that had made her stomach twist all day.

It was gone.

Just gone.

The relief didn’t fade right away.

That terrified her.

She walked the length of the hallway carrying it like contraband, afraid someone might notice it on her face. Afraid she’d smile at the wrong moment. Afraid relief would turn into gratitude if she wasn’t careful.

She tried to make herself feel worse.

She replayed the fall again. Rachel’s hands. The laughter. The phones.

It didn’t undo the relief.

It just layered guilt on top of it.

Allison left school fast.

She didn’t wait for Kara. She didn’t linger near the front entrance. She didn’t stop at her locker twice like she sometimes did when she forgot something.

She just walked.

Her feet carried her toward home without thinking, until she realized she wasn’t on the main road.

She’d turned down the narrower path behind the houses without deciding to.

The creek waited at the edge of town like it always did, water moving steadily over stones, indifferent and constant.

Allison dropped her backpack and sat hard on the bank, hugging her knees.

She tried to breathe the way she usually did here.

In through her nose.
Out through her mouth.

She counted the seconds without meaning to.

The water didn’t help.

The rhythm didn’t sink in.

Her chest stayed tight, breath shallow and uneven.

She dug her fingers into the dirt beside her, grounding herself in something real. Cold soil packed under her nails.

It didn’t fix anything.

The creek had always been where things settled.

Today, nothing settled.

“I should’ve done something,” Allison said aloud, and the words sounded strange in the open air.

She stared at the current and tried to replay the moment again in a way that ended differently.

If she stepped forward.

If she picked up papers.

If she said, “Hey, it’s fine,” in a voice loud enough for people to hear.

If she glared at the kids with phones.

If she was brave.

If she wasn’t herself.

Allison exhaled slowly.

She picked up a stone and rolled it between her fingers, feeling the damp grit against her skin. Then she tossed it into the water. It disappeared with a small plunk, ripples spreading outward and then fading.

Nothing dramatic.

Just movement.

She wanted to tell herself that was what today had been.

A ripple. A moment. Something that would fade.

But the memory didn’t feel like it was fading. It felt like it was taking root.

What scared her wasn’t just that she hadn’t acted.

It was that she’d noticed herself not acting.

That she’d watched herself hesitate and recognized it as a choice.

That she’d known she was choosing safety over kindness and couldn’t stop herself.

And then later, when the video disappeared, she’d felt relief like she’d earned it.

Allison pressed her palms into her knees.

If my thoughts matter, she whispered, and stopped.

The words felt too big, too ridiculous, too close to something she wasn’t ready to say.

She tried again, quieter.

If my thoughts… do anything…

The air swallowed the sentence.

She stared at the water until her eyes hurt.

What if it was coincidence?

What if she was doing the thing she feared—turning ordinary patterns into stories because she wanted to believe she mattered?

But what if it wasn’t?

What if her silence mattered too?

The thought turned her stomach.

Because silence was her whole life.

Silence at breakfast while Emily talked.
Silence in class unless called on.
Silence in hallways while other people took up space.
Silence while Rachel’s papers scattered on the floor.

Silence was how she survived.

If silence was suddenly a choice, if it suddenly carried weight… then what did that make her?

She sat at the creek until the light shifted and the cold seeped through her jeans. The water kept moving, unconcerned. Leaves drifted past, caught briefly on stones and then released again.

Allison wished she could be like that.

Drift past.

Get released.

Not stick.

Eventually, she stood, shoulders stiff, and slung her backpack on. The walk home felt longer than it should have, as if the air itself had thickened.

At home, the house felt too normal.

Emily’s voice floated from somewhere. A door opened and closed. The smell of dinner started to gather in the kitchen.

Allison went straight to her room and shut the door.

She sat on her bed and stared at her phone for a long moment before unlocking it.

Then she opened Notes.

# Chapter 4

# Chapter 4 — Rules for the Day

Allison woke up already braced.

Not for anything specific. Just for the idea of the day itself—its length, its unpredictability, the way it would unfold whether she felt ready or not.

The house sounded the same as always. Emily’s door opened and closed. Someone turned on the shower. The kitchen light flicked on downstairs. Normal noises, in a normal order.

Allison lay still for a moment and tried to decide how to exist inside it.

She sat up slowly, careful not to think too hard while she did it. Her body felt stiff, like she’d slept wrong, but she knew it wasn’t that. It was the same tightness she’d been carrying since yesterday, lodged somewhere behind her ribs.

She reached for her phone and stopped herself.

Not the notes. Not yet.

Downstairs, breakfast unfolded without friction. Emily talked about something from social media—someone’s outfit, someone else’s comment thread. Their parents half-listened, responding when expected, moving through their morning like it was a script they’d rehearsed enough times to stop paying attention to.

Allison ate, nodded when necessary, answered questions with as few words as possible.

“You’re quiet again,” her mom said, glancing at her.

“I’m just tired,” Allison said.

It was becoming her default answer. It worked because it was unprovable.

Her mom studied her for a second longer than usual, then nodded. “Don’t forget your lunch.”

“I won’t.”

Allison rinsed her bowl, grabbed her bag, and left before the conversation could deepen into something she didn’t have the energy to navigate.

Outside, the air was cold and clear, sharp enough to cut through the fog in her head. The street was quiet, early enough that most people were still inside. Allison walked with her hands tucked into her sleeves, shoulders hunched slightly forward.

Halfway down the block, she started making rules.

She didn’t mean to. It just… happened.

Don’t wish for anything today.

That one came first, immediate and obvious.

Don’t think about wishing either, she added quickly, like she could outpace her own thoughts if she moved fast enough.

The sidewalk stretched out ahead of her, familiar cracks and uneven patches marking time. Allison stepped over a long split in the concrete without looking down.

Don’t want things, she told herself.

That one felt harder.

She tried again, softer. Just don’t want anything big.

The problem with rules was that they required clarity.

Allison didn’t have any.

She tried to simplify them as she walked, breaking them down into smaller, more manageable pieces.

Don’t want things was too vague. Wanting was constant, automatic. Wanting warmth when the wind cut through her sleeves. Wanting the walk to be shorter. Wanting the day to already be over.

She amended it.

Don’t want outcomes.

That felt smarter. More precise.

Except outcomes were everywhere too. Outcomes were bells ringing and teachers calling on students and assignments being due whether she felt ready or not.

Her steps slowed without her noticing.

A car passed too close to the curb and splashed water from a shallow puddle onto the sidewalk. Allison jumped, heart stuttering, then exhaled sharply at herself.

Get it together.

She hated that phrase. Hated how often she used it. Like her emotions were something scattered she was responsible for gathering back into place.

By the time she reached the edge of the school grounds, the rules felt less like guidelines and more like apologies she was rehearsing for a version of herself that didn’t exist yet.

By the time the school building came into view, Allison felt like she’d already failed at something she couldn’t quite name.

The hallway noise hit her all at once—lockers, voices, laughter bouncing off tile and metal. Allison moved through it automatically, her body remembering the routes her mind was busy monitoring.

Just get through the day, she told herself.

In first period, she slid into her seat and kept her eyes on her desk. The teacher droned on about attendance, announcements, things Allison barely registered.

Then the classroom door opened.

“Before we start,” the teacher said, glancing toward the doorway, “we have a new student joining us today.”

Allison looked up despite herself.

The boy standing at the front of the room didn’t look nervous. He didn’t look overly confident either. He just stood there, hands at his sides, posture relaxed but attentive, like he was waiting for something specific to happen.

“Everyone, this is Evan,” the teacher continued. “He’s just transferred in. Evan, you can take the empty seat near the back.”

Allison’s stomach tightened.

She didn’t tell it to do that.

Evan nodded, murmured something that might have been a thank you, and started down the aisle.

Allison looked away quickly, focusing on the edge of her notebook. She could feel him passing without seeing him, like a shift in air pressure. When he slid into the empty seat two rows over, she resisted the urge to glance back.

Rules, she reminded herself.

Her body didn’t care.

She told herself she was only aware of Evan because he was new.

Newness always stood out.

That explanation lasted exactly three seconds.

She felt him before she saw him again, a subtle shift in the room that made her spine straighten. She focused harder on the teacher’s voice, on the board, on the steady scratch of her pen.

Don’t notice.

Her body ignored her.

She could sense the direction of his attention without looking. It wasn’t heavy or invasive. It didn’t crawl.

It just… existed.

Like he was listening to something quiet.

Her jaw tightened. She forced herself to move, to flip a page, to act like nothing had changed.

Inside, everything felt slightly misaligned.

Between classes, they passed each other in the hallway.

It was nothing—just a moment where paths crossed, shoulders narrowly missing. But Evan’s eyes flicked to her face, sharp and curious, like he was noticing something specific.

Allison looked away first.

Her heart thudded too hard for no reason she could explain.

At lunch, Kara dropped into the seat across from her with a grin. “Okay, so. New guy.”

Allison stared at her food. “What about him?”

Kara laughed. “Don’t pretend you didn’t see him. Evan. He’s cute.”

Allison shrugged, deliberately casual. “I guess.”

Kara raised an eyebrow. “That’s it? ‘I guess’?”

“I didn’t really notice,” Allison lied.

Kara scoffed. “Sure you didn’t.”

Kara talked the way people always did when something new entered their orbit—easily, playfully, without weight. Allison watched her and felt like she was on the wrong side of a glass wall.

She wanted to ask Kara if she ever worried about being seen wrong.

If she ever felt like attention was something that stuck to you and didn’t let go.

She didn’t ask.

The questions felt dangerous. The kind that would make Kara look at her differently.

Allison smiled when Kara laughed. She nodded at the right moments. She played her part carefully, afraid that if she slipped even a little, Kara would notice the strain underneath.

She didn’t want to be intense.

She didn’t want to be dramatic.

She didn’t want to be the girl people lowered their voices around.

Across the cafeteria, Evan sat alone, eating quietly, eyes scanning the room in a way that didn’t feel bored.

It felt deliberate.

Allison forced herself to look away.

Don’t want anything, she reminded herself.

The thought that slipped through anyway was small and dangerous:

I wish he wouldn’t look at me like that.

She shut it down instantly, breath catching.

No. Not that.

The rest of the day passed with a strange tension threaded through it.

After school, Allison didn’t wait around.

She took the familiar path toward the creek, her steps quick, mind buzzing.

The creek was quiet when she arrived, sunlight filtering through bare branches, water moving steadily over stone.

Allison sat on the bank and tried to do everything the same way she always did.

She watched the same bend in the water. She counted her breaths.

It should have worked.

It didn’t.

The quiet felt louder instead of softer. The space felt different. Less private.

She glanced over her shoulder even though she knew she was alone.

For the first time, Allison wondered if the creek had ever actually calmed her—or if it had just given her permission to be alone with herself.

Eventually, she stood and headed home, unsettled.

In her room, Allison shut the door and sat on her bed, phone in hand. She hesitated only a second before opening Notes.

# Chapter 5

# Chapter 5 — Perimeter

Allison tried not to look for him.

She failed before she even got through the front doors.

It wasn’t deliberate—not in the way she would have accused herself of if she’d had the energy. It was the kind of noticing that happened before thought, like your eyes tracking movement without asking permission. Like your body deciding something mattered and refusing to explain why.

She walked the same path through the hallway she always did, slipping between clusters of people, keeping her face neutral. She kept her gaze mostly forward, occasionally dropping it to her locker combination as if numbers could anchor her.

Rules, she reminded herself.

She’d made them yesterday like they meant something.

Don’t wish.
Don’t want.
Don’t notice.

In the cold light of morning, they felt less like rules and more like a superstition.

Allison slowed as she approached her locker, aware of every inch of space around her in a way that felt almost predatory.

She could feel people passing behind her, close enough that the fabric of their sleeves brushed the air near her arms. Voices overlapped in bursts—inside jokes, complaints, half-finished sentences that didn’t need to include her.

She wondered if people ever noticed how much effort it took just to stand normally.

She rolled her shoulders back, then immediately worried she’d done it too deliberately. She shifted her weight, then worried that looked uncertain. There was no neutral way to exist once she started thinking about it.

Stop, she told herself. You’re fine.

She focused on the dial.

Thirty-six.
Twenty-one.
Back to nine.

The numbers steadied her hands, even as her chest stayed tight.

She told herself she was being ridiculous. That this was just a hallway. Just a morning. Just another day she’d walked this exact path a hundred times before.

The feeling didn’t go away.

She reached her locker and spun the dial with careful precision. The metal was cold under her fingertips. Her hands didn’t shake, which felt like a victory even though it shouldn’t have mattered.

Voices swelled around her. Laughter. The slam of locker doors. A teacher telling someone to take their hood down. Normal, normal, normal.

Allison’s brain tried to relax into it.

Then she felt it—the shift.

Not sound, not movement. Something subtler. Like a new weight in the air, like the space behind her had changed density.

She didn’t turn.

She kept her eyes on her books, sliding one out, swapping it for another, moving with exaggerated calm.

“Hey.”

The word landed close enough that she felt it in her ribs.

Allison’s fingers froze on the edge of her binder.

She knew that voice already. Calm. Measured. Not loud enough to draw attention, but placed like it was meant to be heard.

She forced herself to breathe in and out before she turned.

Evan stood a few feet away, not crowding her space, not leaning. Just… present. His backpack was slung over one shoulder. His expression was neutral in a way that didn’t feel blank. It felt attentive.

Allison’s pulse ticked up.

“Hi,” she managed.

Evan glanced at her locker, then back to her face. “Is it always this loud in here?”

Allison blinked. “It’s… school.”

A flicker of something crossed his expression. Almost a smile. Not quite.

“Right,” he said. “I’m still adjusting.”

Allison nodded like that was a normal thing to say. Like this was a normal conversation she was built for.

She wasn’t.

“Are you—” Evan paused, eyes shifting briefly past her shoulder as a group of girls walked by, laughing. When he looked back, his gaze landed on her with the same quiet precision as before. “Are you okay?”

For half a second, Allison almost told the truth.

Not the whole truth. Just something small and manageable. Something like I’m tired or I didn’t sleep well or school’s just been weird lately.

The words stacked up behind her teeth, pressing forward.

Then she imagined Evan responding.

Imagined concern, or worse—understanding.

Her chest tightened sharply.

“I’m fine,” she said instead.

The relief she felt was immediate and sour.

Evan didn’t look disappointed.

That somehow made her chest ache.

She watched his face for signs—confusion, judgment, boredom—and found none of them. Just attention, steady and unhurried.

Like he wasn’t waiting for her to be different.

Like he was waiting for her to be honest.

The thought scared her enough that she forced her gaze down, pretending to check the books in her bag.

Evan didn’t push. He didn’t fill the silence.

He nodded once, like he was filing her answer away somewhere.

Allison’s hands tightened around her binder. She forced her grip to loosen.

“Sorry,” she added. “I’m just… tired.”

Evan’s gaze dropped to her hands for half a second.

Then he looked up again, and his voice stayed calm. “Yeah. You seem tired.”

Allison felt heat rise in her face.

Seen.

Her mind started throwing rules at her faster than she could catch them.

Don’t react.
Don’t say something weird.
Don’t think about how he said that.
Don’t wish he’d stop looking at you.

The last one slipped through like a splinter.

Allison swallowed hard.

Evan shifted his weight slightly, glancing down the hallway as if checking time. “Anyway,” he said, like he was giving her an exit. “I just… wanted to say hi. I don’t know anyone yet.”

Allison’s chest tightened.

She knew what it felt like not to know anyone. To exist on the edges of rooms, pretending you weren’t alone.

But Evan didn’t feel like the kind of person who got stuck on the edges.

That thought made her suspicious. Of him. Of herself.

“Yeah,” she said, then realized she hadn’t actually answered him. “Hi.”

Evan’s mouth twitched. “Okay.”

Then, before she could decide whether to speak again, he nodded once and moved on, slipping into the flow of students like he belonged there.

Allison watched him go.

She hated herself for watching.

She hated that part of her was already replaying it.

When she finally looked away, Kara was standing a few lockers down with her eyebrows raised.

“Oh my god,” Kara said, grinning. “He talked to you.”

Allison’s stomach dropped. “No.”

Kara laughed. “Yes. I literally watched it happen.”

Allison turned back to her locker, pretending to be busy. “He just said hi.”

Kara leaned closer. “He said hi to you.”

“I wanted to run away,” Allison muttered.

Kara snorted. “Sure you did. You’re blushing.”

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

Allison shut her locker harder than she meant to. The sound echoed. She flinched.

Kara’s grin softened. “Okay, okay. I’m teasing. But seriously—what was that about?”

Allison forced a shrug. “Nothing.”

Kara studied her for a beat. “You’ve been weird today.”

Allison’s stomach tightened.

“I’m just tired,” she said.

Kara chewed on her straw. “You keep saying that.”

Allison smiled. “It’s been a week.”

Kara accepted the answer, but something about her expression shifted—not suspicion. Distance.

The guilt hit harder than Allison expected.

She wasn’t lying.

She just wasn’t telling the truth either.

Allison spent the rest of the morning struggling to focus.

In English, she stared at the same paragraph without absorbing a single word. Her foot bounced under the desk until she forced it still.

She kept expecting something to happen.

That was the worst part—the waiting.

When the teacher began calling on students to read aloud, dread rose automatically.

She clamped down on the familiar wish.

No wishing.

The names moved closer.

Then skipped her.

Just like that.

Relief hit first.

Then fear.

She was sure she hadn’t wished.

By lunch, Allison felt hollowed out by vigilance.

Kara talked like nothing had changed—teachers, quizzes, someone’s boyfriend drama. Allison smiled when expected, laughed once when appropriate.

Across the cafeteria, Evan sat alone again.

Not lonely.

Unclaimed.

It didn’t make her relax.

After school, Allison didn’t wait around.

She left with her head down and her pace just fast enough that no one could accuse her of lingering.

She crossed the street and turned onto the narrower side road that led away from the school.

She didn’t look back.

She knew better than to look back.

The late afternoon light flattened everything into muted blues and grays. Houses blurred past. Her feet fell into the rhythm she usually relied on.

It didn’t work today.

She turned down the side street that led toward the creek path, pavement cracking into dirt, weeds pressing in.

The sound of traffic faded.

She felt it again—that subtle shift.

“Allison?”

Her name stopped her cold.

She turned slowly, heart slamming.

Evan stood several yards back, hands lifted slightly. “Sorry,” he said quickly. “I didn’t mean to scare you.”

“It’s fine,” she said automatically.

“You go this way a lot?” he asked.

“Sometimes.”

“I’m still figuring out how to get home,” he said. “This town’s smaller than I expected.”

“Yeah,” she said. “That happens.”

“You’re from here, right?”

“Yeah. Born somewhere else. I don’t remember it.”

“Do you like it?”

She hesitated. “It’s just… the kind of place where people already think they know you.”

Evan nodded. “Even if they don’t?”

She looked at him, surprised.

“Where’d you move from?” she asked.

“Michigan,” he said. “My dad got transferred. We’ve moved before, but this one feels different.”

“How?”

“Quieter,” he said. “Like things are happening under the surface and no one talks about them.”

Her fingers curled into her sleeves.

They walked together as the path narrowed.

“You seem like you notice stuff,” Evan said.

“I don’t,” she said too quickly.

He smiled faintly. “You do. You just don’t draw attention to it.”

She stared at the ground. “I try not to.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know,” she said. “It’s easier.”

“I guess I notice people,” Evan said after a moment. “Maybe more than I should.”

She swallowed.

They reached the clearing.

“This is where I usually sit,” she said.

“I get why,” Evan said quietly.

“It’s just a place.”

“Yeah,” he said. “But some places are different.”

She hugged her arms around herself. “I should head home.”

“Yeah. Of course.”

They walked back together.

“Thanks,” Evan said. “For letting me walk with you.”

She nodded. “Yeah.”

“I’m glad I ran into you,” he added.

Her stomach flipped.

“Me too,” she said.

He smiled once. “See you tomorrow?”

“Yeah.”

He turned away.

Allison stayed until he disappeared, then sat down hard at the edge of the path, the creek suddenly too close.

Not hers anymore.
