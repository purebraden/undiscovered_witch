# CHAPTER 1 — The Girl Who Fits Enough

The first thing Allison did every morning was check her phone.

Not because she was popular. Not because she expected a flood of messages. It was more like… habit. A reflex. The way you touch a bruise to see if it still hurts.

Notifications: three.

A weather alert. A reminder from the school app about attendance. And one from her Notes app, flagged because she’d starred it last night.

Don’t forget: you’re allowed to want things.

Allison stared at the line for a second, then locked her phone and rolled onto her back.

The ceiling above her was smooth except for one small crack near the corner—the kind that looked like a tiny lightning bolt. She’d stared at it so much she’d started to think she could map her moods by it. Some mornings it felt like a threat. Some mornings it was just… there.

Today it was just there.

She swung her legs over the side of the bed and listened.

The house was awake.

Not loudly—just enough. The sound of the coffee maker clicking. A cabinet door closing. The faint hum of the dryer in the laundry room downstairs. And, from the hallway, Emily’s music—thin through her closed door, as if she didn’t want anyone to hear and also wanted everyone to hear.

Allison pulled on her favorite sweatshirt, the one that was soft in the sleeves from being washed too many times. It wasn’t stylish. It wasn’t ugly. It was safe.

Safe was most of her wardrobe.

She padded into the bathroom and looked at herself in the mirror.

Her hair was blonde and long, usually too long, the kind that slipped out of ponytails and tangled if you looked at it wrong. She’d never dyed it or cut it dramatically or done any of the things girls in movies did when they wanted to become a new version of themselves. Her eyes were green, a little too bright when the light hit them, and for a second—just a second—she caught herself looking like someone else.

Like a girl in a book jacket.

Then the thought slipped away and she was just Allison again.

Allison Crites.

The name looked normal on forms. The name sounded normal when teachers called roll. It didn’t carry a story.

She brushed her teeth and watched herself do it, the way her mind sometimes drifted out of her body and hovered overhead like a drone. She could see her own shoulders slumped, her own eyes heavy, her own movements automatic.

Survive. Blend. Don’t make a scene.

It wasn’t that she wanted to be invisible.

It was that she didn’t want to be wrong.

Downstairs, her mom was at the counter with her back turned, hair clipped up, wearing the cardigan she wore when she had early meetings.

“Morning,” Allison said.

Her mom turned. “Hey, honey.”

Her smile landed soft and practiced, like she was laying something carefully on a shelf.

Allison opened the fridge. Milk. Leftovers. A box of strawberries that looked like they were on their last day.

“Did you sleep?” her mom asked.

“Yeah.”

“Good.”

Allison grabbed a yogurt and a spoon and sat at the table.

Her dad came in a minute later, already in his work jacket, keys in hand, eyes flicking to the clock.

“Morning, kiddo,” he said, kissing the top of her head as he passed.

Kiddo.

Not Allison. Not honey. Not sweetheart. Kiddo was what you called someone when you didn’t want to get too specific.

Emily came down a few minutes later like a firework.

She didn’t walk so much as appear—hair messy, pajama pants loud, phone in hand, already talking as if she’d been mid-conversation since midnight.

“Mom, I need the purple hoodie. The one that makes my eyes look good. The gray one makes me look like—like a sad Victorian child.”

Her mom laughed. Real laughter, the kind that showed teeth. “It’s in the dryer. If you’d stop leaving clothes on the floor, you’d know where things were.”

Emily spun dramatically toward Allison. “Did you hear that? She’s attacking me. In the morning.”

Allison lifted her spoon. “I’m witnessing.”

Emily grinned, slid into the chair next to her, and stole one of Allison’s strawberries without asking. Allison didn’t stop her.

Emily was fourteen and somehow made every room feel like it was built for her. She had dark hair that fell in perfect waves like it had a personal assistant. She had her mother’s confidence and her father’s ability to move like the world expected her. She was loud in a way that read as charming instead of annoying.

If Allison had been a different kind of person, she would’ve hated her.

Sometimes she tried to.

But mostly she just watched her like someone watching a show they weren’t allowed to be in.

“Are you coming to my game Friday?” Emily asked, already chewing.

“Allison has work,” her mom said immediately.

Allison paused.

“I—” she started, but her mom kept going.

“The school store needs her. She’s responsible.”

Emily rolled her eyes. “Of course. Allison is responsible. Allison is always responsible. Allison should get a trophy for being so responsible.”

The words weren’t mean, exactly.

But they landed like a label.

Responsible: the version of Allison people could explain.

Allison swallowed. “I can probably come after.”

Emily brightened. “Really?”

Allison’s dad glanced over. “If you’re not too tired.”

Allison nodded, even though she hadn’t been asked a question.

She looked down at her yogurt, suddenly not hungry.

Something about mornings always made her feel like she was watching other people’s lives happen around her. Like everyone else had a script and she was improvising, hoping no one noticed the hesitation.

Her phone buzzed.

A message from Kara:

u alive?

Allison’s shoulders loosened.

She typed back:

barely. u?

Kara responded immediately:

same. meet at lockers? i have gossip

Allison’s lips twitched into a smile.

Kara was the kind of friend you didn’t find in movies.

She wasn’t the glamorous best friend who made you popular. She wasn’t the quirky best friend who made you fun. She was… real. She was steady. She was blunt and weird and loyal in a way that made you feel like you could exhale.

Allison tucked her phone into her pocket like it was something precious.

The bus was cold.

Someone had cracked a window and the October air came in sharp, smelling like wet leaves and metal. Allison slid into a seat halfway back, the one she always took, where she could see the front and also pretend she wasn’t watching anything.

Emily sat near the back with her friends, laughing too loudly.

Allison didn’t join them.

Not because Emily didn’t want her there.

Because it would’ve felt like trespassing.

Allison watched houses pass by—white siding, porches, yards with Halloween decorations starting to appear. Alder Creek was the kind of town where people liked things to look nice. The lawns were trimmed. The porch lights worked. The signs in the front yards matched the seasons.

It looked like a town where nothing bad happened.

Allison knew better.

Not because anything dramatic had happened to her in Alder Creek.

Because bad things didn’t have to be dramatic to leave marks.

The school came into view like a brick statement: big enough to matter, old enough to feel permanent.

Alder Creek High.

The bus hissed to a stop. Students poured out like water released.

Allison moved with them, flowing through the doors.

Inside, the hallway smelled like floor cleaner and cheap perfume.

Her locker was near the center, between a girl who always wore too much lip gloss and a boy who seemed to exist purely to slam his locker door as loudly as possible.

Kara was already there, leaning against the lockers like she belonged to them.

“Allison,” Kara said, dragging out the syllables like a greeting and an accusation at the same time. “You look like you’re haunting your own body.”

Allison twisted the combination. “That’s my brand.”

Kara grinned. Her hair was in a messy bun, her eyeliner slightly uneven, her hoodie oversized in a way that looked intentional even if it wasn’t.

“I have news,” Kara said.

Allison opened her locker. “Hit me.”

Kara leaned closer. “New student.”

Allison blinked. “Okay?”

Kara widened her eyes. “Not just any new student. A cute new student.”

Allison felt her face pull into an expression that was probably supposed to be interested. “How do you know he’s cute?”

Kara shrugged. “I have eyes.”

Allison closed her locker and turned. “Where did you see him?”

“Front office,” Kara said. “He was with the counselor. Tall. Dark hair. Like… annoying handsome.”

Allison snorted. “Annoying handsome isn’t a type.”

“It is if you’ve ever watched a show,” Kara said. “Anyway, he looked like he knew where he was even though he didn’t. Like he walked in and the building introduced itself.”

Allison paused.

Something about that phrasing scratched at her brain.

“Does he have a name?” Allison asked.

Kara nodded dramatically. “Evan.”

Allison’s stomach did a small, strange flip—uninvited, unnecessary, embarrassing.

“Evan what?” she asked quickly, as if she didn’t care.

Kara shrugged. “No idea. But he’s in our grade.”

“All right,” Allison said, aiming for casual. “Good for the school.”

Kara squinted at her. “You are not immune to cute boys, Allison Crites.”

“I am extremely immune,” Allison said.

Kara snorted. “Liar.”

They started walking to first period.

The hallway was busy, students weaving around each other with practiced avoidance. Allison spotted Jess near the water fountain, waving half-heartedly. Allison waved back.

Then she saw him.

Not because he was loud.

Because he was still.

A boy stood near the office doors, looking at a map on the wall like it had personally offended him. Dark hair, yes. Tall, yes. Not model-handsome, not unreal—just… the kind of handsome that made you look twice and then immediately look away because it felt rude.

He lifted his gaze, and for a second, his eyes met Allison’s.

It wasn’t flirty.

It wasn’t even curious.

It was like he was recognizing something.

Allison’s heart jumped.

Then he looked away.

Allison forced herself to keep walking.

Kara hadn’t noticed.

Or maybe she pretended she hadn’t.

“See?” Kara said smugly. “Annoying handsome.”

Allison didn’t answer.

Something cold settled at the base of her throat.

In English class, Ms. Whitaker handed out essays with a look of exhaustion and quiet judgment.

“Allison,” she said, pausing by her desk. “Good work.”

The compliment was soft, but it landed like a small warm stone in Allison’s chest.

“Allison always does good work,” the girl behind her whispered to her friend.

Allison heard it.

Not as praise.

As expectation.

She stared at her essay, the red marks neat and controlled. A perfect B+.

Good enough. Never outstanding. Never messy.

The girl who fits enough.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. Allison didn’t check it. She didn’t need to. She could almost feel the Notes app pulsing like a second heartbeat, waiting for her to feed it.

She made it through two classes before she finally cracked and pulled her phone out in the bathroom stall like a criminal.

She opened Notes.

There was a new blank note, the cursor blinking patiently.

Allison typed:

Day 1 of trying to not be weird

The universe has not agreed to this plan.

She stared at the line.

Then added:

I don’t want to be special.
I want to be safe.

Those are not the same thing.

She saved it quickly, like someone might catch her writing her own thoughts.

Lunch was a battlefield disguised as a cafeteria.

Allison and Kara sat at their usual table near the windows. It wasn’t a cool table or a loser table. It was just… theirs.

Jess joined them halfway through, dropping her tray down with a sigh.

“I hate Wednesdays,” Jess declared.

“It’s Monday,” Kara said.

Jess blinked. “I hate Mondays.”

Allison smiled faintly.

Across the cafeteria, Emily sat with the freshman girls, laughing too loudly, hair already perfect again.

Allison watched her for a moment, feeling a familiar ache. Not jealousy exactly. Something closer to grief.

Kara nudged her. “Don’t.”

Allison looked at her. “Don’t what?”

“Don’t do that thing where you stare at her like she’s a different species,” Kara said. “She’s just a kid.”

“She’s a kid who knows where she belongs,” Allison said quietly.

Kara’s expression softened. “You belong too.”

Allison laughed once, sharp and small. “Where?”

Kara opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

That was the truth.

Allison belonged in pieces. In moments. In other people’s spare space.

Not in a place that was hers.

The cafeteria noise swelled—laughter, yelling, chairs scraping.

Allison looked down at her tray and thought, without meaning to:

I wish people would just stop looking at me like I’m a problem.

The thought was small.

Private.

A whisper inside her skull.

She blinked.

Across the cafeteria, a girl who had been glaring at Allison—one of Emily’s friends, maybe—suddenly turned and started laughing at something else. The expression melted off her face like it had never been there.

Allison’s stomach dropped.

She looked around, heart hammering.

No one noticed.

No one reacted.

It was subtle enough to be coincidence.

But Allison felt it in her bones the way you feel the air change before a storm.

Kara was talking about a teacher, unaware.

Jess was texting, unaware.

Allison sat very still.

The thought returned, quieter now, more afraid:

Did I do that?

She didn’t move until the bell rang.

After school, she walked home instead of taking the bus.

She told herself it was because she wanted the fresh air.

Really, she wanted distance.

The town was damp from earlier rain. Leaves clung to sidewalks. The creek ran under the bridge, dark and steady.

Allison paused there, leaning on the railing.

The water moved the way it always did, not caring about her.

For a moment, that was comforting.

Then she heard footsteps behind her.

She didn’t turn.

“You’re Allison Crites,” a voice said.

It wasn’t a question.

Allison’s spine stiffened.

She turned slowly.

Evan stood a few feet away, hands in his pockets, expression neutral.

Not smiling.

Not threatening.

Just… present.

“How do you know my name?” Allison asked.

Evan blinked, like he hadn’t expected resistance. “I heard someone say it.”

“Who?”

He hesitated. “Kara.”

Allison swallowed.

“Right,” she said, because what else could she say?

Evan glanced toward the creek, then back at her. His gaze was steady, like he was trying not to look too hard.

“I’m Evan,” he said.

“I know,” Allison replied before she could stop herself.

He didn’t smile at that. He just nodded, like it confirmed something.

For a second, neither of them spoke.

Then Evan said, softly, “Do you ever feel like… things get quieter around you?”

Allison’s throat went dry.

“What?” she asked.

Evan shrugged, like it was nothing. “Like arguments stop before they happen. Like people change their minds. Like the air… settles.”

Allison forced a laugh that sounded wrong even to her. “No.”

Evan studied her for a moment.

Then he nodded once, as if he’d expected that answer too.

“Okay,” he said. “Just wondering.”

He turned and walked away.

Allison stood frozen on the bridge, hands gripping the railing hard enough to hurt.

The creek moved beneath her, steady and indifferent.

She watched Evan disappear down the sidewalk until he was just another person in Alder Creek.

Then she pulled out her phone with shaking fingers and opened Notes.

A new entry.

She typed:

Something is wrong

I thought I wanted to belong.

But what if belonging is just another word for being claimed?

She stared at the last line.

Her heartbeat slowed.

Not because she felt better.

Because the thought felt true.

Allison saved the note.

And walked home like nothing had happened.
