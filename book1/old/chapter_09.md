# CHAPTER 9 — Ripples

By Friday, Allison had become helpful again.

Not officially. No one announced it. There was no moment where someone pointed at her and said, *You handle this now.* It happened the way things always happened in Alder Creek—quietly, cooperatively, without anyone admitting responsibility.

It started small.

In English, a classmate Allison barely spoke to leaned across the aisle and whispered, “Can you tell him it’s fine if we switch presentation days?” When Allison nodded and relayed the message, the tension dissolved instantly. The teacher smiled. The student mouthed *thank you* like a secret.

In chemistry, two lab partners argued softly over measurements. Allison stood nearby, not involved, not invited. She felt the familiar pull anyway. When she cleared her throat—barely a sound—the argument slowed, then stopped. One of them shrugged and said, “Whatever. It’s fine.”

The teacher glanced over, relief evident. “Good teamwork,” she said.

Allison stared down at her notes, heart thudding.

By lunch, the pattern was unmistakable.

---

Kara wasn’t sitting alone anymore.

Jess had taken Allison’s place without asking. Not maliciously. Just naturally, like water filling a space that had been left open.

Allison slid onto the bench beside Kara anyway, careful not to crowd her. Kara smiled, but it was the polite smile she used with people she wasn’t entirely sure about anymore.

“Hey,” Kara said.

“Hey.”

Jess launched into a story about a teacher losing his temper during third period. Kara laughed in the right places, nodding, adding small comments. She didn’t look at Allison once.

Allison listened, hands folded in her lap.

Across the cafeteria, Evan watched.

Not intently. Not obviously. But he saw what Allison saw—the way people leaned toward her table when voices rose, the way glances flicked to Allison when conversations edged toward discomfort.

He did not move.

---

Halfway through lunch, a girl from Allison’s history class approached the table.

“Hey,” she said, eyes flicking between Kara and Jess before landing on Allison. “Sorry to interrupt, but could you help me with something?”

Allison hesitated. “What kind of something?”

The girl flushed. “My friend’s upset and I don’t know what to say. You’re… good at that.”

The words settled heavily in Allison’s chest.

“I’m in the middle of lunch,” she said carefully.

The girl nodded quickly. “Right. Sorry. Later, maybe?”

Allison glanced at Kara.

Kara was staring down at her tray.

“Maybe,” Allison said.

The girl smiled in relief and left.

Jess raised an eyebrow. “You’re kind of famous lately.”

Allison laughed weakly. “I don’t think that’s the word.”

“It kind of is,” Jess said. “Everyone says you’re really calming.”

Kara’s fork paused halfway to her mouth.

Allison’s stomach tightened.

“I don’t do anything special,” Allison said.

Jess shrugged. “Whatever it is, it works.”

Kara didn’t say anything.

---

By the end of the day, Allison was exhausted in a way she couldn’t explain.

Not physically. Emotionally. Like she’d been bracing herself all day against something invisible.

She walked home slowly, backpack heavy on her shoulders. The sky was overcast again, the air thick with the promise of rain that never quite arrived.

At the creek, the water moved steadily, neither rushing nor still. Allison leaned against the railing and closed her eyes.

She felt it then—the pull, stronger than it had been since the cafeteria.

It wasn’t attached to a specific problem. It wasn’t tied to a person or a moment.

It was ambient.

Like the town itself had learned where to press.

Her phone buzzed.

A text from an unknown number.

“Hey, this is Mark from chem. Sorry to bother you but could you talk to Lisa? She’s freaking out about the test and you’re really good at helping people calm down.”

Allison stared at the message.

She didn’t respond.

Another buzz.

“Kara gave me your number.”

That stung more than she expected.

---

At home, Emily was at the kitchen table, sprawled over her homework with dramatic flair.

“This is stupid,” Emily announced as Allison walked in. “Why do I need to know the quadratic formula?”

Allison dropped her bag. “Because someday it will save your life.”

Emily snorted. “Lies.”

Allison smiled faintly and poured herself a glass of water.

Emily watched her for a moment, then said, “You’re quieter.”

“I’m always quiet,” Allison said automatically.

“No,” Emily said. “You’re… tired-quiet. Not hiding-quiet.”

Allison leaned against the counter. “People keep asking me to help.”

Emily frowned. “Help how?”

“Talk to people. Fix things. Make stuff less weird.”

Emily grimaced. “That sounds exhausting.”

“It is.”

Emily chewed on the end of her pencil. “Why do they think you can do that?”

Allison opened her mouth.

Closed it.

“I don’t know,” she said.

Emily studied her. “You know you don’t have to, right?”

“I do,” Allison said.

“But do they?”

---

That night, Allison’s phone buzzed again.

This time, it was Kara.

“Hey. Can I ask you something?”

Allison’s heart jumped.

“Of course.”

There was a pause.

“Do you ever feel like people expect you to fix things?”

Allison closed her eyes.

“Yes. All the time.”

Another pause.

“I think that’s why people like you so much.”

Allison’s fingers trembled.

“Is that a good thing?”

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

“I don’t know. I just know it makes it hard to breathe sometimes.”

Allison stared at the screen.

She didn’t know how to respond without doing the thing Kara was afraid of.

So she didn’t.

“Thank you for telling me.”

Kara reacted with a heart.

Nothing else followed.

---

Monday morning brought rain at last.

Not a storm. Just a steady, soaking drizzle that blurred the edges of everything. Umbrellas bumped into each other at the school entrance. Shoes squeaked on the tile floors.

Allison shook water from her jacket and headed to her locker.

It jammed.

She froze.

That hadn’t happened in days.

She tugged harder. The metal resisted stubbornly.

“Need help?” a voice asked.

Allison turned.

Evan stood a few feet away, hands in his pockets, watching her carefully.

She laughed, a little hysterically. “It’s stuck.”

“That happens,” he said mildly.

She shot him a look.

He smiled faintly and reached past her, giving the locker door a sharp tug. It popped open.

Allison exhaled shakily.

“You okay?” he asked.

“I think I’m becoming… useful again,” she said.

Evan nodded. “I noticed.”

“That’s bad,” she said.

“Not inherently,” he replied. “But it’s dangerous.”

“Because I’ll do it again,” Allison said.

“Yes.”

She leaned against the locker, the cold metal grounding her. “I don’t want to hurt people.”

“I know.”

“But I don’t know how to stop them from asking.”

Evan considered that. “You can start by not answering every time.”

“I tried,” she said. “They just ask again.”

“That’s how systems work,” Evan said quietly. “They adapt.”

The word sent a chill through her.

“Do you ever feel like the town is… watching?” she asked.

Evan hesitated.

“Sometimes,” he said carefully.

She studied his face. “You’re holding something back.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because if I tell you now,” he said, “you’ll feel responsible for it too.”

Allison swallowed. “I already do.”

Evan met her gaze. “That’s exactly the problem.”

---

That night, Allison opened her Notes app.

Her hands moved slowly, deliberately.

> **Pattern**  
>  
> People ask.  
> Things end faster.  
>  
> I feel worse.

She stared at the screen.

Then she added:

> This isn’t accidental anymore.

She didn’t delete it.

She didn’t add an explanation.

She set the phone down and lay back, listening to the rain hit the window.

It didn’t settle.
