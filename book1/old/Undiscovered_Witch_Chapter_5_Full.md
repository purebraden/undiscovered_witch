# CHAPTER 5 — What Doesn’t Settle

The first thing Allison noticed was that the house didn’t quiet.

She came home late on Monday—later than usual, anyway. Evan had walked with her as far as Main Street before peeling off with an easy wave and a promise of “tomorrow,” and she’d taken the long way after that, letting the cool air and the fading light stretch the day out.

Normally, by the time she stepped through the front door, the house had already adjusted. The television volume would be low. Her parents’ voices would be muted, contained behind careful tones. Emily’s presence would be loud or absent, but never volatile.

Tonight, the tension was waiting.

It clung to the air like humidity.

Allison paused just inside the doorway, her backpack still on one shoulder, keys cold in her hand. From the kitchen came the sharp clink of a pan being set down too hard. Her mother’s voice followed—tight, clipped.

“I’m not saying you’re wrong. I’m saying we need to talk about it.”

Her father replied, quieter but edged. “We’ve talked about it. We keep talking about it.”

Allison closed the door softly behind her.

Down the hall, Emily’s door was open. Music blared from her room, loud enough to be defiant rather than careless. Allison could hear movement—drawers opening, footsteps pacing.

The house felt… crowded. Not with people, but with unprocessed feeling.

Allison set her bag down slowly.

This is fine, she told herself.  
It’s just one of those nights.

She moved toward the kitchen.

Her parents both looked up when she entered, like they’d been caught mid-sentence. Her mother’s mouth tightened into a smile that didn’t reach her eyes.

“Hey, honey,” she said. “You’re home late.”

“Sorry,” Allison said automatically. “I lost track of time.”

Her father nodded. “Everything okay?”

“Yes,” Allison said.

It wasn’t a lie. It just wasn’t the point.

Her mother turned back to the sink, scrubbing a dish that was already clean. The sound grated.

Allison hovered, waiting for the familiar pull—the moment when she’d say something neutral, calming, and the tension would slide away.

It didn’t come.

Her chest tightened.

“Do you need help with dinner?” she asked.

Her mother shook her head. “No. It’s almost done.”

Another clink. Louder this time.

Allison nodded and retreated.

---

Emily was sitting on her bed, knees pulled to her chest, phone abandoned beside her. She looked up when Allison appeared in the doorway, eyes sharp.

“What?” Emily said.

“Nothing,” Allison said quickly. “I just—are you okay?”

Emily scoffed. “Why does everyone keep asking me that?”

Allison stepped inside, careful. “Because you seem upset.”

“I am upset,” Emily snapped. “And before you say it—no, it’s not ‘nothing,’ and no, it’s not going to be fine.”

Allison froze.

Emily stood abruptly, pacing the room. “I’m sick of everyone acting like I’m a problem that needs to be managed.”

“That’s not—” Allison began.

“Don’t,” Emily said, holding up a hand. “Don’t do the thing.”

Allison swallowed. “What thing?”

“The calm voice,” Emily said. “The ‘it’s okay’ voice. The one where everything just… stops.”

Allison felt the words land like a slap.

“I’m just trying to help,” she said quietly.

“I know,” Emily said. “That’s what makes it worse.”

Silence stretched between them, brittle and uncomfortable.

Allison waited for the house to shift. For the pressure to ease.

It didn’t.

Emily stared at her, breathing hard. “I don’t want it to stop,” she said. “I want to be mad.”

Allison’s hands curled into fists at her sides.

“That’s not good for you,” she said, and immediately wished she could take it back.

Emily laughed, sharp and humorless. “See? That. That’s exactly what I mean.”

She grabbed her hoodie and shoved her arms into it. “I’m going out.”

“It’s dark,” Allison said. “You can’t just—”

“I can,” Emily said. “Watch me.”

She brushed past Allison, shoulder bumping hers hard enough to hurt.

Allison staggered back a step.

The house did not correct itself.

---

Emily stormed out the front door.

The sound echoed through the house, too loud, too final.

Allison stood in the hallway, heart pounding. Normally, this was where she’d act—say something sharp enough to stop, soft enough to soothe. Normally, the air would thicken, then smooth.

Nothing happened.

Her mother appeared at the end of the hall, alarm flashing across her face. “What just happened?”

Allison opened her mouth.

No words came.

Her father followed, jaw tight. “Where is she going?”

“I don’t know,” Allison said, and the admission felt dangerous.

Her mother swore under her breath and grabbed her coat. “I’ll get her.”

“I’ll come,” Allison said quickly.

“No,” her mother said, too fast. She hesitated, then softened. “Just—stay here. We’ll handle it.”

The words stung more than Allison expected.

She nodded anyway.

The front door closed behind them, leaving Allison alone in the house.

It was loud without them. The refrigerator hummed. The clock ticked too loudly. The television murmured from the living room, forgotten.

Allison sank onto the bottom step of the stairs.

Her chest felt tight, like something was pressing outward from inside.

She waited.

The house stayed unsettled.

---

Her phone buzzed.

A text from Evan.

*You okay?*

Allison stared at the screen, pulse racing.

*Yeah,* she typed. *Just stuff.*

She watched the three dots appear, then disappear.

*Want company?* Evan wrote.

Her first instinct was to say no. To smooth things herself. To wait until it passed.

It wasn’t passing.

*Maybe,* she typed. *Can you meet me at the creek?*

*On my way.*

---

The night air was colder than Allison expected. She pulled her hoodie tighter as she walked, breath puffing faintly in front of her.

The creek sounded louder than usual, swollen from the recent rain. The water moved fast, restless.

Evan was already there, leaning against the railing, hands tucked into his sleeves. He looked up when he saw her and straightened.

“Hey,” he said gently.

Allison nodded. “Hey.”

She moved to stand beside him, close enough to feel his warmth but not touching. They stood in silence for a moment, the sound of water filling the space between them.

“You don’t have to talk,” Evan said. “I just—figured you shouldn’t be alone.”

Allison swallowed. “I’m not very good at being… not useful.”

He glanced at her. “What do you mean?”

She stared down at the water. “When things get like this. I usually know what to do.”

“And tonight?”

“I don’t,” she said. The words came out thin, almost frightened.

Evan nodded slowly, like he’d expected that answer. “What happens if you don’t do anything?”

Allison laughed weakly. “That’s what I’m afraid to find out.”

They stood there, watching the creek churn.

After a moment, Evan spoke again. “Can I tell you something?”

She hesitated, then nodded. “Okay.”

“My family moves a lot,” he said. “Not in the normal way. There’s always a reason, but it’s never the reason.”

Allison frowned. “What does that mean?”

“It means we leave places right before things… settle too much,” he said. “Or right after.”

She looked at him sharply. “Settle how?”

He met her gaze, steady. “Like someone pressed down too hard.”

Allison’s breath caught.

“You ever notice,” Evan continued, “how people here don’t stay angry for long?”

“Yes,” Allison said automatically. Then she stopped.

Evan didn’t smile. “That’s not a compliment.”

The words hung between them, heavy and undeniable.

Allison’s vision blurred suddenly, tears pricking her eyes. She blinked them back, embarrassed.

“I didn’t mean to—” she began.

“I know,” Evan said quickly. “I’m not blaming you. I just… I think you’re carrying something that isn’t yours.”

She shook her head. “I don’t know how not to.”

He considered that. “Maybe you’re not supposed to.”

They stood there until Allison’s breathing evened out.

Eventually, she said, “My sister hates it when I make things stop.”

Evan nodded. “That tracks.”

Allison huffed a laugh despite herself. “She said she misses being mad.”

“That also tracks.”

They shared a quiet smile.

---

When Allison got home, her parents were back.

Emily was not.

Her mother sat at the kitchen table, hands wrapped tightly around a mug that had long since gone cold. Her father stood by the counter, staring at nothing.

“We can’t find her,” her mother said immediately. “She’s not answering her phone.”

Allison’s heart lurched. “Where did you look?”

“Everywhere she usually goes,” her father said. “Friends’ houses. The park.”

Allison felt the familiar surge of panic—and with it, the urge. The pull.

Make it stop.

She closed her eyes.

For a moment, the world seemed to lean toward her, like it always did.

She thought of Emily’s face, flushed with anger. Of her words.

I want to be mad.

Allison forced herself to breathe.

“I’ll help look,” she said instead.

Her parents nodded, relieved, already moving.

The house remained tense behind them.

---

They found Emily an hour later, sitting on the swings at the old playground near the woods. Her legs dragged through the dirt as she rocked back and forth slowly, eyes red.

Allison approached carefully.

Emily looked up and saw her—and didn’t calm.

That, somehow, hurt more than if she had.

“You scared us,” their mother said, rushing forward.

“I didn’t mean to,” Emily muttered.

Allison stopped a few feet away, hands clenched.

“I’m sorry,” Emily said suddenly, voice breaking. “I didn’t want to stop being mad, but I didn’t want you to worry.”

Allison’s chest ached.

“It’s okay,” she said, and waited.

Nothing shifted.

Emily sniffed. “You didn’t do the thing.”

Allison swallowed. “What thing?”

“The thing where everything just… goes quiet,” Emily said. “You let it be bad.”

Allison nodded, tears finally spilling over.

“I don’t know how to fix it,” she said.

Emily stood and walked into her arms.

Allison held her, shaking.

The night stayed messy.

---

Later, alone in her room, Allison opened her Notes app.

Her hands trembled as she typed.

> **Failure**  
>  
> Emily didn’t calm down.  
> The house stayed loud.  
>  
> No one thanked me.

She stared at the words.

Then she added:

> It didn’t end.

She didn’t delete it.

She set the phone down and lay back, staring at the ceiling crack.

For the first time, she didn’t wish it would disappear.
