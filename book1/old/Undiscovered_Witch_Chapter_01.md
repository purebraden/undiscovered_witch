# Chapter 1 — The Girl Who Fits Enough

The first thing Allison did every morning was check her phone.

Not because she was popular. Not because she expected a flood of messages. It was more like… habit. A reflex. The way you touch a bruise to see if it still hurts.

Notifications: three.

A weather alert. A reminder from the school app about attendance. And one from her Notes app, flagged because she’d starred it last night.

Don’t forget: you’re allowed to want things.

Allison stared at the line for a second, then locked her phone and rolled onto her back.

The ceiling above her was smooth except for one small crack near the corner—the kind that looked like a tiny lightning bolt. She’d stared at it so much she’d started to think she could map her moods by it. Some mornings it felt like a threat. Some mornings it was just… there.

Today it was just there.

She swung her legs over the side of the bed and listened.

The house was awake.

Not loudly—just enough. The sound of the coffee maker clicking. A cabinet door closing. The faint hum of the dryer in the laundry room downstairs. And, from the hallway, Emily’s music—thin through her closed door, as if she didn’t want anyone to hear and also wanted everyone to hear.

Allison pulled on her favorite sweatshirt, the one that was soft in the sleeves from being washed too many times. It wasn’t stylish. It wasn’t ugly. It was safe.

Safe was most of her wardrobe.

She padded into the bathroom and looked at herself in the mirror.

Her hair was blonde and long, usually too long, the kind that slipped out of ponytails and tangled if you looked at it wrong. She’d never dyed it or cut it dramatically or done any of the things girls in movies did when they wanted to become a new version of themselves. Her eyes were green, a little too bright when the light hit them.

For a second—just a second—she caught herself looking like someone else.

Like a girl in a book jacket.

Then the thought slipped away and she was just Allison again.

Allison Crites.

The name looked normal on forms. The name sounded normal when teachers called roll. It didn’t carry a story.

Survive. Blend. Don’t make a scene.

It wasn’t that she wanted to be invisible.

It was that she didn’t want to be wrong.

Downstairs, her mom was at the counter with her back turned, hair clipped up, wearing the cardigan she wore when she had early meetings.

“Morning,” Allison said.

Her mom turned. “Hey, honey.”

Her smile landed soft and practiced, like she was laying something carefully on a shelf.

Allison opened the fridge. Milk. Leftovers. A box of strawberries that looked like they were on their last day.

“Did you sleep?” her mom asked.

“Yeah.”

“Good.”

Allison grabbed a yogurt and a spoon and sat at the table.

Her dad came in a minute later, already in his work jacket, keys in hand, eyes flicking to the clock.

“Morning, kiddo,” he said, kissing the top of her head as he passed.

Kiddo.

Not Allison. Not honey. Not sweetheart. Kiddo was what you called someone when you didn’t want to get too specific.

Emily came down a few minutes later like a firework.

She didn’t walk so much as appear—hair messy, pajama pants loud, phone in hand, already talking as if she’d been mid-conversation since midnight.

“Mom, I need the purple hoodie. The one that makes my eyes look good. The gray one makes me look like—like a sad Victorian child.”

Her mom laughed. Real laughter, the kind that showed teeth. “It’s in the dryer. If you’d stop leaving clothes on the floor, you’d know where things were.”

Emily spun dramatically toward Allison. “Did you hear that? She’s attacking me. In the morning.”

Allison lifted her spoon. “I’m witnessing.”

Emily grinned, slid into the chair next to her, and stole one of Allison’s strawberries without asking. Allison didn’t stop her.

Emily was fourteen and somehow made every room feel like it was built for her. She had dark hair that fell in perfect waves like it had a personal assistant. She had her mother’s confidence and her father’s ability to move like the world expected her to. She was loud in a way that read as charming instead of annoying.

If Allison had been a different kind of person, she would’ve hated her.

Sometimes she tried to.

But mostly she just watched her like someone watching a show they weren’t allowed to be in.

“Are you coming to my game Friday?” Emily asked, already chewing.

“Allison has work,” her mom said immediately.

Allison paused.

“I—” she started, but her mom kept going.

“The school store needs her. She’s responsible.”

Emily rolled her eyes. “Of course. Allison is responsible. Allison is always responsible. Allison should get a trophy for being so responsible.”

The words weren’t mean, exactly.

But they landed like a label.

Responsible: the version of Allison people could explain.

Allison swallowed. “I can probably come after.”

Emily brightened. “Really?”

Allison’s dad glanced over. “If you’re not too tired.”

Allison nodded, even though she hadn’t been asked a question.

She looked down at her yogurt, suddenly not hungry.

Something about mornings always made her feel like she was watching other people’s lives happen around her. Like everyone else had a script and she was improvising, hoping no one noticed the hesitation.

The hallway smelled like floor cleaner and too many different perfumes layered on top of each other.

Allison moved through it the way she always did—shoulders slightly tucked in, eyes forward but unfocused, aware without appearing to be.

She preferred being unnoticed.

She paused near the guidance office, leaning her shoulder against the wall where the noise dimmed just enough to breathe.

She pulled out her phone and opened Notes.

Allison locked her phone and slid it back into her pocket. The hallway noise rushed back in, too loud after the quiet she’d made for herself.

She pushed off the wall and kept walking.

Kara didn’t say anything when Allison sat down at lunch.

That was one of the reasons Allison liked her.

The cafeteria was already loud—chairs scraping, voices overlapping, someone laughing too hard at something that wasn’t funny. Allison slid into the seat across from Kara, setting her tray down carefully like noise might spill out if she wasn’t gentle.

Kara was halfway through a carton of chocolate milk, eyes fixed on her phone. She glanced up, lifted her chin in a brief acknowledgment, then went back to whatever she was reading.

No questions.
No pressure.

They ate in silence.

Allison watched the room instead.

She felt like she was behind glass.

A raised voice near the exit snagged her attention.

Please don’t, she thought.

The argument fizzled out almost immediately.

Coincidence.

Her heart kept racing.

Kara slid a doodled napkin toward her.

“For you,” she said. “It’s supposed to be a dragon but I panicked.”

Allison smiled and folded it carefully, tucking it into her pocket.

The bell rang.

They walked to class together, shoulder to shoulder in the crowd.

Allison wasn’t alone.

And she still felt completely, terrifyingly isolated.

The hallway felt louder after lunch.

Not in a way anyone else would have noticed—just sharper at the edges, like sounds had gained teeth. Allison walked with Kara partway, then peeled off toward her next class.

She didn’t slow down until she reached the stairwell.

The air there was cooler, the concrete walls muting the noise into something manageable. She rested her hand against the railing.

A group of students crowded the landing above her, voices overlapping.

Allison took the first step up.

Someone laughed—too sharp. Someone else snapped back.

Her foot paused mid-step.

She didn’t want to be here.

She didn’t want to feel the pressure blooming behind her eyes.

Please don’t, she thought.

The voices cut off abruptly.

Allison froze.

For a heartbeat, the stairwell was completely silent.

Then one of the students scoffed and walked away.

Allison’s pulse roared.

By the time she reached class, her hands were shaking.

Dinner was spaghetti.

Emily talked nonstop.

Allison listened.

“You’re quiet,” Emily said.

“Just tired,” Allison replied.

Her mom nodded and turned back to Emily.

After dinner, Allison retreated to her room.

She lay on her bed and stared at the ceiling crack.

Later, when the house was quiet, Allison opened Notes again.

| new rule.

| don’t think things when you’re scared.
|
| fear feels too much like permission.
|
| i don’t know what happens if i want the wrong thing.

Allison locked her phone and set it down.

She curled under the blanket.

She didn’t know what she was.

But she knew this wasn’t going to stop.
