# CHAPTER 2 — Small Wishes

Allison did not sleep well.

She didn’t have nightmares—nothing cinematic or obvious. No screaming, no running, no monsters with teeth. Instead, she drifted in and out of shallow sleep, her mind snagging on fragments of thought that refused to stay quiet.

Did I do that?
He noticed.
Things don’t just change.

At some point near dawn, she dreamed she was standing in the cafeteria again, surrounded by noise that suddenly stopped—not because she asked it to, but because the sound itself decided she was done with it. The silence pressed in so hard it made her chest ache.

She woke up with her heart racing and her sheets tangled around her legs.

For a moment, she lay there staring at the ceiling crack, waiting for the familiar grounding sensation of normal to return.

It didn’t.

Her phone buzzed on the nightstand.

Allison flinched before she even registered the sound.

A text from Kara:

u alive today or should i bring snacks to your funeral

Allison exhaled a breath she hadn’t realized she was holding.

She typed back:

alive. emotionally pending.

Kara responded with a skull emoji and a heart.

Allison sat up and swung her legs over the bed.

The house was quiet again—too quiet. Emily must’ve already left for school early practice. Allison hadn’t heard her music. The absence felt loud.

She padded into the bathroom and caught her reflection mid-yawn.

She looked the same.

Same hair. Same face. Same faint shadows under her eyes.

That helped. A little.

She leaned closer to the mirror and lifted her hands, palms up, inspecting them like they might be different somehow.

They weren’t.

Her fingers trembled anyway.

“Get it together,” she whispered to herself.

The words echoed in the tiled room, sharper than she’d meant them to be.

Downstairs, her mom was already gone. A note sat on the counter beside the coffee maker.

Early meeting. You’re on your own this morning. Love you.

Allison stared at the handwriting—neat, looping, controlled. Her mom’s handwriting always looked like it belonged in a planner, not a letter.

She poured herself cereal and ate standing up, the crunch loud in the quiet kitchen.

Her phone buzzed again.

Another message—this one from an unknown number.

Allison froze.

She stared at the screen until the text preview appeared.

Guidance Office Reminder: Please stop by today if you have time.

Her stomach dropped.

She read it again.

No greeting. No name. Just… expectation.

If you have time.

As if she ever did.

Allison locked her phone and set the bowl in the sink untouched.

Her appetite was gone.

The walk to school felt longer than usual.

Every sound grated—cars passing too close, a dog barking behind a fence, the crunch of gravel under her shoes. Her senses felt dialed up, like the world had decided to test how much she could notice.

She passed the bridge and hesitated, remembering Evan standing there yesterday, his voice calm in a way that made her skin prickle.

Do you ever feel like things get quieter around you?

The memory tightened something in her chest.

She kept walking.

At school, the hallway buzzed with its usual chaos, but Allison felt like she was moving through a thinner version of it, like a copy printed on cheaper paper.

Kara spotted her immediately.

“There she is,” Kara announced, dramatic as ever. “The haunted one returns.”

Allison tried to smile. “You say that like it’s new.”

Kara leaned closer, lowering her voice. “Okay but seriously. Are you okay?”

Allison hesitated.

The easy answer sat right there.

Yeah. Just tired.

The real answer tangled in her throat.

“I don’t know,” she said instead.

Kara studied her face, eyes sharp despite the humor she usually wore like armor.

“Is this about yesterday?” Kara asked.

Allison swallowed. “Partly.”

Kara nodded slowly. “The cafeteria thing was… weird.”

Allison’s pulse jumped. “You noticed?”

“Not like—” Kara waved a hand vaguely. “Not noticed noticed. Just… the vibe shifted. That girl stopped looking at you like she wanted to start something.”

Allison’s mouth felt dry. “People change their minds all the time.”

“Yeah,” Kara said. “But that fast? With no reason?”

Allison didn’t answer.

They reached their lockers.

Kara leaned against the metal and lowered her voice further. “And the new guy? Evan? He was watching you.”

Allison’s fingers fumbled with her combination. “He was probably just… looking.”

Kara snorted. “That boy does not look at things accidentally.”

Allison closed her locker harder than necessary.

“Stop,” she said.

Kara raised her hands in surrender. “Okay. Okay. But if you suddenly discover you can read minds or something, I’m calling dibs on being your sidekick.”

Allison huffed a weak laugh.

Sidekick.

The word landed strange.

First period passed without incident.

Second period too.

Allison almost started to believe she’d imagined the whole thing.

Then, in third period history, it happened again.

They were discussing a group project—something about local history, small presentations. The teacher was trying to assign groups, the class restless and loud.

“I don’t want to work with him,” a girl across the room muttered, loud enough to hear. “He never does anything.”

The boy stiffened, face flushing.

The teacher opened her mouth to intervene.

Allison felt it before she thought it.

A tightening. A pull. Like a thread being drawn through her chest.

Please don’t let this turn into a thing.

The thought barely finished forming when the girl shrugged.

“Whatever,” she said suddenly. “I guess it’s fine.”

The tension evaporated.

The teacher blinked. “All right then.”

Allison’s pen slipped from her fingers.

Her heart slammed against her ribs.

She didn’t breathe until Kara nudged her under the desk.

“You okay?” Kara whispered.

Allison nodded too fast.

“Yes. No. Maybe.

She stared at the page in front of her, the words blurring.

This was not coincidence.

Coincidence didn’t feel like this—like something inside her was leaning forward, listening.

At lunch, Allison barely touched her food.

She watched the room the way someone watches a storm roll in—trying to predict where the damage would land.

Evan sat at a different table today.

Not close. Not far.

Close enough to see.

He wasn’t staring.

That was somehow worse.

He looked like he was… waiting.

Allison’s phone buzzed in her pocket.

The Notes app icon glowed on her lock screen.

She hadn’t starred this note.

Her stomach flipped.

She unlocked the phone and opened it.

A new entry.

Blank.

The cursor blinked.

Allison stared at it, fingers hovering.

Finally, she typed:

Theory

Either I am losing my mind
or something is listening.

She paused.

Deleted the last line.

Rewrote it.

or someone is listening.

Her pulse slowed as she wrote.

The fear didn’t go away—but it sharpened into something more manageable. Like naming it gave her leverage.

She added:

I need rules.
I need to know what I’m allowed to think.

That thought scared her more than the others.

She locked her phone and shoved it back into her pocket.

Across the cafeteria, Evan finally looked up.

Their eyes met.

This time, he didn’t look away.

Allison’s stomach twisted.

He nodded once—small, almost imperceptible.

Like acknowledgment.

The guidance office smelled like lemon cleaner and stale coffee.

Allison sat in the chair outside, knees bouncing, staring at a motivational poster on the wall.

YOU MATTER.

She wondered who decided that.

The counselor—Mrs. Hayes—opened the door with a polite smile.

“Allison! Come in.”

The office was warm and too neat, everything in its place.

“Allison,” Mrs. Hayes said, folding her hands. “How are you feeling lately?”

The question sounded casual.

It wasn’t.

“I’m fine,” Allison said automatically.

Mrs. Hayes smiled. “You’re doing very well academically. Teachers have noticed your calm presence.”

Allison stiffened.

“Calm,” Mrs. Hayes continued, “can be a real asset in group settings.”

Allison nodded, unsure what she was agreeing to.

“We’re exploring some peer-support initiatives,” Mrs. Hayes said. “Nothing formal yet. Just… noticing students who naturally help others regulate.”

Regulate.

The word scraped.

“I don’t really—” Allison began.

Mrs. Hayes raised a hand gently. “No pressure. Just something to think about.”

Allison’s chest tightened.

She thought of the way arguments fizzled. The way tension slid off people when she wished it would.

“I’m not—” Allison stopped herself. She didn’t know how to finish the sentence.

Mrs. Hayes watched her carefully.

“You don’t have to decide anything,” she said. “You have time.”

Allison nodded.

She left the office feeling like she’d agreed to something she couldn’t name.

That night, Allison lay on her bed with the lights off, phone glowing in the dark.

She opened Notes again.

Typed:

Rule Attempt #1

Don’t think things you don’t want to happen.

She laughed quietly, the sound brittle.

Deleted it.

Typed again:

Rule Attempt #1

If I don’t mean it, it doesn’t count.

She stared at the line.

Did she mean things when she thought them?

Did wishing something gone count as wanting it gone?

She added another line.

I miss when my thoughts were private.

The cursor blinked.

Patient.

Allison turned the phone face down.

In the quiet of her room, she felt the weight of something pressing in—not from outside, but from within.

Like the world had leaned just a little closer.

And was waiting to see what she would do next.
