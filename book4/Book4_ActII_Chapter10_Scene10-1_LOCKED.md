## Scene 10.1 — The Prohibition

They did not return to the agreeable room.

That was the first difference Allison noticed, and it mattered more than the change in corridor. The space Tomas guided her toward was smaller, less curated. Still calm. Still human-scaled. But without the warmth that suggested someone wanted her to feel comfortable about agreeing.

There was no table this time. Only three chairs arranged in a shallow arc, all facing the same direction.

Facing a wall panel that wasn’t lit yet.

Consent optics again, Allison thought, and felt the reflexive discomfort at how quickly the pattern presented itself. She was learning to recognize design the way she recognized magic: structure first, intention second.

Tomas stopped just outside the doorway.

He didn’t enter immediately.

That pause was not for her benefit. It felt like a timing check—an internal alignment, not magical, but procedural. Then he stepped in with her, and the door sealed behind them with a soft sound that wasn’t quite a lock.

A man stood near the panel. He looked unremarkable by Confluence standards: calm posture, neutral expression, presence tuned to avoid dominance. If he had been chosen for anything, it was his ability to make restriction sound like care.

“Allison,” he said, as if they had already met. “Thank you for your flexibility.”

Allison sat in the chair closest to the door without being directed. Tomas remained behind and slightly to the side, where his presence was unmistakable and his authority clearly limited.

“This will be brief,” the man said. “We’re issuing operational guidance. Not commentary.”

Operational guidance meant it would apply whether she agreed with it or not.

The wall panel illuminated without fanfare. Not data. Not graphs. A short list of statements, arranged like policy but written like conversation.

The man didn’t look at the panel when he spoke. He watched Allison instead.

“We are continuing observation under current parameters,” he said. “That remains appropriate.”

Appropriate.

“And we are limiting exposure to non-instructional contexts,” he continued, “until review concludes.”

Allison held her face steady. The phrasing was clean enough that she could feel the edges of what it contained.

“Specifically,” he said, “you will not pursue additional alignment pathways.”

The words were simple.

Allison felt them land like something physical.

She did not ask what he meant. She already knew. She had felt the pathways. She had recognized their shape in her own awareness, the way possibility gathered at the edges of a moment.

She kept her hands folded.

The man continued, tone unchanged. “You will not attempt to deepen recognition beyond supervised sessions. You will not attempt to reproduce or refine what has been observed.”

Refine.

As if she had been doing this on purpose.

“As we stated previously,” the man added, “your compliance is not in question. This is not responsive. It is precautionary.”

Precautionary was one of those words that sounded reasonable in any context. It made restriction feel like maturity.

Tomas spoke once, a narrow correction. “For clarity,” he said, “recognition events may still occur involuntarily.”

The man inclined his head, as if grateful for the precision. “Correct,” he said. “Recognition may occur. The constraint is on pursuit.”

Pursuit.

Allison felt the word tighten around her chest.

She was being told she could not want something too openly. She could not lean toward it. She could not ask herself the next question in a way that became observable.

The man’s gaze remained steady. “This guidance is being issued for stability.”

Allison heard the repetition of that word as a kind of seal. Not explanation. Not justification. A category that overrode discussion.

“You are not being punished,” the man said, as if reading her thoughts. “You are being protected from premature escalation.”

Allison almost asked: protected by whom? from whom?

The questions rose and fell inside her without reaching her mouth.

“Premature escalation,” the man continued, “creates interpretive conflict. It forces classification before we are prepared to classify.”

The admission was quiet and unsettling: they didn’t know what she was, not in their own terms. And they were solving that uncertainty the only way institutions solved uncertainty.

By narrowing.

The panel updated slightly—one line shifting down as if someone had inserted it after the meeting began.

“Any deviation from this guidance,” the man said, “will result in suspension of exposure until further review.”

Allison’s pulse ticked up.

She did not react.

Further review could mean anything. Further review was a hallway that didn’t end.

The man’s tone softened by half a degree. Not warmth. Just care in delivery. “We will reassess,” he said, “when conditions support it.”

When conditions support it.

Not when she was ready. Not when she asked. When conditions—external, institutional, undefined—shifted.

Allison nodded once. A small motion. Minimal, safe.

“Thank you,” the man said immediately, as if her nod had completed a contract. “That is the response we expected.”

Allison felt the quiet pressure of being thanked for agreement she had not yet given.

Tomas remained silent.

The man glanced briefly toward him, then back to Allison. “Tomas will continue to supervise your sessions,” he said. “And he will continue to stop them early when necessary.”

As if ending her access to experience were a routine safety feature. As if Tomas’s caution were part of the design, not a personal intervention.

Tomas’s jaw tightened once—so small Allison almost missed it.

“Yes,” Tomas said.

The man smiled faintly. “We appreciate your cooperation,” he said to Allison. “It helps maintain stability for all parties.”

All parties.

Allison understood the phrase as a quiet reminder: the Fairy World was an interested party. The Confluence was an interested party. She was not the only one affected by her recognition.

Maybe she wasn’t even the primary one.

The panel dimmed.

The meeting ended without ending. No dismissal. No closing words. Just the cessation of instruction.

Allison stood because there was nothing else to do.

Tomas placed himself beside her—not touching, not leading, simply aligning his position with hers like a shield that wasn’t allowed to be visible.

As they left the room, the man spoke one last time.

“Allison,” he said, tone unchanged. “This is temporary.”

Allison turned her head just enough to acknowledge him.

Temporary was a word people used when they wanted you to stop asking how long.

She nodded again.

In the corridor, the hum felt louder. Allison realized she had been holding her breath.

Tomas did not speak until they were far enough away that no panel could activate without warning.

Then he said, very quietly, “Do not interpret ‘temporary’ as permission.”

Allison’s throat tightened.

“I won’t,” she said.

Tomas looked ahead, not at her. “Good,” he said. “Because they will.”
