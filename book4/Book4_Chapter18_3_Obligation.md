Chapter 18.3 — Obligation

Naelis does not explain obligation by defining it.

She lets Allison encounter it.

They remain near the threshold—not pressed up against it, not withdrawn. Close enough that the Fairy World breathes around them, far enough that nothing is expected of Allison beyond attention. Tomas is still present at her back, steady and silent, a reminder that this moment exists within a structure even as it stretches it.

Naelis gestures lightly, not to a person but to a pattern.

A group nearby has gathered around something low to the ground—an uneven place where the land dips and light behaves strangely, refracting in ways that suggest something unresolved. No one is panicking. No one is rushing. The fairies there move with purpose, but not urgency, each action small and precise.

“They will tend that for a while,” Naelis says.

Allison watches. “Is it broken?”

Naelis considers the word. “It is strained.”

“What’s the difference?”

“Broken things ask to be fixed,” Naelis replies. “Strained things ask to be held.”

Allison absorbs that, watching as one fairy adjusts something subtle—placing a hand, murmuring a word that seems less like a spell and more like a reminder. Another arrives later, adding nothing new, simply staying.

No one assigns roles.

No one gives orders.

“They aren’t being directed,” Allison observes.

“No,” Naelis agrees. “They are responding.”

“Because it’s their responsibility?”

Naelis tilts her head. “Because it is theirs.”

That feels important.

Allison frowns slightly. “So obligation is ownership?”

Naelis shakes her head. “Ownership implies choice. Obligation precedes it.”

Allison turns that over. “Then what creates it?”

Naelis gestures again, broader this time, encompassing the space, the people, the strain in the light. “Presence,” she says. “Attention. The fact of being able to affect something.”

“That sounds…unfair,” Allison says slowly.

Naelis’s mouth curves faintly. “Yes.”

The simplicity of the agreement startles her.

“You don’t try to make it fair,” Allison says.

“No,” Naelis replies. “We try to make it honest.”

They watch as one fairy steps back from the strained place, another stepping in without comment. There is no sense of completion, only continuation.

“If someone ignored it?” Allison asks.

“They would still be obligated,” Naelis says. “They would simply be failing.”

Allison winces at the bluntness. “There’s no neutrality.”

Naelis looks at her directly. “Neutrality is a story people tell themselves when they benefit from not choosing.”

That lands with uncomfortable familiarity.

Allison thinks of the Confluence. Of silence framed as caution. Of Evan putting himself on record so that someone would have to carry what had already been placed.

“So obligation isn’t about being told what to do,” she says.

“No.”

“And it’s not about fixing everything you notice.”

“Certainly not.”

“Then how do you know what you’re obligated to do?”

Naelis is quiet for a moment. When she speaks, her voice is softer—not evasive, but precise. “You listen for what would be worse if you walked away.”

Allison feels that settle deep, like a stone dropped into still water.

“What if everything would be worse?” she asks.

Naelis meets her gaze. “Then you choose where your attention does the least harm.”

That answer doesn’t comfort her.

It steadies her.

Allison looks back toward the strained place. The fairies there are still tending it, unremarkable in their persistence. No one thanks them. No one relieves them. Others come and go as needed.

“And you don’t resent this?” Allison asks.

Naelis follows her gaze. “Resentment comes from pretending obligation was optional,” she says. “We do not pretend.”

The Fairy World hums around them, alive with countless such moments—small strains held, quiet continuities maintained. Allison realizes that none of this would read as heroic from the outside. It would be easy to miss. Easy to dismiss.

“Back home,” she says, choosing her words carefully, “responsibility usually comes with authority.”

Naelis’s expression sharpens—not unkindly. “That is a human preference.”

“And here?”

“Here,” Naelis says, “authority follows endurance.”

Allison breathes out slowly. She understands now why Fairy obligation is not taught in lectures. It cannot be. It has to be witnessed, felt, recognized in the body.

She becomes aware again of Tomas—still silent, still watching. She doesn’t look at him, but she feels the shape of his attention shift, like someone noting a threshold crossed internally even if no one has moved.

Naelis turns slightly toward Allison. “You asked what Fairy is like when nothing is broken,” she says. “This is it.”

Allison nods. “And when something is?”

Naelis’s smile is small and unsentimental. “Then we do this louder.”

The words linger.

Allison doesn’t reach forward. She doesn’t step in. But something in her has aligned, quietly and irrevocably.

Obligation, she understands now, isn’t a demand.

It’s the refusal to look away once you know you matter.
