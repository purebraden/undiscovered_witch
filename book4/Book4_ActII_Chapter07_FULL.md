# Chapter 7 — Orientation

The orientation room was warmer than the briefing spaces.

Not noticeably so at first—just enough that Allison became aware of the difference after she had already sat down. The stone walls carried a softer hue, the lighting angled to reduce shadow rather than eliminate it. The chairs were wider, their surfaces responsive in a way that adjusted slowly instead of instantly, as if comfort were something to be negotiated rather than provided.

Allison noticed all of that without meaning to.

She also noticed that the room still had no windows.

“This space is designed to be supportive,” Lysa Merewen said, her voice calm and careful as she took the seat across from Allison. “Orientation can feel overwhelming if it’s handled too clinically.”

Tomas stood near the wall to Allison’s left, hands folded, posture unchanged from the earlier briefings. He did not sit. He did not speak.

Allison nodded once. Supportive, she thought, did not mean permissive.

“This isn’t training,” Lysa continued, as if reading from a script she had refined over time. “And it’s not assessment. Orientation is about context—about making sure you understand the framework you’re operating within.”

Allison felt a faint tightening at the word operating.

“You are not expected to perform,” Lysa added quickly. “You’re not here to demonstrate anything. What you experienced earlier was a controlled exposure, nothing more.”

Tomas shifted his weight almost imperceptibly.

“Within approved parameters,” Lysa amended.

Allison folded her hands together, resting them in her lap. “What parameters?”

Lysa smiled, not dismissively, but with practiced patience. “Those will be introduced gradually.”

“The Confluence,” Lysa continued, “has a responsibility to ensure that individuals under its protection are not placed at unnecessary risk. That includes cognitive risk, emotional strain, and structural exposure.”

Tomas spoke without turning his head. “Clarify.”

Lysa nodded. “Structural exposure refers to prolonged contact with systems that operate on incompatible assumptions.”

“Incompatible with what?” Allison asked.

“With each other,” Lysa replied. “And, sometimes, with the individual.”

“This is why orientation matters,” Lysa said. “We want to ensure that what you notice doesn’t become burdensome.”

“Noticing already feels burdensome,” Allison said.

“That’s understandable,” Lysa replied. “But noticing doesn’t have to mean responsibility.”

“Noticing creates responsibility regardless of intent,” Tomas said.

The difference in emphasis lingered between them.

“This orientation,” Lysa said gently, “is meant to help you remain grounded while we proceed carefully. You are not alone in this process.”

“You’re helping us understand how recognition functions,” Lysa added, earnest.

“Allison is not here to help us,” Tomas said. “She is here to be protected.”

“I didn’t mean to confirm anything,” Allison said quietly.

“That’s part of what makes it useful,” Lysa said.

“This concludes the first orientation segment,” Lysa said after a pause.

“That is sufficient,” Tomas agreed.

As Allison stepped back into the corridor, she felt the quiet sense of being accounted for.

---

The second room was smaller.

Less distance between surfaces—not cramped, but arranged so her attention had nowhere to wander. The table was narrower. The chairs were closer together. Even the ceiling felt lower.

“This portion of orientation is interactive,” Lysa said. “Within limits.”

“How often will exposures happen?” Allison asked.

“At first, infrequently,” Lysa said. “Later will depend on stability indicators.”

“Who decides when something is stable?” Allison asked.

“That’s a shared determination,” Lysa replied. “Patterns, not moments.”

“Does nothing going wrong count?” Allison asked.

“Yes,” Lysa said. “It still informs the model.”

“And if something does go wrong?”

“Exposure will be suspended,” Tomas said.

“How quickly?”

“As close to immediately as the system allows.”

“What happens if I notice something outside an exposure?”

“You disengage,” Tomas said.

“And report?”

“Yes. If and when you are asked.”

“We don’t want you monitoring yourself,” Lysa added. “You can think. Just not toward action.”

Allison realized then that her questions were data.

She would ask less.

---

The third space avoided being called a data room.

Light fell in precise bands across the far wall, resolving into abstract lines that shifted subtly.

“This is aggregated,” Lysa said. “Trend-focused.”

Allison took a step without realizing it.

“Do not step closer,” Tomas said sharply.

“This suggests recognition alters outcomes,” Lysa said. “Without escalation.”

“Allison does not need comparative baselines,” Tomas said.

“You’re helping us see what’s possible,” Lysa said.

Allison understood then that she was not a participant.

She was a proof.

---

Her assigned room was exactly the size it needed to be.

No more. No less.

The walls were pale stone, unmarked. The light adjusted but did not soften further. A narrow bed. A single chair. Nothing personal.

She stood for a moment, then sat.

The room did not react.

Remain as you are.

The phrase surfaced without invitation. Allison did not examine it.

She felt, distantly, that something about her awareness had shifted—not expanded, not sharpened, just adjusted.

She did nothing with that knowledge.

No one came to check on her.

No one asked what she was thinking.

She lay back on the bed, hands folded, eyes on the ceiling.

She was aware, unchanged, and certain that whatever mattered had already been recorded.

She waited.
