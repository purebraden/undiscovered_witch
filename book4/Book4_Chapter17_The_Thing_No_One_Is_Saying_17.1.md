### Chapter 25.1 — The Honest Conversation (Iria)

The room Iria chose wasn’t one of the briefing chambers.

Allison knew what those felt like now—the way they were built to be clean, neutral, unmemorable. Rooms that kept your eyes moving and your thoughts from settling anywhere too long.

This space did the opposite.

It was smaller than the briefing rooms but not tighter. The walls weren’t polished smooth; they held faint striations, like stone that had been allowed to keep its history. There was a single window—real, not conjured—cut into one wall at shoulder height. Beyond it, Allison could see only sky and a suggestion of distance, as if the Confluence had decided that sometimes it helped to remember there was an outside.

Two chairs, not three. No table.

That detail mattered so much that Allison felt it in her throat before she understood why.

Iria Vell was already there, seated with her hands folded loosely in her lap, her posture relaxed in a way that did not invite Allison to relax with her. It was a posture that said I won’t lean on you. I won’t push you. Not because Iria was kind, exactly—but because she was careful about power.

Tomas waited in the corridor.

He didn’t hover. He didn’t loom. He stood just outside the threshold, visible if Allison turned her head, present without claiming the room.

Which meant, in Confluence language, this is allowed—and also this is not yours.

“Allison,” Iria said, and used her name like it belonged to a person, not a file. “Thank you for coming.”

Allison stepped in and stopped just inside the doorway, a habit she couldn’t seem to break. The room didn’t tighten around her the way some spaces did. It didn’t adjust its attention. It simply… registered.

“I didn’t have a choice,” Allison said before she could soften it.

Iria’s mouth curved slightly. Not a smile. An acknowledgment.

“No,” Iria agreed. “You didn’t.”

That honesty landed harder than anything comforting would have.

Allison moved toward the second chair and sat slowly, as if her pace might matter. Her hands folded in her lap on instinct. She felt herself trying to become orderly.

She caught it. Why am I doing that? The thought came sharp, then quieted as quickly as it arrived.

Iria watched her—not like a monitor, not like a test, but like someone reading the shape of a decision being made too early.

“This is not disciplinary,” Iria said.

Allison’s fingers tightened anyway. “Everyone keeps saying that.”

“Yes,” Iria said simply. “Because you keep needing to hear it.”

Allison swallowed. The bluntness made her eyes sting, which was ridiculous. Nothing had happened. Nothing was happening.

And yet she felt like she’d been standing at the edge of something for days, trying not to look down.

Iria leaned back slightly in her chair, creating space. “I asked for this conversation because the silence is doing damage.”

Allison blinked. “The silence?”

“The parts we’ve withheld,” Iria clarified. “The parts we’ve circled. The way we’ve used careful language to avoid… accelerating.”

Allison’s chest tightened at that word. “Accelerating what?”

Iria did not answer immediately. She let the question exist without grabbing it and turning it into a rule.

Then she said, “Responsibility.”

Allison stared at her. The word didn’t fit the feeling Allison had been carrying. She had expected Iria to say danger. Or risk. Or something like stability, which was Confluence code for we’re afraid but we won’t admit what of.

Responsibility didn’t feel like code.

It felt like a door opening into a room Allison hadn’t been allowed to notice.

“I don’t understand,” Allison said carefully. “I haven’t done anything.”

“I know,” Iria said, and the certainty in it made Allison’s shoulders sag a fraction.

Allison forced them back into place. She hated that she did that now—correcting herself before anyone asked.

Iria’s eyes flicked to Allison’s shoulders, then away. She didn’t comment. But Allison felt seen.

“I’m going to say something plainly,” Iria said. “And I need you to listen without trying to solve it.”

Allison nodded once.

Iria’s gaze held steady. “You are not being protected from danger.”

The sentence landed clean. Not comforting. Not cruel.

Just true.

Allison waited for the second half.

Iria gave it to her.

“You are being protected from responsibility.”

Allison’s mouth went dry. “That doesn’t—” She stopped. She tried again. “How is responsibility dangerous?”

Iria exhaled through her nose, the closest thing to frustration Allison had ever heard from her. “It isn’t. Not inherently.”

She paused, then corrected herself the way Tomas did—like precision mattered more than pride.

“It isn’t inherently dangerous to you,” Iria said. “It is dangerous to systems.”

Allison stared at the window. Sky. Distance. The idea of places not arranged around her.

Systems, she thought. Confluence systems. Fairy systems. Whatever else existed beyond the Confluence’s stone.

“You’re afraid I’ll break something,” Allison said.

Iria didn’t flinch. “We’re afraid you’ll be made responsible for something.”

That hit differently.

Allison turned back. “Made?”

“Yes,” Iria said. “Because you’re already being treated as… a possibility.”

Allison’s throat tightened. “A possibility of what?”

Iria’s jaw set for a moment, like she was choosing what to say and what not to say, not for secrecy’s sake, but because truth here could behave like a catalyst.

“Of authorization,” Iria said.

Allison frowned. “I don’t have authorization.”

“No,” Iria agreed. “You don’t.”

Then she added, very quietly, “But authorization isn’t always granted by us.”

Allison felt cold in her hands.

She thought of the Fairy World’s attention—how it had accounted for her without asking. She thought of words spoken there that weren’t questions.

Allison looked down at her fingers, folded too neatly. “So what is everyone scared of, then?”

Iria’s gaze softened by a fraction. “Precedent.”

Allison blinked. “Precedent like… laws?”

“Precedent like patterns,” Iria said. “Like a system that learns something and then can’t unlearn it.”

Allison’s stomach turned. “About me.”

“About what happens around you,” Iria corrected. “That distinction matters.”

Allison wasn’t sure it did.

Iria continued, voice steady. “There have been cases—rare cases—where someone arrives in a system and the system begins to re-evaluate what it considers permissible.”

Allison kept still.

“Most people,” Iria said, “are contained by ignorance. By distance. By not knowing what they’re near.”

“And some people,” Iria said, “are contained by caution. They learn restraint quickly. They don’t push. They don’t test. They don’t reach.”

Allison’s throat tightened. “That’s good.”

“It is good,” Iria said. “And it’s also what makes this worse.”

Allison stared at her. “Why?”

“Because you are not escalating,” Iria said. “You are not acting. You are not trying to gain leverage.”

Allison waited.

“You are doing what the Confluence values,” Iria said. “You are behaving.”

Allison flinched.

“And that means when the system responds to you, we can’t dismiss it as provocation.”

Allison whispered, “The system responds to me?”

“Yes.”

Allison’s breath caught.

“That’s why Tomas watches your attention,” Iria said. “That’s why you get measured for recognition instead of action.”

Allison felt her eyes burn.

“Because something did happen,” Allison whispered.

Iria nodded.

“But I didn’t do anything.”

“I know,” Iria said. “That’s what I’m trying to get you to understand.”

Allison stared at the window again.

“Allison,” Iria said. “The Confluence is not afraid you’ll hurt someone. The Confluence is afraid someone will decide you are responsible for stabilizing what you notice.”

Allison’s stomach dropped.

“That sounds like a job,” Allison said faintly.

“It sounds like a role,” Iria corrected.

Allison laughed once, brittle. “So why are you keeping me quiet?”

“Because the moment you start planning,” Iria said, “your planning becomes part of the system’s evidence.”

Allison froze.

“We have been delaying,” Iria said, “because we know what happens when a system recognizes an ethical actor.”

Allison whispered, “Ethical actor?”

“You keep asking what you should do,” Iria said. “That is the shape of a person systems lean on.”

Allison sat very still.

“So you’re keeping me contained,” Allison said slowly, “to protect me from being used.”

“Yes,” Iria said.

Allison’s throat ached. “I thought if I followed the rules, things would calm down.”

“They would,” Iria said, “if the only danger was you.”

Silence settled.

“This is the thing no one has been saying,” Iria said.

Allison looked at the slice of sky again.

The world was already moving.

She kept her hands open on her knees.

Not acting.

Not disappearing.

Just staying.
