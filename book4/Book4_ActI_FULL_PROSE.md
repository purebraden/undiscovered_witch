# Reintegration

The invitation did not include instructions.

That was the first thing Allison noticed.

It arrived through the same channel the Confluence used for confirmations and updates—clean formatting, neutral tone, no greeting line to soften it. The message did not tell her where to go, only when she was expected to arrive. It did not frame her return as welcome. It did not acknowledge what it cost to come back or what it might cost to stay.

It simply stated that arrangements had been made.

Allison read it twice, then a third time more slowly, as if repetition might reveal an implied directive she had missed. It did not. The wording remained exact, complete in its incompleteness.

She closed the slate and stood still for a moment, letting the absence of instruction settle into her chest.

In her old life, not knowing what to do next would have felt like a gap to fill. Here, it felt like the first step in a process that expected her to learn patience as a form of cooperation.

She packed with care.

Not because she needed much—most of what she owned had already been placed elsewhere during the weeks after her departure, when she had not known where she would be staying—but because packing gave her something sequential to do. Fold. Stack. Place. Objects arranged so they could be found again without thought.

She chose clothing that would not draw attention. Soft, neutral colors. Nothing that suggested she was trying to belong. Nothing that suggested she was trying not to.

She set her notes into her bag last, not because she expected to use them, but because having them close made her feel less like she was walking into something empty-handed.

When she finished, she stood in the center of the room and waited.

She was not waiting for a message—there had already been one. She was waiting because moving without direction felt premature, and because the Confluence had taught her that premature movement created friction that would be corrected anyway.

Waiting, she reminded herself, could be chosen.

She recognized the thought as it formed.

*Chosen* was the word she used when she needed to believe she still owned the shape of her day. It was not untrue. But it was also a way of smoothing over the fact that she was responding to something she had not been asked about.

Allison held that awareness quietly and did not change what she was doing.

She waited until the time was close enough to expected arrival that leaving would not become its own question.

Then she left.

---

The transport arrived on time.

No one introduced themselves. No route was explained. The doors opened, Allison stepped inside, and the doors closed again without comment. She sat with her hands folded in her lap and watched the city slide past through tinted glass, familiar streets giving way to quieter roads and then to spaces where the edges of the world felt less defined.

The Confluence did not announce itself.

There was no visible threshold. No sign. No marker. Only a subtle change in air pressure and the sensation—quiet but unmistakable—that she had entered a system that would continue whether or not she understood it.

The vehicle stopped.

The doors opened.

Allison stepped out.

And waited.

There was an open area beyond the drop point that looked intentionally plain. A corridor mouth, a wide section of floor, a few chairs placed too far apart to suggest comfort. The space did not invite conversation. It did not invite complaint. It invited stillness.

Allison stood where she had been placed.

Five minutes passed.

Then ten.

People moved around her without urgency. Some nodded in acknowledgment, others passed as if she were already accounted for. No one hurried. No one apologized. Allison adjusted her stance, shifted her weight, and kept her gaze neutral.

Waiting was not presented as an inconvenience.

It was simply the condition she now occupied.

A woman approached, slate held at chest height.

“Allison Crites,” she said.

“Yes,” Allison replied.

“Please confirm,” the woman said.

Allison confirmed her identity.

The woman tapped the slate once. “Thank you. Please remain here.”

She turned and walked away.

Allison did not ask how long.

She waited.

At first she counted breaths, then stopped when counting began to feel like impatience wearing a disguise. She watched a pair of Confluence members pass with a cart of sealed containers, moving as if the weight of the contents mattered more than speed. She watched a small group stop near a doorway, exchange three sentences, and separate without any visible resolution. She watched someone pause at a wall panel, adjust a setting, and move on as if they were resetting air circulation rather than something more complex.

A door opened somewhere behind her and closed again. Soft footsteps crossed the corridor. A distant voice spoke a name she didn’t recognize. Then quiet returned.

Time passed in small, unmarked increments.

If she were in distress, she thought, would anyone notice?

Then she corrected herself: they would notice. They just might not respond in the way she expected.

Allison forced her shoulders to loosen and let her hands hang at her sides instead of clasped in front of her. She wasn’t on display. She was simply present.

Thirty minutes after she had arrived—she knew because her watch told her, not because anything else changed—a different liaison approached. This one was older, carrying himself with the unhurried confidence of someone whose job was to manage waiting, not solve it.

“Allison Crites,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Follow,” he said.

No greeting. No apology. No explanation.

Allison followed.

The corridor they entered looked identical to the one she had been standing near. Walls the same color. Floor the same dull stone. Lights the same muted intensity. Direction was conveyed by subtle shifts in the architecture—door placements, the presence of wall panels, the occasional etched symbol that meant nothing to her but clearly meant something to everyone else.

They stopped at a desk.

No one behind it looked up immediately.

The liaison did not speak. He stood beside Allison as if they had all the time in the world.

They waited.

When the person behind the desk finally looked up, her expression was polite and blank.

“Allison Crites,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Please state it,” she said, without changing tone.

“Allison Crites.”

The clerk nodded and entered it into her slate.

“Confirm date of birth,” she said.

Allison answered.

“Confirm place of residence prior to this arrival,” the clerk said.

Allison hesitated. “Do you mean my last address?”

The clerk looked down at her slate. “Confirm place of residence prior to this arrival,” she repeated.

Allison gave the address.

The clerk entered it without comment.

“Confirm relationship status to the Confluence,” the clerk said.

Allison’s throat tightened slightly. The wording was precise in a way that made her feel less like a person and more like a case file.

“I have been under Confluence oversight,” Allison said carefully. “By invitation.”

The clerk paused, then said, “Confirm.”

Allison repeated the sentence.

The clerk entered it.

“Confirm your understanding that all access is tiered,” the clerk continued, “and will be adjusted without notice based on review.”

“I understand,” Allison said.

The clerk’s fingers moved. “Confirm again.”

Allison repeated it.

The clerk nodded, as if the second statement had been the one that mattered.

The liaison beside Allison shifted slightly—an almost imperceptible movement that suggested this was normal.

Normal for them.

Allison’s mind offered her the word again: *chosen.*

Waiting was chosen. Compliance was chosen. Repetition was chosen.

She recognized the way she used the word to make something passive feel active.

And she did not stop.

The clerk continued. “Confirm your awareness that your classification is provisional.”

“I’m aware,” Allison said.

“State it as a sentence,” the clerk replied.

Allison took a breath. “My classification is provisional.”

The clerk entered it.

“Confirm your awareness that your quarters assignment may change,” the clerk said.

Allison nodded once and forced herself to speak instead of gesturing. “I’m aware my quarters assignment may change.”

“Confirm,” the clerk said.

Allison repeated it.

After several more lines of this—details, confirmations, confirmations of confirmations—the clerk set her slate down.

“Hold,” she said.

They waited.

The clerk stood, walked to a side panel, touched it, paused, then returned and sat again. She picked up her slate and glanced down at it.

“Proceeding,” she said.

The liaison turned slightly toward Allison. “Follow.”

They walked again.

Down another corridor.

Stopped at another desk.

This one was smaller, and the person behind it did not speak at first. He stared at the slate in front of him as if it contained something difficult to align.

The liaison did not fill the silence.

Allison waited.

Finally, the man spoke without looking up. “Allison Crites.”

“Yes.”

“Confirm,” he said.

Allison confirmed.

“State classification,” he said.

“My classification is provisional,” Allison replied.

There was a pause.

“State it with full designation,” the man said.

Allison’s pulse ticked faster. “Integrated resident… provisional?” she said, testing the shape of the phrase.

The liaison spoke for the first time since entering the room. “Integrated Resident, Provisional,” he said, tone even.

Allison repeated it. “Integrated Resident, Provisional.”

The man entered it.

Then he asked, “Confirm.”

Allison repeated it again.

The man looked up for the first time, eyes flicking over her as if she were a diagram that did not match the record in front of him.

“Consent scope unchanged,” he said.

It wasn’t a question. It still wanted an answer.

“Yes,” Allison said. “My consent scope is unchanged.”

The man nodded and entered it.

“All access remains tiered,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Confirm.”

“All access remains tiered,” Allison repeated.

“Temporary quarters assigned,” the man said. “Duration undetermined.”

Allison waited for the follow-up.

There wasn’t one.

The man tapped the slate once. “Proceed.”

The liaison gestured. “This way.”

They walked.

They stopped.

They waited at a door.

The liaison did not open it immediately. He stood beside it as if opening it required a timing Allison did not understand.

Allison stood beside him and waited.

Finally, the door opened.

The room beyond was not her old quarters.

It was smaller. Cleaner. Furnished with the bare minimum: a narrow bed, a desk, a cleared surface already prepared for paperwork she had not yet been given.

“This space is provisional,” the liaison said. “Do not make alterations.”

“I won’t,” Allison said.

“You may be asked to relocate with limited notice,” the liaison added.

“I understand.”

The liaison hesitated for half a second, then said, “You will remain available.”

Available.

It sounded polite. It functioned as expectation.

Allison nodded.

The liaison stepped out. The door closed with a soft, final sound.

Allison remained standing.

She did not unpack.

For a moment she held still, listening to the quiet, feeling how quickly her body tried to interpret it as safety. A closed door. A room. A place to sit.

Then she noticed what wasn’t there.

No schedule.  
No next instruction.  
No time window.  
No person to ask.

Temporary quarters assigned, and then nothing.

This was the false completion: paperwork that implied arrival, and silence that proved she was still in process.

Allison walked to the desk and set her bag down carefully. She placed her notes on the cleared surface as if claiming a small piece of the room through habit, then stopped and looked at her own hands.

She was doing what she always did when her environment shifted: creating a pattern. Establishing a routine. Making the unknown feel manageable.

It was coping.

It was also compliance.

If she unpacked, she would be demonstrating readiness to stay available.

If she didn’t unpack, she would be demonstrating readiness to move.

Either way, she would be demonstrating something.

Allison sat in the chair and waited.

Five minutes passed.

Then ten.

No one came.

She considered leaving the room to find someone—then remembered the intake clerk’s words, and the liaison’s last instruction.

Remain available.

Available to be found, she realized. Available to be redirected.

She waited.

The silence stretched long enough that her mind began to invent reasons for it. They were finishing review. They were checking a name. They were matching records across departments. They were deciding how close she should be kept to the center of the Confluence, how much access she should have, which corridors she should be allowed to walk without an escort.

She stopped herself.

Speculation didn’t change procedure. It only changed her.

Allison breathed in slowly and let her shoulders drop.

Waiting, she told herself, could be chosen.

She recognized the thought immediately, almost with irritation this time. There it was again—her mind trying to turn imposed stillness into voluntary discipline.

She didn’t correct it.

She let the word sit in her head, acknowledged it as a tool, and used it anyway.

An hour after she had been left alone, a soft tone sounded from the door panel. Not an alarm. Not a knock. A notification.

Allison stood.

She did not open the door until the tone repeated.

When it did, she stepped to the panel and placed her hand where the symbol indicated.

The door opened.

A different liaison stood outside, slate in hand, expression neutral.

“Allison Crites,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Please confirm,” the liaison said.

Allison confirmed.

The liaison nodded, tapped the slate, and looked up. “You are cleared for provisional occupancy.”

Allison waited for the rest.

The liaison continued, “This is not a permanence statement.”

Allison nodded once. “I understand.”

“Confirm,” the liaison said.

Allison felt her jaw tighten, then loosened it deliberately. “I understand this is not a permanence statement.”

The liaison nodded, as if the phrasing mattered.

“You will receive scheduling when it is assigned,” the liaison added.

“When,” Allison echoed quietly before she could stop herself.

The liaison’s eyes flicked to her. Not unkind. Simply acknowledging the word.

“Yes,” she said. “When.”

Allison did not ask how long that might take.

She had learned that the Confluence did not apologize for time.

The liaison held out the slate. “Acknowledgment.”

Allison took it and read the brief lines, each one familiar in content and slightly different in language—like the same rule described from a different angle.

Access is tiered.  
Status is provisional.  
Review is ongoing.  
Relocation may occur without notice.  
Availability is expected.

Expected.

Not requested.

Allison signed.

She handed the slate back.

The liaison tapped it once, then stepped away. “Remain available,” she said again, and left.

The door closed.

Allison stood in the center of the room and let the word *expected* settle.

Expectation replacing invitation.

She could feel the shift even here, even this early. Not as a threat. As gravity.

Allison turned back to the desk and set her notes in a neat stack. She unpacked only what she needed to sleep: a change of clothes, a toothbrush, the smallest gestures of permanence she could allow without pretending she believed in it.

Then she sat again.

Not because she had been told to.

Because she needed one small act that was unquestionably hers.

And even as she did it, she felt herself reframing it, quietly, into compliance.

Remaining available, she thought.

By choice.

She recognized the thought.

And she did not stop.

# Assigned Use

Allison learned she had been scheduled when she discovered she had already missed the opportunity to consent.

The slate appeared sometime before she woke.

She knew this because the room felt different when she opened her eyes—not brighter, not louder, but subtly adjusted, as if its attention had shifted toward her. The Confluence rarely used alarms. It preferred presence: a quiet change in the environment that assumed awareness would follow.

She lay still for a moment, testing herself for urgency. There was none. Only the familiar, contained awareness that something had been decided nearby.

She sat up and reached for the slate resting on the desk.

**Observation Block — Tiered Access**  
**Escort: Tomas Calder**  
**Commencement: When Available**

When available.

Not *if*.

Allison read the line twice, then again more slowly. The phrasing was neutral, but its implication was firm: the block existed whether or not she acknowledged it. Her participation had already been incorporated into the day.

She set the slate down and swung her legs over the side of the bed.

There was no start time. She had already learned that this did not mean flexibility. It meant readiness would be determined externally, and notice would follow only after the fact.

She dressed carefully. Neutral clothing. Familiar layers. Nothing that would require adjustment if she were asked to move again. She paused with her notes in her hands, then placed them into her bag. After a moment, she removed them again and left them on the desk.

Observation block. Tiered access.

This was not the kind of day where private documentation traveled freely.

She opened the door.

The corridor beyond was quiet.

Not empty—never empty—but unoccupied in the way that suggested people were nearby and intentionally absent. Allison stepped out and closed the door behind her.

She waited.

The waiting here felt different from the previous day. Less provisional. More expectant. As if the system now knew her rhythms and had simply chosen not to interrupt them yet.

Five minutes passed.

Then ten.

Allison shifted her weight, then corrected herself when she realized the movement might read as impatience. She didn’t feel impatient. She felt suspended—held in place by something that did not need to touch her to be effective.

Footsteps approached.

“Allison.”

Tomas Calder stopped a respectful distance away, slate held loosely at his side. He looked exactly as he always did: composed, attentive, unhurried. His presence did not resolve uncertainty, but it shaped it.

“Good morning,” he said.

“Morning,” Allison replied.

“You saw the block,” Tomas said. Not a question.

“Yes.”

“Good.” He glanced briefly at the door behind her, then back to her face. “Before we proceed, I want to restate scope.”

Allison nodded.

“This is calibration,” Tomas said. “Not assessment. Not testing. Nothing new will be introduced.”

“And nothing will be used,” Allison said.

“Correct,” Tomas replied. “Recognition only. And recognition will be interrupted if it begins to drift.”

Drift. Not fail. Not breach.

The words settled with practiced precision.

They walked.

Not quickly. Not slowly. At a pace that discouraged urgency without inviting delay. The corridors they passed through felt more internal than the ones she had seen the day before—fewer people, fewer visual cues, more identical doors.

They stopped outside a room Allison did not recognize.

Tomas did not open it.

Instead, he consulted his slate, adjusted something, then looked at her. “Before we enter, I need to confirm a few things. Some of these will repeat information you’ve already provided.”

Allison nodded.

“This repetition is intentional,” Tomas added. “It reduces interpretive error.”

“If it becomes uncomfortable?” Allison asked.

“Say so,” Tomas replied. “We’ll pause.”

“Okay.”

“Confirm your name.”

“Allison Crites.”

“Confirm your current classification.”

“Integrated Resident, Provisional.”

“Confirm that your consent scope is unchanged.”

“It is unchanged.”

Tomas entered the information, then paused. “Confirm again.”

Allison repeated it.

Tomas nodded once. “Thank you.”

He opened the door.

The room beyond was spare, its surfaces deliberately uncluttered. A table. Two chairs. No visible instrumentation. Whatever work happened here was not meant to be watched.

Tomas gestured for her to sit.

He remained standing, watching her posture as she settled.

“This session will take time,” he said. “We will stop. We will restart. We may revisit the same threshold more than once.”

“All right,” Allison said.

“This is not inefficiency,” Tomas continued. “It is caution.”

He sat.

They began with silence.

Not meditative. Working.

Tomas observed her breathing, the angle of her shoulders, the way her hands rested in her lap. Allison was acutely aware of being watched—not as a subject, but as a system interacting with itself.

“Tell me what you feel,” Tomas said.

“Nothing,” Allison replied.

“Good,” he said. “Tell me again in a few minutes.”

Time passed.

“Allison,” Tomas said. “Describe the boundary between recognition and alignment.”

“Recognition is noticing,” she said. “Alignment is allowing that noticing to change how I interact.”

“And resonance?”

She hesitated. “Resonance is when alignment stops being deliberate.”

“Good.”

They paused again.

Several times Tomas interrupted her—not to correct, but to slow. “Say it again. Use different words. Start earlier. End sooner.”

Each repetition felt like sanding down an edge she hadn’t known was sharp.

At one point, Tomas raised a hand. “Pause.”

Allison stopped.

“Do you feel strain?” he asked.

“Pressure,” she said after considering. “Like holding a shape in place.”

“That’s within tolerance,” Tomas replied.

They continued for several more minutes before Tomas set his slate down.

“We’ll pause here,” he said. “Continuation without rest introduces error.”

He stood.

Outside the room, Evan was waiting.

He straightened when he saw Allison, relief flickering across his face before he smoothed it away.

“This portion is complete,” Tomas said. “Further access isn’t authorized.”

“I understand,” Evan said, though his jaw tightened.

“How long?” Evan asked.

Tomas consulted his slate. “Undetermined.”

Evan nodded once. “Right.”

They did not move immediately.

Instead, Tomas gestured to a bench set into the wall. “We’ll wait.”

They waited.

Allison sat. Tomas remained standing. Evan paced once, then stopped himself and leaned against the opposite wall.

Ten minutes passed.

Then twenty.

A Confluence staff member approached, glanced at Tomas’s slate, then at Evan. “You’re welcome to remain here,” she said politely. “Please do not follow.”

“I wasn’t planning to,” Evan replied.

She nodded and moved on.

Another staff member came later. Checked the slate. Repeated the instruction. Apologized without changing anything.

Eventually, Tomas’s slate chimed.

“Stand,” he said to Allison.

They moved again—this time into a small, unadorned space marked only by faint lines on the floor.

“This is not a session,” Tomas said. “Just awareness.”

They stood.

Allison felt the same pressure as before. No increase. No change.

“Describe,” Tomas said.

“Pressure,” she replied. “Consistent.”

“Confirm.”

“No change.”

“Good.”

They left almost immediately.

When Allison returned to her quarters hours later, another slate waited on the desk.

**Observation Block — Continued**  
**Status: Pending Release**

Pending.

Not delayed.  
Not canceled.  
Pending.

Allison sat on the edge of the bed and read the line until the words dulled.

She was useful now.

Not because she had acted.

Because she had remained available.

Waiting, she thought, could still be chosen.

She recognized the thought.

And let it stay.


---

The slate chimed again near evening.

Not sharply. Not urgently. Just enough to register once Allison had already begun to settle into the idea that nothing else would happen today.

She had been sitting at the desk, notes still untouched where she had left them, watching the slow shift of light along the wall that marked time more reliably than any schedule the Confluence had offered her.

She picked up the slate.

**Availability Window — Tiered Access**  
**Escort: Pending**  
**Commencement: When Available**

When available.

The phrase no longer startled her. That unsettled her more than it should have.

She did not move immediately.

There was no instruction telling her to report. No location listed. Just the window, opened without explanation, as if her presence could now be drawn on if needed.

Allison sat with the slate in her hands and noticed how easily her thoughts adjusted around it.

*I should be ready,* she thought.

She paused.

The thought had not come from anywhere specific. No one had said it. It had formed anyway, smooth and automatic, shaped by the day she had already lived.

She set the slate down and stood.

She did not rush. She did not delay. She positioned herself in the room so that she could respond quickly if asked, then waited.

Minutes passed.

Then more.

The slate remained unchanged.

When the chime sounded again, it was to close the window.

**Availability Window — Released**  
**No Further Action Required**

No further action.

Required.

The words sat oddly together.

Allison let out a slow breath she had not realized she was holding. The release did not feel like relief so much as a loosening—pressure reduced, not removed.

She sat again.

Outside her door, voices passed. Footsteps paused, then moved on. Somewhere down the corridor, a door opened and closed.

Time continued.

Later, another chime.

This one was softer still, almost apologetic in tone.

**Observation Block — Deferred**  
**Review Ongoing**

Deferred.

Not canceled.

Allison read the line twice.

She could feel the day arranging itself in retrospect: the calibration, the waiting, the partial engagement, the expectation that she would be ready again before being told to be.

Usefulness did not announce itself with action.

It accumulated through availability.

There was a knock at the door.

A real one this time—soft, deliberate.

Allison stood and opened it.

Evan stood outside, hands in his pockets, expression carefully neutral.

“They said I could check on you,” he said. “Briefly.”

“Okay,” Allison replied.

They stood in the doorway, neither of them stepping fully into the room.

“You all right?” Evan asked.

“Yes,” she said. It was true, as far as it went.

He nodded. “They kept telling me ‘not yet.’ Then ‘not necessary.’ Then ‘remain nearby.’”

Allison absorbed that. The parallel language did not surprise her.

“I waited,” Evan said, not accusing. Just stating fact.

“I know,” she said.

A pause stretched between them.

“They didn’t say when,” Evan added.

“No,” Allison agreed.

Another pause.

“I don’t like that,” Evan said finally.

“I know,” she said again.

She felt the urge to justify it—to explain that this was process, that this was caution, that this was necessary.

She stopped herself.

Explanations would turn his waiting into something reasonable. She wasn’t ready to do that yet.

Evan looked at her for a long moment, then nodded. “Okay,” he said. “I’ll still be here.”

“I know.”

He hesitated, then stepped back. “Get some rest.”

She watched him go, his footsteps fading into the corridor.

Allison closed the door and leaned her forehead briefly against it.

The day had asked nothing dramatic of her.

It had asked her to remain.

She crossed the room and sat on the bed, hands folded loosely in her lap.

Waiting, she thought, could still be chosen.

The thought came more slowly now. Less reflexive. More deliberate.

She recognized it.

And this time, she held it up to the light before letting it stay.

# Rumor Gravity

Allison did not hear the rumors directly.

That, she would later understand, was part of how they worked.

They did not arrive as statements or questions. No one asked her what she could do. No one speculated aloud in her presence. Instead, the Confluence adjusted around her in ways that were small enough to deny and consistent enough to measure.

She noticed it first in how often she was asked to wait.

Not the long waits of intake, nor the deliberate pauses of calibration, but the smaller ones threaded through the day. Being asked to remain in place while someone checked a slate. Being told a conversation would resume shortly—and then having it do so, just late enough to register. Being thanked for her patience in tones that suggested patience was now assumed rather than appreciated.

She was no longer provisional in the way she had been on arrival.

She was provisional in a way that required observation.

The first notice arrived midmorning, quiet and precise.

**Calibration Review — Continuation**  
**Escort: Tomas Calder**  
**Scope: Recognition Only**

Continuation.

Not follow-up.  
Not reassessment.

Allison read the notice twice before setting the slate down. She dressed with the same care she had begun to apply to everything here—neutral clothing, familiar layers, nothing that would require explanation if she were asked to move again. The repetition felt deliberate now. Not comfort, exactly, but continuity.

When she stepped into the corridor, Tomas was already there.

“You saw the notice,” he said.

“Yes.”

“This will be shorter than yesterday,” Tomas added. “But not lighter.”

Allison absorbed that. Tomas never framed difficulty dramatically. If he said something would not be lighter, it meant the weight would be subtle enough to miss if she stopped paying attention.

They walked.

The room they entered was not the same calibration space as before, though it was clearly related. Similar proportions. Different orientation. The table sat closer to the wall, the chairs angled slightly instead of facing each other directly.

“Nothing about this room is symbolic,” Tomas said, as if responding to her observation. “But arrangement affects perception.”

“All right,” Allison said.

She sat.

Tomas sat immediately.

“That’s the first difference,” he said. “Yesterday we established baseline. Today we’re checking for drift.”

Drift again.

“Confirm your internal state,” Tomas said.

Allison closed her eyes briefly—not to search, but to listen. “Pressure,” she said. “Consistent with yesterday. No new sensations.”

“Any compulsion to respond?”

“No.”

“Any discomfort associated with recognition?”

“Yes,” Allison said after a pause. “Contained.”

“Describe contained.”

“It feels like something I’m holding at a distance,” she said. “Not pushing away. Just… not engaging.”

Tomas nodded. “Holding at a distance is not suppression,” he said. “That distinction matters.”

They moved slowly through the same thresholds as before. Tomas stopped her more often this time. Asked her to restate answers she had already given. Asked her to describe what had not happened instead of what had.

At one point, he raised a hand.

“Pause,” he said.

Allison stopped.

“Your responses remain within tolerance,” Tomas said. “But the margin has narrowed.”

The statement landed with careful neutrality.

“Is that bad?” Allison asked.

“It’s information,” Tomas replied. “Not judgment.”

He marked something on his slate, then set it aside.

“Do not try to correct for this,” he added. “Overcorrection introduces more variance than drift.”

“All right,” Allison said.

They ended sooner than the previous session, but Allison felt more tired afterward—not drained, exactly, but aware of the sustained effort restraint required.

As they left the room, Tomas slowed.

“There’s an additional stop,” he said. “Not for you.”

“For my record?” Allison asked.

“For your record,” Tomas confirmed.

The archive occupied a quieter region of the Confluence.

Not empty—never empty—but hushed in a way that suggested voices were unnecessary rather than forbidden. The archivist did not look up when they approached, fingers moving steadily across her slate.

“Tomas,” she said.

“Mariel,” he replied. “We’re updating an active record.”

The archivist nodded. “Name?”

“Allison Crites.”

Her fingers paused.

Not long. Just long enough to register.

“Integrated Resident, Provisional,” the archivist said, reading. “Observation ongoing.”

“Yes.”

“And adaptive markers?” the archivist asked.

Tomas hesitated—not in uncertainty, but in care. “Present. Unaligned.”

The archivist made a notation.

“Adaptive lines remain theoretical,” she said. “No precedent for activation without alignment.”

“That remains our position,” Tomas replied.

“Records will reflect monitored expandability under constraint,” the archivist continued. “Flagged for longitudinal review.”

Flagged.

Allison felt the word settle into the space around her, altering it in ways she could not yet map.

The archivist glanced up briefly, eyes flicking to Allison—not curious, not alarmed. Simply noting.

They left.

“That wasn’t about me,” Allison said once they were back in the corridor.

“It was,” Tomas replied. “Just not to you.”

The second half of the day unfolded differently.

No new blocks appeared. No explicit scheduling followed. Instead, Allison found herself present in spaces where she had not been before—invited to sit quietly at the edge of discussions that did not require her input. Asked to remain nearby while documents were reviewed. Thanked for her flexibility.

She noticed how often her name appeared on slates not addressed to her.

How conversations adjusted when she entered a room—not stopping, but narrowing. How explanations grew more careful, even when they changed nothing.

The notice arrived without sound.

Allison noticed it only because the slate’s surface warmed beneath her fingers as she passed it on the desk.

**Record Review — Internal**  
**Attendance: Not Required**  
**Status: Ongoing**

Attendance not required did not mean irrelevance. It meant the discussion had been structured to proceed without her presence.

Later, in a corridor she recognized by the slight echo of its ceiling, she saw her name again—this time not as a heading, but as a reference point in a larger document displayed briefly on another person’s slate.

**Adaptive — Monitored**

The person noticed her looking and angled the slate away, expression apologetic without embarrassment.

“Sorry,” they said. “Habit.”

“It’s fine,” Allison replied.

It was.

She meant it. That unsettled her.

Late in the day, Tomas found her in a small waiting alcove with a low bench and a single light panel set into the wall.

“This isn’t a session,” he said.

“All right,” Allison replied.

He sat beside her, not facing her directly.

“They’re talking,” Tomas said.

“About me,” Allison said.

“Yes.”

“Nothing actionable,” Tomas continued. “No recommendations. Just framing.”

Framing.

“They’re afraid of destabilization,” Allison said.

“Yes,” Tomas replied. “And they’re afraid of being unfair.”

Those two fears sat uncomfortably close together.

“They asked if I was concerned,” Tomas added.

“And are you?” Allison asked.

Tomas considered. “I’m attentive,” he said. “Concern comes later.”

The light panel dimmed slightly as evening approached.

“You’re doing well,” Tomas said. “That’s not reassurance. It’s an observation.”

“All right,” Allison said.

When he left, she remained seated for several minutes.

Back in her quarters, Allison opened her notes.

She did not write immediately.

When she did, it was two lines.

*I am being observed as a system, not a person.*  
*Systems notice me before people do.*

She closed the notebook.

Compliance, she realized, was becoming easier.

That frightened her more than resistance would have.

She recognized the thought.

And, as she had learned to do here, she let it remain without acting on it.

# The Line That Holds

Allison was not surprised when the meeting room was not where she expected it to be.

The notice had been precise about time and imprecise about location, which she was beginning to recognize as intentional. She arrived ten minutes early and waited in the indicated corridor, standing where the light panel shifted subtly when someone passed nearby.

No one hurried.

When the door finally opened, it did so without announcement. A woman Allison did not recognize glanced out, checked a slate, and nodded once.

“This way.”

The room beyond was larger than the calibration spaces, but less finished. Not austere—simply incomplete. Several sections of the wall were unadorned, their surfaces marked with faint lines that suggested future panels or overlays. The table at the center was broad and functional, its surface clear except for three slates placed with deliberate symmetry.

Elder Ren Caldris stood near the far end of the room.

He did not move when Allison entered. He did not smile. He acknowledged her presence with a slight inclination of his head—the kind that marked recognition without familiarity.

“Allison Crites,” he said. Not as a greeting. As a confirmation.

“Yes,” she replied.

Tomas stood to one side of the room, hands folded loosely behind his back. Evan was not present.

That, too, felt deliberate.

“Please sit,” Elder Ren said.

Allison took the chair nearest the door. It was not offered, but it was not contested either.

“This is not a disciplinary meeting,” Elder Ren said once she was seated. “And it is not an assessment.”

Allison nodded.

“It is,” he continued, “an explanation.”

She waited.

“The Confluence exists to maintain continuity,” Elder Ren said. “Not stability. Stability is brittle. Continuity allows for strain.”

Allison listened carefully.

“Our systems are designed to absorb variance,” he continued. “But only when that variance behaves within known parameters.”

He paused, as if checking that the explanation had reached its end—not that it had been understood.

“Expandability is not new,” Elder Ren said.

The word settled heavily, but without emphasis.

“What is new,” he continued, “is expandability without alignment.”

Allison did not respond. She waited for the thought to complete itself.

“We do not restrict expansion because it is dangerous,” Elder Ren said. “We restrict it because it is consequential.”

He let the distinction stand on its own.

“Danger can be mitigated,” he said. “Consequence cannot be undone.”

Allison shifted slightly in her chair, then stilled herself.

“You are not being limited because of what you might do,” Elder Ren continued. “You are being limited because of what your existence requires the system to account for.”

That felt closer to the center of it.

“Our infrastructure is built on precedent,” he said. “On accumulated response to known patterns. When a pattern emerges without historical analogue, the system slows.”

He stopped speaking.

Nothing about the pause suggested an opening for response.

“We don’t do this to withhold,” Elder Ren said after a moment. “We do it to prevent cascading response.”

Allison chose her words carefully.

“Is this about trust?” she asked.

Elder Ren looked at her directly for the first time.

“No,” he said. “Trust is interpersonal. This is structural.”

She nodded.

“Then… what is expected of me?” she asked.

Elder Ren folded his hands on the table.

“Availability,” he said. “Transparency. Restraint.”

The list was delivered without emphasis.

“You will continue to be consulted,” he said. “You will continue to be observed. You will not be asked to act.”

Not yet was implied, but not spoken.

“The system requires time,” Elder Ren continued. “And so do we.”

The meeting did not end with dismissal. It simply concluded when there was nothing more to explain.

Allison stood when Elder Ren did. Tomas inclined his head toward her, a subtle signal to follow.

The corridor outside felt narrower than the room they had left.

They walked in silence for several turns before Tomas slowed.

“That was infrastructure,” he said quietly.

“I know,” Allison replied.

“They weren’t wrong,” Tomas added. “But they weren’t complete either.”

She looked at him.

“That’s not a contradiction,” he said. “It’s a boundary.”

He stopped walking.

“You should know,” Tomas said carefully, “that expandability changes how systems respond even when it isn’t used.”

“I know,” Allison said.

Tomas studied her for a moment, then nodded. “Good.”

He did not say more.

Allison slowed.

“Tomas,” she said.

He stopped immediately.

“When Elder Ren talks about expansion,” she said, “what does he mean?”

Tomas did not answer right away. Not because he didn’t know—because he was choosing the boundary of the answer.

“In this context,” he said finally, “it means your capacity to recognize systems that are not native to you.”

“Recognize,” Allison repeated.

“Yes,” Tomas said. “Not access. Not use. Not alignment.”

She waited.

“Recognition creates obligation,” Tomas continued. “Once a system is legible to you, your behavior within its influence matters—whether you engage or not.”

“That sounds like pressure,” Allison said.

“It is,” Tomas agreed. “Not power.”

She absorbed that.

“And that’s all?” she asked.

“That’s all you need for now,” Tomas said. “Anything more would imply permission.”

Allison nodded.

That distinction mattered.

They resumed walking.


Later that evening, Evan asked if she wanted to eat.

Not formally. Not as an invitation that required acceptance. Just a quiet question when they crossed paths near the residential wing, his hands already full with a paper bag from one of the kitchens.

“I grabbed extra,” he said. “In case.”

Allison hesitated for half a second—long enough to notice that she had begun to calculate whether eating together would count as something. Then she nodded.

“Okay.”

They ate in a small communal space off one of the residential corridors. It wasn’t private, but it was rarely busy this late. A long table. Soft lighting. The faint hum of systems behind the walls.

Evan unpacked containers methodically, setting them between them rather than claiming either side. Nothing fancy. Warm food. Familiar enough to register as comfort without being indulgent.

“How was today?” he asked, once they’d both started eating.

Allison considered the question carefully.

“Long,” she said finally.

Evan nodded, accepting that as a complete answer.

After a few bites, she asked, “How’s everyone doing?”

“They’re fine,” he said. “Your mom texted twice. Once to ask if you’re sleeping. Once to ask if you’re eating.”

Allison huffed quietly. “Figures.”

“She’s trying very hard not to panic,” Evan added. “Which I think is making it worse.”

“That tracks,” Allison said.

“Kara’s busy,” he continued. “Emily’s mad you didn’t call last night but won’t admit it. She told me to tell you she’s not mad.”

Allison smiled despite herself.

“And Mara?” she asked.

“Watching everyone,” Evan said. “Making lists. Pretending she isn’t.”

They ate in silence for a few minutes. The kind that didn’t need to be filled.

Evan took another bite, then set his fork down.

“The people here,” he said—not needing to specify who he meant. “The Confluence.”

“They’re being careful,” Evan continued. Not accusing. Observational.

“Yes,” Allison said.

“And that means they’re also… pulling back,” he said.

“Yes.”

Evan exhaled slowly. “I don’t like that.”

“I know,” Allison said.

“Tomas talked to me,” Evan said. “Not officially. Just context.”

Allison waited.

“He said it’s not about stopping you,” Evan continued. “It’s about what happens if they don’t.”

“That sounds like him,” Allison said.

Evan looked at her. “Are you okay with this?”

Allison set her fork down.

“I’m choosing it,” she said.

“That’s not the same thing,” Evan said.

“No,” she agreed. “But it’s close enough to matter.”

They finished eating without rushing. Evan cleaned up, stacking containers, wiping the table even though it didn’t need it.

At the doorway, he hesitated.

“I’m here,” he said. “You know that, right?”

“I know,” Allison said.

When Allison returned to her quarters, the slate on her desk was unchanged. No new blocks. No new notices.

That absence felt deliberate.

She sat on the edge of the bed and rested her hands on her knees.

The door was visible now.

She knew where it was.

She also knew what opening it would cost.

Allison lay back and stared at the ceiling.

For now, she would remain.

And she knew exactly what that choice meant.
