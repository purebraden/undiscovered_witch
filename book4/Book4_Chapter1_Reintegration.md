# Chapter 1 — Reintegration

Allison had learned the quiet rhythms of the Confluence well enough that they no longer startled her.

The lights in her quarters shifted gradually, never switching on all at once. The temperature adjusted before she noticed she was cold. The faint hum that threaded through the walls—machinery, wards, systems she did not fully understand—remained constant enough to fade into background existence.

This room was hers.

Not borrowed. Not provisional in the way her early visits had been. Assigned, documented, maintained.

That distinction sat with her every morning now, heavier than the stone floors beneath her feet.

She sat on the edge of the narrow bed, hands resting loosely in her lap, and waited for the sense of orientation to settle. Not disorientation—she didn’t wake confused here—but the quieter recalibration of remembering where she was *in relation to everything else*.

Not home.  
Not a guest space.  
Not temporary housing.

Residence.

The word had appeared in her documentation three days ago.

Not announced. Not highlighted. Just… there.

Allison stood and crossed to the small desk set into the wall. Its surface was empty except for a folded page placed with deliberate neatness in the center.

She hadn’t put it there.

That, too, was becoming familiar.

The paper was not marked urgent. No seals. No sigils. No magic she could feel pressing against her skin. Just a single page, folded once, waiting to be acknowledged.

She didn’t open it yet.

Instead, she went through the motions that anchored her mornings: washing her face, tying her hair back, adjusting the sleeves of the sweater she’d chosen because it didn’t carry memory with it. The Confluence did not require uniforms, but it noticed patterns. Allison had learned that neutrality was a kind of armor.

When she returned to the desk, she unfolded the paper.

**Reintegration Review — Session One**  
**Attendance Requested, Not Required**

The language was precise enough to be calming and unsettling at the same time.

Requested, not required.  
But recorded either way.

A location followed—Registrar’s Office, West Corridor—and a time that did not rush her. There was no consequence listed for delay. No penalty for refusal.

Only an expectation that she would understand what the request *meant*.

Allison exhaled slowly through her nose.

She was no longer being handled as an anomaly.

She was being processed as infrastructure-adjacent.

She folded the paper again, more carefully this time, and slid it into the inner pocket of her bag. Not because she needed it, but because it mattered where it was kept.

Ownership still counted.

Outside her door, footsteps passed. Voices murmured. The Confluence waking into itself—not as a place that slept, but as one that shifted modes.

Allison stepped into the corridor and closed the door behind her. The soft click echoed faintly, then vanished into the layered quiet.

She walked without hurry. There was no need to rush when the system already accounted for your arrival.

At the first junction, Evan waited.

Not standing directly in her path. Not leaning in a way that suggested he’d been pacing. Just present, as if he belonged there too—which, increasingly, he did.

“Morning,” he said.

“Morning,” Allison replied.

He glanced at her bag, then at her face. “Registrar?”

“Yes.”

He nodded once. “They’ve been circulating revised terminology.”

Allison’s mouth tightened slightly. “That sounds like a Toren sentence.”

Evan huffed quietly. “It is. He’s not wrong.”

“That doesn’t make it better.”

“No,” Evan agreed. “It just makes it inevitable.”

They walked together, their pace naturally aligning. No touching. No dramatic closeness. Just the steady reassurance of shared direction.

“Do I need to be worried?” Allison asked.

Evan thought before answering, which she appreciated. “Worried isn’t the right word,” he said. “Attentive is.”

She nodded. That matched what her body already knew.

They reached the Registrar’s Office without ceremony. The door stood open, as it often did, a visible signal of transparency that didn’t quite counterbalance the power of what happened inside.

Evan stopped just short of the threshold. “I’ll be here.”

“I know.”

She stepped inside alone.

The room had not changed since her last visit, which somehow made it worse. Two chairs. One table. Neutral walls that refused to offer emotional cues.

Iria Vell stood as Allison entered.

“Allison,” she said, calm as ever. “Thank you for coming.”

Allison inclined her head slightly. “You said attendance was requested.”

“Yes,” Iria replied. “And your presence is noted without inference.”

The words slid into place with practiced ease.

Allison sat.

Iria joined her, positioning herself at a slight angle—not confrontational, not deferential. A mediator’s posture.

“You are now residing within Confluence space,” Iria said. Not as news. As context. “That shifts several internal classifications.”

Allison waited.

“Your prior status was guest-adjacent,” Iria continued. “Your current status is *integrated resident, provisional*.”

There it was.

Provisional.

Allison felt the word settle against her ribs—not painful, but present.

“What does provisional mean here?” she asked.

“It means reversibility,” Iria said. “And scrutiny.”

Allison nodded once. “Those usually arrive together.”

“Yes,” Iria agreed. “Which is why we begin with framing rather than function.”

Iria placed a blank form on the table between them. Headings only. No prefilled assumptions.

**Role**  
**Scope**  
**Consent Boundaries**  
**Review Interval**

“This is not an assignment,” Iria said. “It is a record. One you help define.”

Allison studied the page.

Being made legible.

She recognized the shape of it now—not a trap, not a threat, but a narrowing of possibility into named channels.

Infrastructure did not demand loyalty.

It demanded reliability.

“I want something noted before we begin,” Allison said.

Iria’s pen hovered. “Go ahead.”

“I am useful,” Allison said carefully. “But I am not neutral. And I will not be treated as a stabilizing object.”

Iria looked at her for a long moment—assessing, not judging.

“Understood,” she said finally. “That distinction matters.”

The pen touched paper.

Outside the room, the Confluence continued to breathe—procedural, calm, quietly afraid of destabilization.

And Allison sat at the table, not becoming more powerful.

Just becoming visible.
