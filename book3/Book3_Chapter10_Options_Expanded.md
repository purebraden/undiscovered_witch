# Chapter 10 — Options
*(Allison POV)*

Allison was asked to appear before the elders.

Not summoned.

Asked.

The distinction mattered here.

The request came through a channel she didn’t yet recognize as formal, but the tone was unmistakable—precise, neutral, optional in wording and inevitable in practice. She was given a time, a location, and no additional context.

No warning.  
No reassurance.  
No implied outcome.

She arrived early.

The chamber wasn’t what she’d expected. There was no dais, no elevated seating, no sense of hierarchy imposed through architecture. Instead, the room was circular and open, with a ceiling high enough to let light fall from above without casting hard shadows. The walls curved gently, breaking up the sense of edges. The air felt still, but not heavy—like a place designed for conversations that needed space to unfold.

No one sat higher than anyone else.

That, more than anything, told her what kind of authority this was.

The elders arrived one by one, not as a group. Each acknowledged Allison as they entered—small nods, brief smiles, the kind that didn’t demand response but made room for it. They took their seats without claiming them, settling rather than occupying.

One of them gestured lightly toward the seating. “You can sit wherever you’re comfortable.”

Allison chose a chair that faced the center without being central. She noticed that no seat placed her at a disadvantage. She noticed that she wasn’t being positioned.

When the last elder took their seat, the room settled—not because anyone signaled it, but because everyone present recognized when it was time.

Mara was there as well, seated to the side. Not among the elders. Not absent. Context, not authority.

“Thank you for coming,” one of the elders said. Her voice was warm without being familiar. “I’m Elira. I work with long-term continuity—tracking how decisions echo forward, where small choices accumulate into lasting effects.”

Allison nodded. That sounded like someone who thought in years instead of moments.

The man beside her inclined his head. “Jonas. Structural mediation. When systems start pulling against themselves, I look for the points where alignment can be restored without force.”

That felt… practical. Repair rather than control.

The third elder smiled faintly. “Maeve. Cross-domain ethics. I focus on where obligations overlap—especially when different worlds bring incompatible assumptions into the same space.”

Allison felt that one land. Not rules. Assumptions.

The fourth spoke last. “Ruthan. Pattern inheritance. I study recurring behaviors—what passes through families, institutions, and roles whether people intend it to or not.”

That one stayed with her. Pattern inheritance wasn’t abstract. It was observational.

“I’m Allison Crites,” she said.

“Yes,” Elira replied, smiling again. “We know. But it matters that you say it.”

That settled something Allison hadn’t realized was unsettled. She hadn’t been placed here without her participation. She’d been included.

“You’ve interacted with the Confluence recently in ways that merit disclosure,” Jonas said. “This isn’t a response to risk. It’s a response to participation.”

“We want to be clear about that,” Maeve added. “You’re not here because something went wrong.”

Allison let that sit, then nodded. “Okay.”

Ruthan folded his hands loosely. “Visibility changes what structures are available.”

Available.

The word carried weight here. Not obligation. Not expectation.

Possibility.

“There are two,” Elira said. “They are independent. You may accept one, both, or neither.”

No pressure followed. No silence sharpened into demand.

“The first is a liaison,” Jonas continued. “A liaison exists to reduce friction. Translation, alignment, clarification. It prevents unfamiliarity from becoming structural conflict.”

“So… interface,” Allison said.

“Yes,” Jonas replied. “Administrative, not directive.”

“And optional,” Maeve added. “Always.”

Allison nodded slowly, then asked, “And the second?”

“The second is a tutor,” Ruthan said.

There was a pause.

Not hesitation.

Weight.

“A tutor provides pattern guidance,” he continued. “Not instruction in obedience. Instruction in recognition.”

“Recognition of what?” Allison asked.

“Influence,” Ruthan replied. “Inheritance. Recurrence.”

Maeve spoke next. “Tutors of this type are historically associated with the royal line.”

That landed exactly as heavily as it was meant to.

Not dramatic.

Contextual.

“Royal-adjacent,” Allison said.

“Yes,” Elira confirmed. “Not royal authority. But informed by its patterns.”

Allison felt the shape of that settle—not a crown, not a title. A lineage of behavior, of choices repeated often enough to be studied.

“This option is offered because of how you act,” Jonas said. “Not what you are.”

No one watched her for a reaction.

That, more than anything, kept the room from tilting into coercion.

“If I decline both,” Allison asked, “what changes?”

“Nothing,” Elira said immediately.

“If I take one later?”

“That remains possible,” Maeve replied.

“If I take one now and withdraw later?”

“That is also possible.”

The room didn’t narrow around her questions. It widened.

Allison considered for a moment, then asked, “Do you have anyone in mind?”

The elders didn’t answer immediately.

Not because the question surprised them—but because it required precision.

“For liaison,” Jonas said at last, “there are several appropriate candidates. The role is structural. Compatibility matters more than history.”

“And for a tutor?” Allison asked.

That pause carried different weight.

“There are fewer,” Ruthan said. “By design.”

“Because of the royal association,” Allison said.

“Yes,” he replied. “And because pattern guidance requires restraint as much as insight.”

“No assignment would occur without consent,” Maeve added. “From both sides.”

Allison nodded. That mattered.

“So nothing is decided,” she said.

“Nothing is decided,” Elira confirmed.

“Good,” Allison said.

She didn’t elaborate. She didn’t need to.

“These options exist to reduce friction,” Jonas said. “Not to contain you.”

Allison glanced once at Mara, who did not intervene, did not signal, did not lead.

Good.

“I’ll need time,” Allison said.

“As expected,” Elira replied.

“No action is required today,” Maeve added. “Consent is explicit or it is invalid.”

That sentence felt deliberate.

Anchoring.

The elders rose without ceremony. No dismissal was spoken, but the meeting was clearly complete.

Mara stood as well, falling into step beside Allison as they left the chamber.

The options hadn’t closed around her.

They had simply been placed within reach.
