# Chapter 8 — First Deliberate Use
*(Allison POV)*

Tomas told her what the meeting needed to *not* do.

He did not tell her what to do.

They stood just outside the room, close enough that Allison could hear the low murmur inside—voices held to a professional register, not raised, but compressed. The sound of people already speaking past one another without realizing it yet.

“This is a trade session,” Tomas said quietly. “Not arbitration. If it closes today, it closes wrong.”

Allison nodded once. She had learned quickly that nodding here wasn’t agreement. It was acknowledgment.

“What goes wrong?” she asked.

“The assumption,” Tomas said. “Both sides are treating the shortage as intent instead of constraint. That locks positions.”

“And if they lock?” Allison asked.

He glanced toward the door, then back at her. “Supply collapses in six weeks. Not because there isn’t honey. Because no one can move it without admitting fault.”

“That’s… bad,” Allison said.

“Yes,” Tomas agreed. “But not dramatic enough to stop anyone from pushing.”

She absorbed that. Structural failure rarely looked urgent from the inside.

“This meeting needs to end stable,” Tomas continued. “Not resolved. Stable.”

The word clicked into place with satisfying precision. *Cleared isn’t final. Stability buys time.* She didn’t repeat it aloud. She didn’t need to.

“Do you want me to—” she started.

Tomas shook his head once, firm but unpressured. “I’m telling you what the system needs. Not asking you to supply it.”

That distinction mattered more than anything else he could have said.

He opened the door and gestured her in ahead of him.

The room was smaller than she expected. A long table ran the length of it, polished but visibly worn—edges softened by years of hands resting there, decisions leaning forward. Six people were seated, two standing behind them, not guards so much as anchors.

The Confluence seal was etched into the far wall. Not centered. Not elevated. Just present, like a watermark.

Three of the delegates had wings.

Allison registered it without staring. The wings were folded tight, held close to the back, restrained with the same care you’d give equipment that could become disruptive if deployed casually. The faerie representatives sat with controlled posture, shoulders subtly set to account for the space behind them.

Nothing ornamental. Nothing threatening.

Just… real.

The faerie speaker—tall, dark-haired, features precise without being sharp—paused when Tomas and Allison entered. Her gaze flicked to Allison, then away again, cataloging rather than questioning.

“We were discussing yield variability,” the faerie said. Her voice was calm, clipped, perfectly modulated. “The explanation offered does not account for the magnitude of loss.”

An Earth-side delegate responded immediately, fingers tightening around a stylus. “Weather patterns have been unpredictable. Transport delays compound—”

“That does not explain intent,” the faerie said. “Your suppliers have fulfilled contracts before under similar conditions.”

Allison took a seat near the wall, slightly back from the table. No one acknowledged her presence. No one dismissed it either. She was exactly where Tomas had said she would be.

An observer.

The Earth delegate leaned forward. “We’re not hoarding. There’s no incentive.”

“Incentive is irrelevant,” the faerie replied. “Reliability is the concern.”

A second faerie spoke, quieter but no less firm. “Ambrosia production cannot tolerate this level of volatility. If your systems cannot guarantee continuity, we must revise terms.”

*Revise* landed like a lever being set.

“You’re talking about penalties for circumstances outside our control,” the Earth delegate said, heat creeping in despite the formal tone.

“You are describing them as outside your control,” the faerie answered. “We are not convinced.”

The slope steepened.

Allison felt it—not emotionally, but structurally. Each response narrowed the next possible answer. Each assumption hardened into policy. They weren’t arguing facts anymore; they were negotiating blame.

She remembered Rowan’s voice: *Patterns behave like weather.*

This one was about to break.

Allison did not feel power gather. There was no pressure, no warning sensation, no sense of something waiting to be released. There was only clarity.

*If this closes now, it closes wrong.*

She waited through two more exchanges, letting the shape of the argument complete itself. She watched how both sides leaned on the same assumption—that someone had chosen this outcome.

Then she chose.

She leaned forward slightly and spoke—not loudly, not formally, but into the narrow space between statements.

“What if the issue isn’t reliability,” she said, “but visibility?”

The room stilled.

Every eye turned to her, including Tomas’s. He didn’t react. He simply watched.

Allison kept her voice even. “You’re treating the fluctuation as intent because you can’t see where the delay actually occurs. And you,” she nodded toward the Earth delegate, “are treating it as uncontrollable because you don’t see which parts are compressible.”

Silence followed—not hostile, not approving. Recalibrating.

The faerie speaker tilted her head a fraction. “You are suggesting partial transparency.”

“I’m suggesting a temporary constraint,” Allison said. Her heart beat faster now, but her voice held. “A short-term disclosure window. Not full access. Just enough to identify where variability actually enters the chain.”

“That information is proprietary,” the Earth delegate said immediately.

“Temporarily,” Allison replied. “And narrowly. You don’t need to share methods. Just timing.”

The second faerie delegate folded her hands. “You would expect us to accept delay without penalty in exchange for this?”

“I’d expect you to accept delay because it’s demonstrably not malice,” Allison said. “And because locking terms now guarantees worse instability later.”

She felt it then—not power, exactly, but alignment. Like the ground beneath the conversation had shifted because she had stepped onto a different load-bearing point.

The faerie speaker considered her for a long moment. Then she said, “This would require suspension, not revision.”

The Earth delegate swallowed. “We could agree to a limited window. Thirty days.”

“Sixty,” the faerie countered.

“Forty-five,” the Earth delegate said.

They were negotiating.

Not because Allison had commanded anything.

Because she had changed what it was reasonable to say next.

Tomas remained silent. He didn’t look at Allison. He watched the table the way someone watched a gauge settle back into range.

The meeting ended without ceremony. No signatures. No satisfaction. Just a shared understanding that terms would not lock today.

As the delegates filed out, the faerie speaker paused beside Allison.

“You are not part of this negotiation,” she said.

“No,” Allison agreed.

“But you altered its course,” the faerie said. Not accusation. Not praise. Statement of fact.

“Yes,” Allison said.

The faerie inclined her head once, then moved on, wings still folded, restraint intact.

Outside the room, Tomas finally looked at her.

“That bought time,” he said.

“Yes,” Allison replied.

He waited, as if checking for hesitation, for justification, for deflection. When it didn’t come, he nodded once.

“Responsibility travels with outcomes,” he said. “You understand that now.”

Allison did.

She hadn’t fixed the supply chain. She hadn’t solved the dispute. She had done something smaller—and heavier.

She had chosen to act.

And the system had responded.

She stepped back into the corridor, the Confluence moving around her as it always had—quiet, precise, indifferent.

Except now, one of its paths bore her footprint.

On purpose.
