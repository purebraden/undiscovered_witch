# Chapter 13 — Calibration
*(Allison POV)*

The room they brought her to wasn’t ceremonial.

It wasn’t the elders’ chamber, and it wasn’t one of the open commons either. It felt administrative without being cold—clean lines, warm light, a table sized for conversation rather than judgment. A place where decisions were conveyed, not debated.

A woman Allison didn’t recognize stood as Allison entered. She wore no insignia, no visual markers of status.

“Allison,” she said. “I’m Mira. I serve as registrar for this tier of Confluence coordination.”

Allison nodded. “Okay.”

Another presence sat to the side—an older man Allison had seen once before, briefly, in passing. He didn’t introduce himself. He didn’t need to. He was there as witness, not authority.

“This won’t take long,” Mira said. “I’m here to notify you of two procedural developments.”

Allison waited.

“First,” Mira continued, “a liaison request has been submitted.”

Allison’s eyebrows lifted despite herself. “Already?”

“Yes,” Mira said evenly. “The request was filed following your consent to structural roles. The applicant is Evan.”

The name landed more solidly than Allison expected. Not because she hadn’t anticipated it—some part of her had—but because she hadn’t framed it as something that would happen this quickly.

She didn’t speak right away.

Mira watched her without pressure.

“I understand,” Allison said finally.

“You are not required to respond at this time,” Mira said. “You may decline without consequence. If you choose to accept, the assignment will be provisional and reversible.”

Allison nodded.

“And the second development?” she asked.

“A tutor has been identified,” Mira said. “They’re available now, if you wish to proceed.”

Allison blinked. “Now?”

“Yes.”

She considered whether she should have more questions. Credentials. Scope. Expectations.

But she didn’t feel resistance.

“Okay,” she said. “Yes.”

Mira smiled faintly. “There is one clarification I’m required to offer.”

“All right.”

“The tutor role is advisory and diagnostic,” Mira said. “Not directive. You may pause or discontinue at any point.”

“I understand,” Allison said.

The older man rose then, nodding once toward Allison before moving to the door. Mira stepped aside.

“This way,” she said.

They didn’t walk far.

The next space was even simpler. Two chairs. A low table. A window that looked out onto nothing Allison could identify, but which still felt open.

A person stood near the window, hands folded loosely behind their back.

They turned as Allison entered.

“Hello,” they said. “I’m Tomas.”

Allison paused. “You’re the tutor.”

“Yes,” Tomas said.

Mira didn’t stay. When the door closed behind her, the room didn’t change. It simply became quieter.

Tomas gestured to one of the chairs. “Please.”

Allison sat. Tomas took the opposite seat, posture relaxed but focused.

Tomas studied her for a moment longer than was comfortable.

“Let me ask it differently,” he said. “When you’re in a situation where multiple outcomes are possible, how do you decide how much influence to apply?”

Allison considered the question carefully.

“I try not to decide it for people,” she said. “I narrow things. Remove options that would cause harm. Shape the space so what’s left is survivable.”

Tomas nodded once. “And when you do that, where does the cost land?”

She opened her mouth, then stopped.

“…On me,” she said. “Internally.”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “You absorb the friction so others don’t feel pushed.”

“That’s just being careful,” Allison said.

“No,” Tomas replied. “It’s being responsible. But responsibility and cost aren’t the same thing.”

He leaned forward slightly, elbows resting loosely on his knees.

“You’ve learned to constrain yourself before you ever constrain a situation,” Tomas continued. “That keeps you from dominating outcomes. It also means you carry more internal load than is strictly necessary.”

Allison frowned. “So I should push harder.”

“No,” Tomas said immediately. “You should notice when you’re paying a cost that isn’t yours.”

She sat with that.

“This pattern,” Tomas went on, “shows up in people who grow into influence without scaffolding.”

Allison’s shoulders tightened. “I’m not—”

“I’m not naming lineage,” Tomas said calmly. “Or authority. I’m naming behavior.”

He met her gaze. “Voluntary constraint. Outcome awareness. Ethical pre-loading.”

Allison exhaled slowly.

“So what does calibration actually look like?” she asked. “Day to day.”

Tomas’s mouth curved into a faint smile. “It looks like accompaniment.”

That made her blink. “Accompaniment.”

“For now,” Tomas said. “When you’re asked to attend a meeting, observe a negotiation, or offer adjustment where influence is expected, I’ll be present.”

“Watching?” Allison asked.

“Not exactly,” Tomas said. “I’ll intervene if distortion appears. I’ll name it. Sometimes to you. Sometimes to the room.”

Her eyebrows lifted. “You’d interrupt a meeting.”

“If necessary,” Tomas said. “That’s part of keeping the load from defaulting to you.”

“Allison,” Tomas said then, not sharply but with intent, “this isn’t theoretical.”

She looked at him.

“You’re already being noticed,” he continued. “Not for power. For effect.”

Her stomach tightened. “Noticed how?”

“Requests are starting to route toward you,” Tomas said. “Informally. Quietly. People ask whether you’ll be present. Whether your input is expected.”

Allison sat very still. “I didn’t agree to that.”

“I know,” Tomas said. “And that’s why calibration is happening now, not later.”

He leaned back slightly. “When someone consistently reduces conflict without escalating authority, systems adapt around them. They start leaning.”

“That sounds dangerous,” Allison said.

“It can be,” Tomas replied. “Not because of intent. Because of accumulation.”

She felt the shape of that settle, uncomfortably familiar.

“You’ve been compensating for that instinctively,” Tomas continued. “Slowing yourself down. Absorbing friction. Making space so others don’t feel displaced.”

“And now?” Allison asked.

“And now,” Tomas said, “the Confluence is choosing not to let that happen unchecked.”

She frowned. “So accompaniment is about protecting me?”

“It’s about protecting the work,” Tomas said. “And making sure the cost doesn’t default to you simply because you’re capable of carrying it.”

Allison exhaled slowly.

“So this isn’t because I need help,” she said.

“No,” Tomas said. “It’s because you don’t.”

She considered that.

“And if I don’t want accompaniment,” she said.

“Then we don’t do it,” Tomas replied. “Calibration only works with consent.”

She nodded slowly.

“And this isn’t permanent,” Tomas added. “It’s transitional. Once you’re no longer absorbing cost automatically, I step back.”

“So you’re not here to make decisions for me,” Allison said.

“No,” Tomas said. “I’m here to make sure you don’t disappear while making them.”

That settled more deeply than she expected.

Allison leaned back in her chair. This wasn’t instruction. It wasn’t surveillance.

It was support with an exit clause.

“All right,” she said. “We can try that.”

Tomas inclined his head. “Good.”

The session ended without ceremony.

As Allison stepped back into the corridor, she didn’t feel elevated or diminished.

She felt the weight of visibility—and the relief of not carrying it alone.

The Confluence hadn’t given her a role.

It had adjusted how much weight she was expected to carry at once—and who would notice if it became too much.
