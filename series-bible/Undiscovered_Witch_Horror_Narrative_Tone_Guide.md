# HORROR NARRATIVE TONE GUIDE
## Writing Like King & Koontz — What ChatGPT Gets Wrong

### Purpose

This document identifies specific techniques that create the King/Koontz style of horror: real characters, lived-in worlds, and dread that comes from psychological truth rather than spectacle. Use this for prompting, editing, and revision.

---

## THE CORE PROBLEM WITH "POLITE" HORROR

ChatGPT defaults to:
- Elevated, literary prose that creates emotional distance
- Clean epiphanies that resolve tension
- Characters who think in thesis statements
- Abstract emotional language ("she felt a wave of dread")
- Conflict that clarifies rather than complicates
- Prose that sounds *written* rather than *lived*

King and Koontz do the opposite. Their horror works because it feels like it's happening to someone you know, in a place you've been, with thoughts you've had.

---

## TECHNIQUE 1: VERNACULAR INTERIORITY

**The Problem:** ChatGPT makes characters think like essayists.

**Example of Polite Writing:**
> Allison recognized the familiar tightness in her chest. The pattern was becoming clear—her presence shifted emotional dynamics in ways she could not fully control.

**King/Koontz Approach:**
> The tightness was back. That stupid, useless tightness that made her want to scream or throw up or both. She'd smoothed another person without meaning to and now she was standing in the hallway like an asshole, pretending she hadn't noticed.

**What Changed:**
- Self-judgment in real language ("like an asshole")
- Physical sensation over emotional abstraction
- Incomplete thoughts, interrupted reasoning
- The character is *frustrated*, not *observing*

**Prompting Language:**
> "Write this scene in close third person. The character should think the way a real seventeen-year-old thinks—with profanity, self-interruption, petty judgments, and incomplete realizations. No thesis statements. No clean epiphanies."

---

## TECHNIQUE 2: MUNDANE ANCHORING

**The Problem:** ChatGPT creates generic settings. Everything feels like *a* place, not *this* place.

**King grounds horror in specific, dated, consumer reality:**
- Brand names (Chevy, Coke, Marlboro)
- Pop culture (specific songs, TV shows, movies)
- Regional details (the way people talk, local businesses)
- Ugly ordinary spaces (convenience store lighting, the smell of school cafeterias)

**Example of Polite Writing:**
> The convenience store was quiet. Fluorescent lights hummed overhead. Allison grabbed a drink and headed to the register.

**King/Koontz Approach:**
> The Quik-E-Mart smelled like hot dogs that had been rotating since the Clinton administration. The fluorescent tube above the Slurpee machine flickered arrhythmically, and some country song about trucks and heartbreak oozed from a speaker she couldn't locate. Allison grabbed a Diet Coke—the bottle was sticky—and got in line behind a woman buying scratch tickets with the desperate focus of someone who'd done this before and would do it again tomorrow.

**What Changed:**
- Named the store
- Specific sensory details (smell, sound, texture)
- Humor that isn't cute
- A minor character rendered as a real person with a life

**Prompting Language:**
> "Include specific brand names, sensory details that aren't pleasant, and at least one minor character who has their own small story happening. Make the setting feel lived-in and slightly run-down."

---

## TECHNIQUE 3: UGLY THOUGHTS

**The Problem:** ChatGPT makes protagonists morally coherent. They have understandable fears and justified reactions.

**King's characters have ugly thoughts:**
- Petty resentments toward people they love
- Selfish relief when bad things happen to others
- Sexual thoughts at inappropriate moments
- Judgmental observations they'd never say aloud
- The impulse to abandon, betray, or hurt

**Example of Polite Writing:**
> Allison watched Emily storm out, feeling the weight of everything unspoken between them. She wanted to follow, to fix this, but knew that wasn't possible anymore.

**King/Koontz Approach:**
> Allison watched Emily storm out and felt—God help her—relief. Let her be someone else's problem for ten minutes. Let her go cry in her room and come out tomorrow pretending nothing happened, the way she always did. Allison hated herself for the thought even as she had it, but there it was: she was tired of being the one who cleaned up other people's feelings.

**What Changed:**
- The protagonist has a morally ugly reaction
- She recognizes it as ugly but has it anyway
- The emotion is specific (tired of cleaning up feelings)
- No clean resolution or self-forgiveness

**Prompting Language:**
> "The character should have at least one thought in this scene that they would never say out loud—something petty, selfish, or cruel. Don't excuse it. Don't resolve it. Let it sit."

---

## TECHNIQUE 4: BODY HORROR OF THE ORDINARY

**The Problem:** ChatGPT describes emotions abstractly. Fear is "a cold sensation," dread is "weight."

**King puts horror in the body:**
- Nausea, salivation before vomiting
- Bowel cramping (yes, really)
- The specific feeling of a panic attack (vision tunneling, numbness in fingers)
- Sweat, trembling, the taste of adrenaline
- Sleep deprivation symptoms (gritty eyes, the world slightly too bright)

**Example of Polite Writing:**
> Fear moved through her like a wave. Her heart raced. She couldn't catch her breath.

**King/Koontz Approach:**
> Her stomach dropped so fast she thought she might actually shit herself. That happened to people—she'd read about it—soldiers, accident victims, people who got genuinely scared. Her mouth flooded with spit the way it did before throwing up, and she swallowed hard, tasting copper, tasting the burger she'd had for lunch reasserting itself.

**What Changed:**
- Specific, embarrassing bodily reality
- A digressive thought (she'd read about it)
- Detailed sensory experience (taste of copper, taste of lunch)
- The horror is happening TO a body, not abstractly

**Prompting Language:**
> "Describe fear through specific physical sensations. Include at least one embarrassing bodily response—nausea, sweating, trembling, the urge to pee. Make the reader feel it in their own body."

## TECHNIQUE 5: INTERRUPTED STRUCTURE

**The Problem:** ChatGPT writes complete scenes with clear arcs. Beginning, middle, end. Tension, climax, resolution.

**King interrupts constantly:**
- Scenes end mid-beat
- Chapters cut away at maximum tension
- Important information arrives in fragments
- Characters realize things partially, wrongly, or too late
- The reader often knows more than the character (dramatic irony)

**Example of Polite Writing:**
> The conversation ended with both of them knowing more than they said. Allison walked home understanding that something fundamental had shifted.

**King/Koontz Approach:**
> "Allison—" Evan started.
>
> But she was already walking, and she didn't want to hear whatever he was about to say, because if he said it she'd have to know it, and right now the only thing keeping her upright was the possibility that she was wrong.

**What Changed:**
- Scene ends on interruption
- Character actively avoiding knowledge
- Tension carried forward rather than resolved
- The reader is left wanting

**Prompting Language:**
> "End this scene without resolution. Cut away at the moment of maximum tension. Do not let the character reach understanding—let them walk away still confused, still afraid."

---

## TECHNIQUE 6: THE LONG WAIT

**The Problem:** ChatGPT accelerates toward revelation. It wants to explain.

**King delays relentlessly:**
- The monster doesn't appear until you've lived with the dread
- Information is wrong, partial, or misleading
- Characters make reasonable assumptions that miss the point
- The reader knows something is wrong before knowing what

**For the Undiscovered Witch series specifically:**

The horror isn't that Allison has power. The horror is:
- Watching her use it while believing she's helping
- Knowing the cost before she does
- Seeing other characters adjust to her without understanding why
- The slow reveal that her "kindness" has erased things that needed to exist

**Prompting Language:**
> "Do not reveal [X] in this chapter. Let the character almost understand, then get interrupted. Let the reader feel something is wrong without being told what."

---

## TECHNIQUE 7: COMPASSIONATE DAMNATION

**The Problem:** ChatGPT wants characters to be sympathetic through their virtues.

**King makes characters sympathetic through their failures:**
- The alcoholic who loves his family and destroys them anyway
- The good person who makes the cowardly choice
- The villain who is right about something important
- The hero who causes more harm than the antagonist

**For Allison:**
She should be sympathetic not because she means well, but because:
- Her harm is recognizable (we've all "helped" someone who didn't want it)
- Her self-deception is familiar
- Her power is an amplification of ordinary human tendencies
- She's wrong in ways we've been wrong

**Prompting Language:**
> "The character is not a villain, but they are causing harm. Make the harm specific and show a real person being affected. Do not excuse the character. Do not have them realize what they've done yet."

## SPECIFIC PROMPTS FOR CHATGPT

### Opening Prompt (System/Custom Instructions):

> "You are helping write literary horror in the style of Stephen King and Dean Koontz. This means:
> - Characters think like real people, including profanity, self-interruption, and petty judgments
> - Settings are specific and slightly ugly, with brand names and sensory details
> - Fear is physical: nausea, trembling, embarrassing bodily responses
> - Epiphanies are partial or wrong; scenes end without resolution
> - Protagonists have ugly thoughts they don't act on but can't stop having
> - Horror comes from psychological truth, not spectacle
> - Dread builds slowly; information arrives in fragments
> 
> Do not write 'polite' prose. Do not give characters elevated, literary thoughts. Do not resolve tension cleanly. Do not explain the horror—let it accumulate."

### Scene-Level Prompt Template:

> "Write a scene where [situation]. 
> 
> Requirements:
> - Close third person POV on [character]
> - [Character] should have at least one ugly/petty thought
> - Include specific physical sensations of [relevant emotion]
> - End the scene without resolution
> - Include at least one specific sensory detail that's unpleasant
> - [Character] should almost understand something but get it wrong or incomplete
> 
> Do not: explain the thematic significance, give the character a clean epiphany, or resolve the emotional tension."

### Revision Prompt:

> "Revise this passage to be less 'polite.' Specifically:
> - Replace abstract emotional language with physical sensation
> - Add one ugly thought the character would never say aloud
> - Make the setting more specific (brand names, sensory details)
> - Remove any clean epiphanies or thesis-statement thoughts
> - End on uncertainty rather than understanding"

## EDITING CHECKLIST

After generating text, check for:

**Language:**
- [ ] Does the character think like a real person or an essayist?
- [ ] Is there at least one profanity or crude thought?
- [ ] Are emotions described through body or through abstraction?

**Setting:**
- [ ] Are there specific brand names or cultural references?
- [ ] Is there at least one unpleasant sensory detail?
- [ ] Does the place feel lived-in or generic?

**Structure:**
- [ ] Does the scene resolve or does it end on tension?
- [ ] Does the character understand fully or partially?
- [ ] Is information revealed or is it still hidden?

**Character:**
- [ ] Does the protagonist have at least one ugly thought?
- [ ] Are they morally complex or morally coherent?
- [ ] Is their harm visible or excused?

## EXAMPLES FROM YOUR EXISTING WORK

### Already Working Well (Book 1, Chapter 16):

> "The crying wasn't loud.
> That was what made it dangerous.
> It stayed contained, trapped behind clenched teeth and shallow breaths. The kind of crying that didn't summon adults or draw crowds. The kind that waited patiently for permission to exist."

This is excellent. The metaphor of crying "waiting for permission to exist" is King-level insight. The physical specificity works.

### Could Be Pushed Further:

> "She knew what she had done.
> And she knew something worse.
> She would do it again.
> Not because it felt right.
> Because it worked."

This is good thematically but slightly too clean. Consider:

> "She knew what she had done. And the worst part—the part that made her want to peel her own skin off—was that she'd do it again. Tomorrow. The next time someone was too loud or too sad or too inconvenient. She'd smooth them down like a wrinkle in a sheet, and she'd feel relieved, and that relief would taste like bile, and she'd swallow it anyway because what else was she supposed to do?"

## APPLYING THIS TO THE SERIES

The Undiscovered Witch series already has the foundation for deep horror:

1. **Allison's power is ordinary control amplified** — This is terrifying because we recognize it. Every parent who wants their kid to "just calm down," every partner who wishes their spouse would "stop overreacting." Allison is that impulse with teeth.

2. **The Veilward Circle's philosophy is recognizable institutional harm** — "Silence is kindness" is the logic of every system that prioritizes stability over truth. The Circle isn't evil; they're wrong in familiar ways.

3. **The cost is invisible to the person causing it** — Allison doesn't see what she erases. That's the horror. Make the reader see it while she doesn't.

**Specific Opportunities:**

- **Emily's POV chapters:** Let the reader experience what it feels like to be smoothed. The frustration of feeling angry and then... not. The hollow confusion afterward. The creeping suspicion that your feelings aren't your own.

- **Minor characters:** Show the long-term effects on people Allison "helped" years ago. Who have they become? What did they lose? Do they even know?

- **Allison's body:** When she uses her power, what does it feel like physically? There should be a cost that lives in her body, something she can't intellectualize away.

---

## FINAL NOTE

The goal isn't shock value or grimdark. King and Koontz write horror that *matters* because it's about real human failures: the husband who drinks because facing his problems sober is too hard, the mother who knows her child is troubled but can't admit it, the town that let something happen because stopping it would have been uncomfortable.

Allison Carter belongs in that tradition. Her horror is the horror of helping too much, of loving in ways that erase, of good intentions that remove agency. That's scarier than any monster because we've all done some version of it.

Make us feel it.
