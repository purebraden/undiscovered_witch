# Chapter 25

They didn’t call it a procedure.

They moved like people who didn’t want words sticking to anything.

The older woman shifted the table first, dragging it a few inches farther from Rachel’s chair without asking. The legs shrieked against the floor. Rachel flinched and stopped herself from adjusting the chair again.

Miri stood up too fast and knocked her knee against the toolbox. She swore under her breath, pressed her palm hard against the bruise, and didn’t look at Rachel when she crossed the room.

“Don’t,” the older woman said, not raising her voice.

Miri froze.

The pressure crowded Rachel’s ribs, tight and irritated, as if it had leaned forward and found something in the way.

“We’re not closing anything,” the older woman said, more to the room than to Miri. “We’re widening.”

The gray-haired man set his mug down and pushed it away from himself. A dark ring marked the table where it had been.

Rachel watched the ring instead of their faces.

“What are you doing?” she asked.

No one answered right away.

Miri pulled a coil of chalk line from the toolbox and snapped it once, the sound sharp in the low room. She moved carefully, laying the line out in a loose arc that didn’t touch the walls, didn’t touch Rachel, didn’t close back on itself.

The pressure shifted, annoyed.

Rachel felt it lean toward the chalk, then pull back, like a hand testing heat.

“Grounding,” Lena said, not sounding convinced.

The older woman shook her head. “Deflection.”

Miri’s hands shook as she worked. The chalk left faint marks on the floor, uneven where her grip slipped. She wiped her palm on her pants and tried again.

Rachel’s chair creaked.

She didn’t move.

The pressure pressed closer, dense and watchful.

The gray-haired man stood and took two steps toward the seam, then stopped and leaned his shoulder against the wall instead. He closed his eyes.

Miri finished the arc and stepped back. “That’s as wide as I can make it.”

The older woman nodded. “Good. Now don’t cross it.”

Miri swallowed.

The older woman looked at Rachel. “This isn’t for you.”

Rachel opened her mouth. Closed it.

The pressure crowded her chest, then slid sideways, skimming the chalk line. One of the bulbs dimmed. Another flickered in response, late.

Miri hissed and clutched her wrist.

“What happened?” Lena asked.

Miri shook her head. “Nothing. It’s—” She stopped. Flexed her fingers. “Pins and needles.”

The pressure coiled tighter.

Rachel felt a pull—not on her body, but on her attention—dragging her focus toward the chalk arc, the unevenness in it, the place where the line thinned and nearly broke.

“Don’t look at it,” the older woman said.

Rachel looked away.

The pressure surged anyway.

The chalk line blurred.

Not smudged.

Shifted.

Miri gasped and staggered back, hand flying to her arm. “It’s—” She sucked in a breath. “It’s pulling.”

The older woman stepped back again, more distance now. “Step out.”

“I can’t,” Miri said. Her feet slid a fraction across the floor, soles whispering. “It’s like the floor’s—”

The chalk snapped.

The sound was small.

The effect wasn’t.

The arc collapsed inward, lines skidding across the floor toward the gap, chalk dust lifting into the air in a pale cloud.

The pressure lunged.

Rachel felt it move before she did. Her chair rocked back, then settled, the motion damped and wrong, like something had grabbed it mid-fall.

Miri screamed.

Not loud.

Breathless.

She dropped to her knees, clutching her arm as if something were sliding under the skin. The bulbs flickered wildly, then steadied.

The gray-haired man lurched forward and stopped short, fists clenched at his sides. “Miri.”

“Don’t touch her,” the older woman snapped.

Miri curled in on herself, breath coming fast and shallow. “It’s not pain,” she gasped. “It’s—wrong. It’s skipping.”

Rachel stared at Miri’s arm.

The skin there wasn’t torn.

It was… off. The outline of the muscle shifted a fraction late when Miri moved, like a reflection lagging behind.

Rachel’s stomach dropped.

“I didn’t—” she started.

The older woman cut her off. “No.”

The pressure crowded Rachel’s ribs, sharp and offended.

“This was the risk,” the older woman said. “You don’t redirect something like that without loss.”

Lena’s voice was tight. “You said it would bleed off.”

“It did,” the woman said. “Just not where you wanted.”

Miri sobbed once, short and startled, like the sound surprised her. “Make it stop.”

The pressure leaned forward.

Rachel felt the pull again, stronger now, like a current finding a new channel. Her hands lifted off her thighs before she noticed they were moving.

“No,” Lena said, sharp.

Rachel froze, fingers curled in midair.

The pressure pressed tighter, impatient.

Rachel dropped her hands back down, palms flat against her legs. Her heartbeat spiked, then smoothed too fast.

Miri screamed again, louder this time.

The chalk dust settled.

The older woman backed toward the seam and pressed her palm to the stone. “That’s enough.”

The room didn’t listen right away.

Then the pressure shifted, pulling back a fraction, like a tide hitting a shelf.

Miri collapsed forward, sobbing hard now, clutching her arm.

The bulbs steadied.

Silence fell, thick and wrong.

Rachel’s breath came shallow and quick. She stared at her hands, at how still they were.

“I can fix it,” she said.

Every head turned.

The older woman’s voice was flat. “No.”

Rachel swallowed. “It moved when she did. If I just—”

“No,” the woman said again, louder now. “You don’t fix things yet. You make them worse.”

The pressure crowded Rachel’s chest and stayed there, tight and aggrieved, like it had been denied something it wanted.

Lena crouched beside Miri, careful not to touch her arm. “We’ll get you stabilized.”

Miri shook her head, tears streaking down her face. “I don’t feel… in order.”

Rachel closed her eyes.

Behind them, the seam creaked softly.

The older woman watched Rachel from across the room, eyes sharp and tired. “This is why we stop the run,” she said. “Because motion hides the damage.”

Rachel opened her eyes.

The pressure leaned forward.

Not moving her.

Waiting.
