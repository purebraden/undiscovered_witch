# Chapter 26

They moved Miri first.

Not lifted. Not carried.

Someone slid a mat across the floor and Miri ended up on it in pieces, her body guided by hands that hovered instead of touched, fingers stopping short and then adjusting again when the first angle proved wrong. She cried under her breath, teeth knocking together, breath coming apart in little skips. Her arm lay against her side in a way that wasn’t broken and wasn’t right, the timing off, like it had missed a step and never caught up.

Rachel stayed in the chair.

She didn’t stand. She didn’t lean. She kept her hands flat on her thighs and her feet planted exactly where they were, even when the pressure crowded her ribs and urged movement—not direction, just motion, the suggestion of it.

The older woman spoke without looking at Rachel. “Stay seated.”

Rachel nodded.

“And don’t watch.”

Rachel dropped her gaze to the floor, to the chalk smear between her boots where someone had tried to wipe a line away and only made it thinner.

“And if something goes wrong—” The woman paused, listening to the room instead of finishing the sentence. “—you don’t step in.”

Rachel nodded again, throat tight.

Lena stood near the seam with her arms crossed, weight set wrong on one hip. She didn’t look at Rachel. She didn’t look at Miri either.

The mat scraped over a seam in the floor and Miri gasped, a sharp, involuntary sound that made Rachel’s teeth itch. The pressure shifted, irritated, sliding sideways like it was testing where to go next.

The gray-haired man reached up and twisted one bulb loose. The light dimmed near Rachel. Another bulb stayed bright, harsh over the mat.

Miri’s breathing hitched. “It’s still—” She stopped and swallowed. “It’s skipping.”

“I know,” the older woman said. “Count.”

Rachel counted too. She didn’t mean to.

One. Two. Three.

The pressure coiled tighter, like it recognized the rhythm.

Miri screamed.

Not loud.

Sudden.

Her body jerked against the mat, legs kicking once before Lena and the gray-haired man pinned the edges down with their weight, careful with where they put it, careful not to touch her arm.

The bulbs flickered. One went out entirely.

Rachel’s chair rocked back and then settled again, the motion damped and wrong, like it had been caught halfway through falling.

She stayed seated.

The pressure surged, impatient.

“Don’t resist,” the older woman said, and it wasn’t clear who she was talking to.

Rachel’s head lifted before she stopped it. “What?”

The woman didn’t turn. “Resisting makes it look for another path.”

Miri sobbed, words tumbling out too fast. “It’s sliding. I can feel it sliding.”

Rachel’s hands lifted a fraction off her thighs before she noticed. She pressed them back down hard, fingers digging into fabric.

The pressure spiked.

“Don’t anticipate,” the older woman said, sharper now.

“I’m not,” Rachel said, and even she could hear the lie in it.

The room answered first.

The pressure slid again, brushing the chalk smear on the floor. The smear thinned, breaking apart.

Miri screamed again, longer this time.

Rachel squeezed her eyes shut.

She pictured weight. Gravity. Everything pulled down and kept there by nothing more complicated than mass.

The pressure leaned forward.

Rachel didn’t move.

Miri’s arm jerked once and then went still.

Too still.

The room froze around it.

The gray-haired man swore softly.

Lena said Rachel’s name, low and tight.

Rachel opened her eyes.

Miri lay on the mat, chest heaving, eyes unfocused. Her arm lay straight now, aligned—but wrong. When she flexed her fingers, the movement came late, like the signal had taken the long way around.

“It stopped,” Miri whispered.

The older woman didn’t look relieved. She watched Rachel instead.

“You followed the rules,” she said.

Rachel nodded, breath shallow. “I did.”

“Yes,” the woman said. “And it still routed through her.”

The pressure crowded Rachel’s ribs, tight and offended, like it had been accused of something unfair.

Lena crossed the room and crouched beside Miri, careful again not to touch her arm. “We’ll keep you stable.”

Miri looked at Rachel. Not angry.

Afraid.

“I didn’t—” Rachel started.

“Intent doesn’t enter into it,” the older woman said.

Rachel’s mouth closed.

The gray-haired man screwed the bulb back in. The light evened out a fraction. The room felt smaller for it.

The older woman stepped closer than she had before. Not close enough to touch. Close enough that Rachel felt it anyway.

“These rules don’t protect you,” she said. “They protect everyone else.”

Rachel nodded.

“And if they don’t?” Rachel asked.

The woman met her eyes. “Then we move you.”

The pressure leaned forward at that.

Not eager.

Interested.

Rachel stayed where she was, hands flat, breath shallow, listening to the room adjust itself around her.

Nothing went wrong right away.

The pressure stayed.

Learning.
