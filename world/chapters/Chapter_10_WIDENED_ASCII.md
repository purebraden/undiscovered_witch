# Chapter 10

Rachel came up through the hatch slowly, the metal resisting at first, warped and stiff enough that she had to brace her shoulder against it and lean her weight forward until it gave with a dull scrape that sounded too loud in the tight space below.

She froze halfway out, breath held, waiting for someone to shout or a hand to grab her ankle.

Nothing happened.

She pulled herself the rest of the way up and let the hatch settle back into place, metal touching stone without a clang, the sound swallowed before it could travel.

The courtyard was narrow, wedged between two buildings whose stone walls rose close on either side, upper windows shuttered tight. The air smelled different here, not cleaner so much as arranged, smoke lingering without heat, like something that had burned earlier and been handled.

Rachel stood still.

The pressure in her chest loosened, just a little.

She stepped forward, slow and careful, testing the ground with each movement. Nothing pushed back. Nothing twisted sideways. She crossed the courtyard and reached the narrow street beyond without the pull tightening.

The buildings were low and plain, their fronts unadorned, no banners or signs hanging from the walls, no lamps burning despite the early hour. The street curved gently out of sight in both directions, the stone underfoot worn smooth and patched where repairs had been made recently.

Rachel limped into it, steps sounding too loud until she adjusted her pace, shifting her weight despite the ache spreading through her leg.

A doorway passed on her left with boards freshly nailed across it, pale wood standing out against older stone. A simple line had been painted across the boards in dull white. Farther along, a window stood open with its curtains pulled back but unmoving in the still air, the room beyond empty.

She slowed.

The pressure stirred, then eased when she stopped.

Rachel stayed where she was.

At the far end of the street, a man appeared carrying a crate against his chest. He walked a few steps before looking up and seeing her. He paused, shifted the crate, then turned without a word and stepped into a side alley, vanishing from view.

Rachel waited, breath shallow, hands loose at her sides.

The pressure stayed low and steady.

She moved on.

The street widened gradually into a small square paved with uneven stones, a dry fountain at its center with a cloth draped over the basin. The same simple line marked the fabric, brushed on with deliberate strokes. Benches lined the edges of the square, their surfaces clean and empty.

Rachel crossed slowly, scanning doorways and windows, noting shutters drawn tight, locks newly set, metal bright against worn wood. When she reached the far side, she stopped again.

The pressure tightened when she turned back.

It eased when she faced forward.

Her throat worked as she swallowed.

She kept walking.

Beyond the square, the buildings grew taller and older, their facades crowded with signs and lamps that burned steady and bright. Voices carried faintly from farther down the road, layered and indistinct, the sound of a city that had not stopped for her.

Rachel slowed at the edge where the quiet ended.

Her foot hovered over the change in stone.

The pressure coiled.

She pulled her foot back.

Behind her, the street she had come from remained still, holding its shape without sound or movement.

Ahead, the city went on.

Rachel turned sideways and followed the edge instead, keeping to the narrow strip where light thinned and sound dulled, moving without drawing notice while people went about their business a few steps away.

A woman sweeping her doorstep glanced up as Rachel passed, eyes sliding over her and away again, the broom never pausing.

Two guards stood talking quietly at an intersection farther on, one gesturing down a side street, the other nodding without looking up. Their conversation did not break as Rachel passed between them.

The pressure eased.

Rachel walked until her leg shook and her mouth tasted of metal, then stopped in a narrow lane where the buildings leaned close together and the air felt heavier. She sank down against the wall and closed her eyes.

The pressure settled.

Somewhere above the rooftops, a bell rang once, then again.

When Rachel opened her eyes, the street she had come from was no longer visible.

She stayed where she was, listening to the city move around her, and did not try to follow the sound.
