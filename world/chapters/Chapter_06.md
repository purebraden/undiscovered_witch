# Chapter 6

Rachel climbed until her lungs burned and every breath tasted like ash.

The tunnel she’d fallen into refused to behave like a tunnel ought to. It split and narrowed, dipped and rose again, and every time she thought she’d found a way out the stone shifted under her feet, slanted just wrong, like the place was rearranging itself out of spite.

Smoke followed her in thin, lazy ropes, never close enough to choke her, never far enough to forget.

Her leg didn’t throb the way it should have, and that was the problem. It should have been screaming at her, open and raw and furious, demanding attention, but instead it sat there in her awareness like a hot coin pressed to the skin—warm, heavy, wrong in a way that made her stomach uneasy.

She kept moving anyway, because stopping felt too much like lying down, and lying down felt like dying.

The pull—different now, quieter—rested in her chest like a hand that wasn’t guiding so much as holding, fingers curled just tight enough to remind her it was there.

She hit a narrow rise and nearly went down when her bad foot slipped. Her hand shot out on instinct and slapped against the wall.

The nausea hit so fast it stole her breath.

Her vision tunneled. Black crept in at the edges. For a horrible second she thought she was going to pass out right there, crumpled in the dark with smoke creeping closer.

Rachel jerked her hand away and sagged against her knees, gagging.

“Okay,” she whispered, shaking. “Okay. Message received.”

The nausea eased—not because she willed it to, but because whatever lived under her skin decided it had made its point.

She pushed on, slower now, testing each step like the ground might punish her for choosing wrong.

The next corridor widened into a low service passage with old iron pipes running along the ceiling, greened with age. Moisture dripped steadily into a shallow channel cut in the floor, and the air smelled of damp stone and rust with something faintly sweet underneath, like bruised fruit left too long in a warm room.

Rachel hated that smell.

It reminded her of the basement, of heat and cages and voices that had begged right up until they burned.

She rounded a bend and found a ladder bolted to the wall. At the top, a rectangle of faint gray light hovered like a dirty coin.

She grabbed the rungs and climbed.

Halfway up, the pull tightened.

Not hard. Just enough.

Rachel froze, breathing through her teeth, hands slick with sweat on the iron. Above her, voices drifted down through the stone, muffled but clear enough to make her stomach drop.

Not shouting.
Not panicked.

Working voices.

“…mark the grate. The lower run is compromised.”
“Move the cordon. If it comes up here—”
“—don’t touch it bare.”

Rachel closed her eyes.

She hadn’t made noise. She hadn’t gone into the streets. She hadn’t done anything except bleed and crawl and survive.

They were already here.

The pull tightened again, sharp enough to hurt, and she understood then that it wasn’t telling her to go up or down.

It was telling her not that way.

Rachel backed down the ladder, rung by careful rung, wincing at every faint clink of iron. Her hands left damp prints behind, shining briefly in the dim light.

When her boots hit the floor, she turned away from the glow above.

The pull eased.

“Fine,” she mouthed silently. “Fine.”

The passage led to another split. The left smelled like sewage and cold air. The right carried a faint trace of smoke.

Rachel took one step left.

Pain flashed behind her eyes, sudden and punishing, like a nail driven too deep. Her knees buckled and she caught herself against the wall, gagging as the nausea surged back harder this time, angry, insistent, as if she’d dared to argue.

“Stop,” she hissed, fury breaking through the fear. “Stop doing that.”

The nausea loosened slowly, like a leash being relaxed one notch.

Rachel stood there shaking, staring at the fork. Left was a no. Right was a maybe. That was the only language she had now.

She limped right.

The corridor rose toward the surface, and she could feel it in the air—cooler, drier, touched by distant wind. Then she heard it: not horns this time, but a bell close enough to vibrate faintly in the stone, and beneath it the sound of people moving. Not running. Shifting. Being managed.

Rachel slowed as the passage opened into a low cellar stacked with crates and a door hanging crooked on one hinge. Moonlight spilled through the crack, pale and dirty.

Outside lay a narrow street, not one of the main roads with lamps and noise, but a back street lined with closed shops, shutters barred, paint peeling.

Across the way, a woman crouched in the shadow of a doorway, feeding something small from a cloth sack. A cat. Or a rat. Rachel couldn’t tell.

The sight of another living thing that wasn’t trying to kill her tightened something in Rachel’s chest.

She stepped out.

Her boot hit the cobbles and the mark on her leg pulsed.

Warmth flooded her blood like a slow pour of whiskey. Her skin prickled. The light under her skin deepened, thickened, as if it had been waiting for open air all along.

Rachel froze, breathing hard.

The woman looked up.

Her eyes traveled over Rachel’s face, then down to her soaked boot, her scraped hands, the soot streaking her clothes, and finally—like a compass needle snapping—locked onto Rachel’s leg.

The woman didn’t scream.

She just went very still.

Rachel took a step forward, palms lifting a little without her really deciding to do it.

“I—” she began.

Her tongue thickened. The word slid wrong in her mouth.

“Don’t,” Rachel heard herself say, and the sound of her own voice made her flinch.

The woman’s mouth opened.

“You—”

Rachel tried again, forcing the word past the numbness.

“Please,” she meant to say.

“Get back,” came out instead.

The woman lurched to her feet, retreating into the doorway as if the shadows might swallow her. Her hand fumbled behind her for something—bolt, latch, charm, Rachel didn’t know.

Panic surged, sharp and familiar, and with it came the pressure, the reflex that had already ruined rooms and bodies and lives.

“No,” Rachel rasped. “Wait.”

The mark pulsed.

A low vibration moved through the street, not a roar or a spell-word, just a sound like the stone itself clearing its throat.

The cobbles between Rachel and the woman shifted.

Only a fraction.

Enough.

The woman stumbled as the ground dipped beneath her, slammed back into her doorway hard enough to shake dust loose from the frame.

Rachel stared, horrified.

“That wasn’t—” she started.

The numbness eased for one brief second, just long enough for one clean thought to land.

It did that to keep her away.

Rachel stepped back.

The pull loosened, pleased, like a dog relaxing after it got what it wanted.

The woman’s breath came fast and shallow. She stared at Rachel with a kind of fear that had nothing to do with being robbed or stabbed.

It was the fear you get when you recognize a storm and realize it sees you too.

Down the street, a lantern flared to life.

Then another.

Bootsteps followed, measured and purposeful.

A calm voice carried through the dark. “Hold positions. No contact.”

Rachel’s heart hammered.

The woman whispered something under her breath and made a sign over her chest with trembling fingers.

Rachel backed into the cellar’s shadow.

The pull tightened again, urging her down, back into the dark, away from open streets and eyes.

Rachel wanted to scream. She wanted to kick the wall. She wanted to rip her own skin open and dig the thing out with her nails.

Instead, she turned and ran the only way she was allowed.

Back underground.

Behind her, lantern light crept closer, sliding along the cobbles like a searching finger, and the calm voice repeated, softer now, as if speaking to a skittish animal.

“Easy. Easy. We know what you are.”
