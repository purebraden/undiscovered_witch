# Chapter 48 — Not Where It Keeps Things

The light changed first.

Not brighter. Not darker.  
Different.

Rachel felt it through her eyelids before she opened them, a pressure shift that didn’t belong to the place she was in, like someone had opened a door somewhere far above and the air had decided to notice. The thing near the wall stilled, its shape compressing in on itself, attention snapping away from her so fast it felt like being dropped.

The pressure inside her recoiled.

That’s not meant to be open, it warned.

Before she could ask what *that* meant, the floor lurched.

Not a slide.  
A failure.

The surface beneath her thinned all at once, no warning, no negotiation, and Rachel screamed as gravity returned with teeth, hauling her down and then up in the same breath, the sensation so wrong it turned her stomach inside out.

The dark tore.

Light punched through in a jagged column, white and hard and real, and the space around her folded inward like it was trying to close a wound. Something grabbed at her ankle—pressure, not hands—and then let go with a sharp, irritated snap.

You’re in the way now, it said.

She hit ground that did not move.

Grass. Gravel. Something wet and cold.

Rachel rolled hard, breath exploding out of her lungs, shoulder slamming into earth that smelled alive and filthy and right. She lay there choking, palms sinking into mud, the night air cutting into her skin like it had edges.

Rain hit her face.

Not a memory.  
Not a projection.

Actual rain, cold and stinging, soaking through her clothes in seconds.

Above her, something boomed.

Not thunder.

Metal screaming under stress. A distant crack like concrete failing wrong. Shouts followed—human voices, panicked and overlapping, words she couldn’t separate but recognized as real because they didn’t smear when they hit her ears.

The pressure inside her chest pulled tight, disoriented.

Outside isn’t quieter, it warned.

“I don’t care,” she gasped, dragging air into her lungs like she’d been underwater too long. Her ankle screamed when she tried to move, pain sharp and grounding, proof she was back in a body that obeyed physics even when it hated her.

She rolled onto her side and vomited into the grass, bile burning her throat, hands shaking so badly she couldn’t keep herself upright afterward. Rain plastered her hair to her face. Mud coated her palms and knees.

The world stayed put.

Lights flared somewhere nearby—harsh, industrial, mounted too high—and silhouettes moved through them, figures running, slipping, shouting orders that overlapped and collided.

Someone yelled her name.

Or maybe just a name.

The pressure inside her shifted, retreating, like it had been shoved out of the room and was deciding whether to try the door again.

They broke something important, it said.

Rachel laughed once, wet and hysterical, and pressed her forehead into the mud.

“Good,” she whispered.

Behind her, the ground shuddered.

Not collapsing.  
Sealing.
Below, whatever had opened sealed. Above, the outer doors must have been thrown wide for evacuation.

A deep, final sound rolled through the earth, like a vault door closing somewhere she couldn’t see. The lights steadied. The shouting changed pitch, urgency reorganizing itself.

Hands grabbed her shoulders.

Rachel flinched violently, gasping, swinging blind, nails scraping against fabric and skin until someone cursed and let go.

“Easy—easy—don’t—”

She curled in on herself instinctively, rain slicking her lashes, heart hammering so hard she thought it might tear free.

The pressure inside her didn’t answer.

Not gone.

Just… pushed back.

Rachel lay there in the mud and rain and noise and let the outside world prove itself by continuing to exist.

She was free of the space.

Whatever else had come with her was going to have to keep up.

Rachel stayed down longer than she meant to.

Rain slid into her hair and down the side of her face, cold enough to sting, steady enough to count. Her ankle throbbed in a slow, ugly rhythm that felt solid, dependable in a way nothing else did. She pressed her palm into the mud and pushed, stopping halfway when the ground dipped under her weight and then settled again like it had changed its mind.

She breathed anyway.

In.  
Out.  
Not evenly.

“Are you—” Her voice cracked and she swallowed hard. “Are you still there.”

Nothing answered right away.

The pressure sat just behind her ribs, not gone, not forward, like something that had leaned back in its chair.

“Did anything come with me,” she whispered, because that felt like the most important question and also the one she least wanted answered.

Rain filled the space where a response should have been. Somewhere nearby, a light buzzed and cut out and buzzed back on.

Her jaw tightened.

“What did they break,” she said, sharper now, words pushed out without care.

The pressure shifted, late.

The voice slid in: you didn’t wait—

“For what,” she snapped, and the anger surprised her with how easy it came.

She wiped rain out of her eyes and forced herself to look properly. Grass torn to mud. A slope that didn’t line up with how she remembered the ground being. A fence farther off, leaning wrong, reflective tape snapping wetly in the wind.

Footprints.

Not hers.

Too many. Too light. Coming toward her instead of away.

Her stomach dropped.

“That’s wrong,” she murmured, and then louder, “No one else was—”

“Rachel?”

The sound hit her late.

She flinched hard, twisting toward it, pain tearing up her leg as she moved too fast. The world slid and then caught.

Lena stood a few yards away.

Not stepping into view.  
Just there.

Rain darkened her jacket. Her hair was plastered to her forehead. She was breathing hard, chest rising and falling too fast, like she’d been running longer than she should have. Mud already coated her shoes.

For a second, Rachel couldn’t make the image settle.

Lena wasn’t supposed to be here.

Not outside.  
Not now.  
Not past the doors.

“How are you out here,” Rachel blurted, the words coming out wrong, accusation bleeding through fear before she could stop it.

Lena blinked. “What?”

“How did you get out here,” Rachel said again, pushing herself upright despite her ankle screaming. The ground dipped and steadied and she ignored it, eyes locked on Lena. “I thought everything was sealed.”

Lena took a step closer and then stopped like she’d hit an invisible line. Confusion flickered across her face. “Rachel, I heard something break and then—” She waved a hand behind her, the gesture incomplete. “People started running. The outer doors were open. I just— I followed.”

Rachel shook her head, slow and small. “That doesn’t line up,” she said. “You shouldn’t have been able to just walk out.”

Rain drummed between them.

The pressure leaned in, curious.

She isn’t where you left her, it noted.

Rachel’s breath hitched.

Lena reached out and then pulled her hand back, fingers curling into her sleeve. “You’re bleeding,” she said instead. “We need to get you up.”

Rachel looked down like that was new information. Mud on her hands. Blood smeared along her sleeve she didn’t remember earning. Her hands shaking hard enough that she had to clench them to make it stop.

“Lena,” she said quietly, because loud felt dangerous now. “How did you get past the doors.”

Lena opened her mouth.

Metal clanged behind them—loud, wrong—followed by raised voices snapping into urgency, boots hitting pavement out of sync.

Lena’s head turned toward the sound.

Rachel didn’t move.

The pressure slid back, repositioning, like it had lost interest in the question and was already looking ahead.

You don’t get to finish this, it said.

Lena’s hand landed on Rachel’s shoulder.

The moment tore.
