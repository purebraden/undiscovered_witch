# Chapter 45 — Shared Weight

They didn’t move right away.

Rachel leaned into the wall because it was there and because the floor still felt undecided about whether standing counted as a stable request. The ache in her ankle had spread upward into a heavy, nauseating throb that made her leg feel borrowed, like it belonged to a slightly misaligned version of her body and hadn’t been returned properly.

A few steps away, the other person shifted their weight without settling, bare feet testing the floor in small, uncertain adjustments. They watched the space more than they watched Rachel, eyes tracking the wrong frame at the center of the chamber and then flicking away again, like looking at it too directly caused problems they’d learned not to invite.

The hum thickened.

Not louder.  
Denser.

It pressed against Rachel’s ears until the air felt packed, resistant.

The pressure pressed a flat statement into her head: you’re carrying excess variance.

Rachel swallowed. “That’s bad,” she muttered, not bothering to ask.

The other person’s head snapped up—not toward the sound, but toward *her*. “What?”

“Nothing,” Rachel said too quickly, and immediately hated how thin it sounded.

The floor twitched.

Not a shake.  
A correction.

Rachel’s shoulder slid against the wall with a soft scrape that echoed from two directions at once. The pressure inside her chest shifted sideways, searching, as if trying to find a position that fit and failing.

The other person rubbed their arms, fingers digging into skin hard enough to leave pale crescents. “When it starts doing that,” they said, nodding at the floor, “you don’t argue with it. You make yourself smaller. Or farther away.”

“From what?” Rachel asked.

They hesitated. “From the part that doesn’t belong.”

Rachel snorted once, sharp and breathless. “That’s reassuring.”

The lights flickered in response, one strip dimming while another overcorrected, brightness rippling unevenly across the ceiling.

The wrong frame answered.

Its internal distortion tightened, light folding inward and then slipping back, the air around it bending just enough to make Rachel’s eyes sting.

Weight is misallocated, it noted.

Rachel pushed off the wall experimentally and immediately regretted it. The floor dipped under her good foot—not instantly, but delayed, as if it had waited to be sure—stealing her balance after she thought she’d found it. She grabbed for the rail and felt a faint pulse under her palm, warmth bleeding into her skin.

The other person stepped closer without meaning to, reaching out and stopping short, hand hovering inches from Rachel’s elbow.

“Don’t touch,” they said sharply, eyes on the rail, not on Rachel.

Rachel froze.

The space reacted anyway.

A low groan rolled through the floor—not sound so much as pressure—like something large had shifted its weight somewhere below them. The painted lines on the floor shimmered, arcs slipping out of alignment and snapping back with a faint visual lag.

Rachel’s heart hammered.

You destabilize first, it said.

“That’s not a choice,” Rachel muttered.

The other person let out a short, humorless laugh. “It always is.”

Boots sounded somewhere distant.

Not above.  
Not below.

Close in a way that didn’t respect the geometry of the room.

Rachel felt it immediately—the pressure inside her tightening, attention snapping toward the sound like it had been waiting for confirmation.

They’re mapping stress through you, it warned.

The wrong frame pulsed brighter, cables sinking imperceptibly deeper into the floor, as if rooting.

The other person took a step back.

The floor under *their* feet responded differently—steadier, less delayed, like it preferred their weight. Rachel saw it happen and the noticing made everything worse.

Her ankle flared with pain, sharp and jagged, and the floor dipped harder beneath her this time, late but decisive, as if it had finally made up its mind.

The other person swore under their breath. “It’s pulling *you*,” they said, voice tight. “That’s not good.”

Rachel laughed once, breathless. “You’re saying that like it’s new.”

You are the variable.

The lights drained again, edges of the room smearing as if someone had dragged a thumb across reality. The hum deepened until it vibrated in her teeth.

The boots were closer now—individual steps audible, cadence breaking and reforming as whoever was moving recalibrated.

The other person widened the distance between them without looking back.

The floor steadied beneath them.

Rachel felt it like a rebuke.

“Hey,” she said, panic sharpening her voice. “Don’t—”

“I’ve done this before,” the other person said, jaw tight. “If you stay close, it collapses faster.”

“Done what?”

“Stayed.”

The wrong frame shuddered, its internal distortion collapsing inward for a heartbeat before surging back brighter, harsher.

They’re rerouting containment around you, it said.

Rachel pushed herself upright despite her ankle screaming, forcing weight through pain that arrived in overlapping waves. The floor rejected her this time—not opening, not breaking, but *pushing back*, like it resented the demand.

She staggered toward the other person, hand outstretched.

“Don’t leave me here,” she said, the words tearing out of her before she could stop them.

The other person hesitated, conflict flickering across their face.

The space held its breath.

Boots hit a nearby threshold.

A voice called out, distorted, stretched. “We’ve got movement. Brace.”

The wrong frame answered with a deep, resonant pulse that blurred Rachel’s vision.

Choose now, it said.

Rachel didn’t know what that meant.

She only knew the distance between them mattered, that the room was already negotiating which of them it could afford.

She took one more step.

The floor dipped.

The other person reached for her at the same instant the space lurched sideways.

Everything slid.
