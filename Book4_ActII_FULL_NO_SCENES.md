# Chapter 5 — Pre-Travel Briefing

The room had been used for briefings long enough that no one bothered to decorate it anymore.

Allison noticed that first. Not the people, not the table, not the faint shimmer of containment woven into the walls—but the absence of any attempt to make the space welcoming. The stone was smooth and pale, the lighting even and shadowless, the chairs arranged with careful neutrality around a table whose surface held nothing but a thin band of etched glyphwork running just inside the edge. No flowers. No banners. No windows.

A place meant to explain things without inviting response.

Tomas waited near the far wall, hands folded in front of him, posture precise in a way that suggested practice rather than tension. He had already been here when Allison arrived, which meant he had arrived earlier still. He always did.

“You can sit anywhere,” he said, before she had taken a step. His voice was calm, steady, almost conversational. “The placement doesn’t matter.”

That alone made her pause.

Allison chose the chair closest to the door. It felt practical, even though she had no intention of leaving. The chair was cool when she sat, its surface neither uncomfortable nor welcoming, as if the material had been selected specifically to avoid reaction.

Tomas took the seat across from her, not beside her, not directly opposite, but offset just enough to avoid a confrontational line. The table between them was wide enough that she could not reach him without leaning forward.

That, she thought, was deliberate too.

Before either of them spoke again, Allison became aware of the quiet. It was not silence—there was a low hum beneath it, a steady resonance that felt less like sound and more like pressure equalizing. She recognized it without knowing why.

Containment. Stabilization. A space prepared to prevent things from happening.

“This is a pre-travel briefing,” Tomas said. He did not look at any notes. “It is not an orientation. It is not training. It is not consent.”

He let that sit between them.

Allison nodded, once. She folded her hands in her lap to keep from fidgeting.

“The purpose,” Tomas continued, “is to ensure that expectations are aligned before you are exposed to a foreign magical structure.”

“Exposed,” Allison repeated quietly.

“Yes.” Tomas did not correct her tone. “Not introduced. Not immersed. Exposure implies limited duration, supervised conditions, and controlled withdrawal.”

He paused, watching her closely—not for reaction, she realized, but for comprehension. When she did not speak, he continued.

“You will not be asked to act,” he said. “You will not be asked to participate. You will not be asked to assist, augment, or stabilize.”

Allison’s attention sharpened. “Asked,” Allison said.

Tomas inclined his head a fraction. “Correct. You will not be asked.”

That was not reassurance. It was boundary definition.

Across the table, the etched glyphwork along the edge pulsed once, faintly, then stilled. Allison felt it register her presence, catalog it, then withdraw. The sensation was subtle, but unmistakable—a system taking note and then choosing not to engage further.

She wondered how many times that had already happened since she arrived at the Confluence.

“You will be accompanied at all times,” Tomas said. “By me, or by a designated liaison. Observation will occur in structured intervals. Sessions may be ended early.”

“May,” Allison echoed.

“Will,” Tomas corrected gently. “If necessary.”

She nodded again.

A door at the far end of the room opened without sound. Lysa Merewen entered with a small stack of thin, flexible slates tucked under one arm. She smiled when she saw Allison, the expression earnest and careful, as if she had practiced it in advance.

“Allison,” Lysa said warmly. “Thank you for being prompt.”

“It seemed important,” Allison replied.

“It is,” Lysa agreed, taking the chair beside Tomas—not close, but within the same conversational triangle. She set the slates on the table but did not activate them. “This is not a complicated briefing, but it is a precise one.”

Tomas did not react to that phrasing, which Allison took as confirmation that it had been agreed upon beforehand.

Lysa folded her hands on the table. “You’ve been approved for supervised exposure to the Fairy World. That approval is narrow.”

“How narrow?” Allison asked.

Lysa’s smile softened but did not widen. “Narrow enough that we are having this conversation.”

Tomas spoke again before Allison could press. “You will cross a boundary,” he said. “That boundary does not behave like the thresholds you are accustomed to.”

“Time?” Allison asked.

“Structure,” Tomas replied. “Obligation.”

Lysa nodded. “The Fairy World operates on recognition. Entry is not simply movement from one place to another. It is acknowledgment—by the world and by the individual.”

Allison felt a faint tightening in her chest at that word. Recognition.

“You will be recognized,” Lysa continued. “That recognition will not grant authority. It will not grant permission. It will not confer role.”

“It will,” Tomas added, “make you visible.”

The word landed heavily. Allison shifted in her chair despite herself.

“Visibility,” Tomas said calmly, “is not inherently dangerous. But it is not neutral.”

Lysa glanced at him, then back to Allison. “That’s why this exposure is supervised. That’s why it’s limited. And that’s why we are being very careful about language.”

Language. Allison thought of all the rules she had learned already, all the ways phrasing mattered more than intent.

“You will not attempt to interact with fairy magic,” Tomas said. “You will not attempt to trace it. You will not attempt to align with it.”

“I won’t,” Allison said quickly.

“I know,” Tomas replied. His tone did not change. “But the rule exists regardless.”

Lysa activated one of the slates. A projection shimmered above the table—not an image, but a diagram of interlocking lines and empty spaces. No labels. No legend.

“This is not a map,” Lysa said, anticipating the question. “It’s a representation of relational density. Fairy magic does not organize itself around discrete effects. It organizes around commitments.”

Allison stared at the projection. The lines seemed to shift slightly when she focused on them, as if responding to attention rather than presence.

“Don’t follow it,” Tomas said quietly.

She looked up at him. He was watching her hands.

“I wasn’t,” she said.

“I know,” he said again. “But your attention matters.”

Lysa deactivated the projection. The air seemed to relax when it vanished.

“This is where we need to be very clear,” Lysa said. “You have demonstrated an ability to recognize magical systems without engaging them.”

Allison felt the weight of that statement. It was not praise. It was categorization.

“That ability,” Lysa continued, “is rare. It is also poorly understood.”

Tomas’s jaw tightened, just slightly.

“The Confluence has protocols for exposure,” Lysa said. “We have metrics. We have analysts. What we don’t have is precedent for someone who notices structure without attempting to resolve it.”

Allison swallowed. “I don’t always notice on purpose.”

“That,” Tomas said, “is one of the concerns.”

Silence settled again. Not empty—never empty—but thick with things unsaid.

Lysa cleared her throat. “The Fairy World has its own observers. You may encounter them.”

“Will they talk to me?” Allison asked.

“Possibly,” Lysa said. “But not in the way you’re used to.”

“You are not to respond,” Tomas said, before Lysa could elaborate.

Allison looked between them. “At all?”

“You may acknowledge presence,” Tomas clarified. “You may not acknowledge obligation.”

She frowned. “What’s the difference?”

Tomas considered her for a moment. “Presence is noticing that something is there. Obligation is recognizing that it expects something of you.”

Allison’s breath caught. She understood that distinction too easily.

“That,” Lysa said softly, “is why this is exposure only.”

The door opened again, and another figure entered without announcement. Naelis of the Thread moved with unhurried precision, their pale hair bound back with a simple cord, expression unreadable. They did not take a seat. They did not greet anyone.

They simply stood near the wall, watching.

Allison felt it immediately—not a pressure, not a pull, but a shift in the room’s orientation. As if the space had decided to pay attention.

“This is Naelis,” Lysa said, her voice respectful. “They will observe.”

Naelis inclined their head a fraction, eyes never leaving Allison.

“Observation,” Naelis said, their voice quiet and even, “is entry without claim.”

Allison nodded, unsure whether that counted as acknowledgment.

“You are not here to take,” Naelis continued. “You are not here to learn. You are here to be seen noticing.”

That sentence settled into Allison’s thoughts and stayed there.

Tomas stood. “That concludes the briefing.”

Lysa blinked. “Tomas—”

“She has enough,” Tomas said firmly. “More would encourage framing.”

Lysa hesitated, then nodded. “All right.”

Naelis did not move.

“Allison,” Tomas said, turning back to her. “We will proceed shortly. Until then, you will wait.”

“Here?” she asked.

“In a prepared space,” he said. “Not alone. Not active.”

Waiting. Again.

As they moved toward the door, Allison glanced once more at the empty table, the inactive glyphs, the slates already stacked away. Nothing had happened. No decision had been made. No power had been touched.

And yet, she felt as though something fundamental had been named.

Not opened. Not granted.

Named.

She followed Tomas out of the room, carrying that recognition with her—and very deliberately doing nothing with it.

They did not take her far.

That was the first thing Allison noticed once they left the briefing room. The corridors bent and narrowed, but the distance between spaces was deliberately short, as if whoever had designed this section of the Confluence had wanted movement without momentum. Tomas walked at a steady pace, not hurried, not slow, his presence ahead of her acting as both guide and limiter.

“This is a holding interval,” he said, as if responding to a question she had not asked. “It is not preparation.”

Allison nodded. She had learned the difference already. Preparation implied readiness. This felt more like containment with courtesy.

The waiting space was smaller than the briefing room, but warmer in tone. The stone here carried faint veins of gold that did not glow so much as remember having glowed once. The ceiling was low enough to feel intentional. There were three chairs, arranged in a loose arc rather than around a table, and a shallow basin of water set into the floor near the far wall. No ripples disturbed its surface.

“This area dampens projection,” Tomas said, stopping just inside the threshold. He did not cross fully into the room. “It does not suppress awareness.”

“That sounds… specific,” Allison said.

“It is,” he replied. “Please sit.”

She chose the middle chair this time, though she could not say why. The seat molded subtly to her weight, adjusting until she was supported without being cradled. Tomas remained standing, one hand resting lightly against the doorframe.

“You are not to engage with the basin,” he said.

“I wasn’t planning to,” Allison said.

“I know,” Tomas said, for the third time that day. “The rule exists regardless.”

The door did not close behind her. It remained open, a quiet reminder that this was not isolation—only pause.

Allison let her gaze drift around the room. The air felt different here. Less neutral. Not welcoming exactly, but attentive in a way that made her aware of her own breathing. She slowed it deliberately.

“How long?” she asked.

Tomas glanced at a small device at his wrist—one she had seen before, though she still did not understand how it worked. “Approximately twelve minutes.”

“Approximately,” she repeated.

“Yes.”

She did not ask why. She was beginning to recognize which questions invited answers and which only produced more rules.

Minutes passed without comment. Allison focused on the basin, careful not to look at it directly for too long. Even in her peripheral vision, she could sense something layered there—depth without distance, a surface that did not reflect so much as absorb.

She wondered, briefly, whether the Fairy World would feel like that.

“You are allowed to notice sensations,” Tomas said quietly. “You are not required to report them unless asked.”

“All right,” Allison said.

She shifted her attention inward instead. There was a familiar hum beneath her awareness now, like a system idling, waiting for instruction it would not receive. Her magic—if it could be called that—felt alert but restrained, as if it had been told very firmly to sit still.

That, she realized, was new.

Not suppression. Direction.

“Tomas?” she asked after a while.

“Yes.”

“If I… recognize something,” she said carefully, choosing each word, “and I don’t act on it—does that still count?”

His answer was immediate. “Yes.”

She exhaled. “Even if no one else notices?”

“Yes,” he said again. “Especially then.”

The weight of that settled slowly. Allison stared at her hands, folded neatly in her lap. Recognition without witness. Impact without intention.

The basin rippled once.

She stiffened, every instinct flaring, but Tomas’s voice cut through the moment.

“Do not track it,” he said sharply.

“I’m not,” she said, though her pulse had quickened.

“I know,” he said, but this time there was an edge beneath the calm. “But the environment responds to attention. That is why we limit duration.”

The ripples faded. The surface returned to stillness, as if nothing had happened.

Tomas checked his device again. “Two minutes remaining.”

Allison nodded, forcing her shoulders to relax. She fixed her gaze on the stone floor instead, counting the faint gold veins, grounding herself in the physicality of the space.

When the time was up, Tomas straightened. “Stand, please.”

She did. The chair released her without resistance.

“This concludes the briefing interval,” he said. “From this point forward, you are under active observation.”

“I thought I already was,” Allison said.

Tomas met her eyes. “There are degrees.”

He stepped aside, gesturing for her to follow him out of the waiting space and back into the corridor. As they walked, Allison felt a subtle shift, like a line being drawn behind her. Not a barrier. A record.

She did not know where it led. She did not try to find out.

For now, she simply walked—aware, contained, and increasingly certain that noticing was already changing more than anyone was ready to admit.

# Chapter 6 — First Crossing

They did not announce the crossing.

That, Allison would realize later, was the point.

They left the holding interval through a corridor she had not seen before—narrower than most, its walls curved just enough to keep her from judging distance accurately. Tomas walked a half step ahead of her, close enough that she could hear his breathing if she focused on it, far enough that she could not see his hands.

“Maintain your pace,” he said, quietly. “Do not slow to examine the environment.”

“I’m not,” Allison said.

“I know,” Tomas replied. “This is procedural.”

The corridor smelled faintly of damp stone, though the air was warm and dry. Allison noticed the contradiction and then immediately felt the tension that followed noticing anything at all. She forced her attention forward instead, fixing on Tomas’s shoulders, the line of his spine, the way his gait never quite changed.

“This transition is not a threshold,” Tomas said as they walked. “There is no boundary you will feel. No moment you should anticipate.”

“All right,” Allison said.

“If you believe you have identified a moment,” he continued, “you have misinterpreted it.”

She frowned, then smoothed her expression again. “So I shouldn’t—”

“You should not try,” Tomas said gently. “Trying implies agency.”

They walked on.

The corridor widened almost imperceptibly. The stone beneath Allison’s feet shifted in texture—not enough to stumble on, not enough to name. The hum she had noticed earlier deepened, not louder but more complex, as if additional tones had been added beneath it.

Allison felt it register her.

Not welcome. Not challenge.

Acknowledgment.

Her breath caught before she could stop it.

“Do not adjust your breathing,” Tomas said immediately.

“I wasn’t—”

“I know,” he said again. “But involuntary responses are still responses.”

The air pressed differently against her skin. Not heavier. More interested. Allison resisted the urge to look around, keeping her gaze forward as instructed, though her peripheral vision seemed fuller somehow, as if there were more information available than she was processing.

“This is exposure,” Tomas said. “Not entry.”

“What’s the difference?” Allison asked before she could stop herself.

“Entry involves claim,” he replied. “Exposure involves being seen without reply.”

They took three more steps.

Then Tomas stopped.

Allison halted automatically, her foot half lifted, then set it down again. Nothing dramatic happened. No shift, no sensation of crossing a line.

“We have transitioned,” Tomas said.

She blinked. “We have?”

“Yes.”

“That was it?”

“Yes.”

Allison waited for the rest of the explanation. It did not come.

She became aware, slowly, that the space around them was no longer quite the same corridor. The walls were still stone, still curved, still close enough to limit her vision—but the way the light touched them had changed. It no longer reflected so much as lingered, clinging faintly to edges and seams.

She felt as though she were standing in a sentence she had entered midway through.

“This is the Fairy World,” Tomas said. “At the margin.”

“It looks…” She hesitated. “It looks the same.”

“At first,” Tomas said.

He raised one hand slightly—not a gesture of command, but of caution. “Do not extend your awareness.”

“I’m not,” Allison said, though her chest felt tight again.

“I know,” Tomas said. “The rule remains.”

They stood in silence.

“You are being recognized,” Tomas said quietly.

She swallowed. “By what?”

“By structure,” he replied. “Not individuals.”

“Twelve minutes,” Tomas said, glancing at his device. “Initial exposure window.”

“So we’re already here.”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “You were before you noticed.”

They moved only a few steps before Tomas stopped her with a brief touch at her elbow.

“Stand here,” he said. “Do not turn.”

She obeyed.

“This is where we pause,” Tomas said. “You will wait.”

Again.

Pathways suggested themselves at the edge of her awareness. Expectations, not invitations.

She did not follow them.

“That sensation,” Tomas said, “is alignment awareness. You are not to pursue it.”

“I won’t.”

“I know. But recognizing it changes outcomes.”

“Even if I don’t act?”

“Yes. Especially then.”

Minutes passed.

“We will withdraw,” Tomas said at last.

The air thinned. The hum resolved.

“We have returned,” Tomas said.

“That’s it?”

“Yes.”

They did not leave the corridor immediately.

“This portion is not for you,” Tomas said. “But you are required to remain present.”

He recorded data quietly.

“Your presence altered recognition density,” he said.

“I didn’t do anything.”

“I know. That is the point.”

They walked again.

“This space is neutral relative to both systems,” Tomas said. “You are not to attempt comparison.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I know.”

A faint shift passed.

“Do not respond.”

“I’m not.”

“That is sufficient.”

Later, near the observation wing, Tomas stopped.

“You may experience delayed recognition effects,” he said. “This does not permit exploration.”

“I know.”

“This exposure window is concluded,” Tomas said formally. “Further Fairy World contact is suspended pending review.”

“Pending whose review?”

“Multiple.”

They parted.

Allison walked on, carrying the quiet certainty that recognition had occurred—and that nothing about it had asked her permission.


The room was not designed to hold attention.

Allison noticed that immediately. There were no focal points, no natural place for her eyes to settle. The walls were the same muted stone as the rest of the Confluence, but smoother, less textured, as if even irregularity had been sanded down. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, evenly distributed, impossible to track.

“Stand there,” Tomas said, indicating a mark on the floor that was only visible when you knew to look for it.

Allison stepped onto it and felt the faintest shift beneath her feet, like the space acknowledging her position.

“This is an observation session,” Tomas said. “Not a test.”

Allison nodded. She clasped her hands in front of her, careful not to fidget. “What am I supposed to do?”

“Remain,” Tomas replied.

She waited, then realized that was the answer.

The air in the room did not change, but Allison felt the expectation of change, the same way she sometimes felt the expectation of rain before clouds formed. Her awareness sharpened despite herself, skimming the edges of the space, noticing how the light seemed fractionally denser near the ceiling, how the silence had a shape to it.

“Do not seek,” Tomas said.

“I’m not,” Allison replied.

“I know,” he said. “This instruction is preventative.”

He moved to the side of the room and adjusted the device at his wrist. Allison did not look directly at it, but she felt the Confluence respond—an almost imperceptible tightening, like a system entering a more precise mode.

“This session establishes baseline behavior,” Tomas continued. “Your baseline.”

Allison swallowed. “Baseline for what?”

“For recognition,” Tomas said. “Not response.”

She frowned slightly. “I don’t—”

“You don’t have to understand it,” Tomas interrupted gently. “Understanding is not required for observation.”

That felt important, though she wasn’t sure why.

Time passed.

Or rather, it stacked, the moments settling on top of one another without obvious markers. Allison became acutely aware of her own breathing, the way her weight shifted subtly as her muscles adjusted to standing still. She noticed patterns in the room—not magical ones, not exactly, but structural rhythms: the way the ambient hum rose and fell in intervals, the way the light seemed to recalibrate itself when she exhaled too deeply.

She noticed.

And then she noticed that she was noticing.

A flicker of something—alignment, maybe—brushed the edge of her awareness. It wasn’t an invitation. It was a possibility, hovering just out of reach.

Allison did not follow it.

Tomas’s voice cut through the moment. “You recognized something.”

Allison startled slightly. “I didn’t do anything.”

“That is correct,” Tomas said. “And expected.”

She hesitated. “How do you know?”

Tomas gestured subtly toward the room. “Because the Confluence registered a change in your attention profile.”

That made her chest tighten. “My attention did something?”

“Yes,” Tomas said. “It shifted.”

“But I didn’t—”

“You did not act,” Tomas said again, firmer this time. “You did not engage. That distinction matters.”

He stepped closer, not invading her space, but enough that she felt anchored by his presence.

“Recognition alone is not prohibited,” Tomas continued. “But it is not neutral.”

Allison absorbed that in silence.

“Observation sessions exist to teach restraint,” Tomas said. “Not because restraint is morally superior, but because untrained curiosity produces unstable outcomes.”

“That sounds… bad,” Allison said quietly.

“It is inefficient,” Tomas corrected. “Which becomes dangerous at scale.”

She nodded, though the words sat heavily in her mind.

Another stretch of time passed. Allison felt the same near-alignment again, a little stronger this time, as if the space were checking whether she would follow the thread she had already seen.

She did not.

Tomas ended the session immediately.

“That’s it?” Allison asked, surprised.

“Yes,” Tomas said. He adjusted his wrist device, and the subtle pressure in the room eased. “Early termination is intentional.”

“Why?”

“Because the goal is not endurance,” Tomas replied. “It is consistency.”

She thought about that. “So if I’d stayed longer…”

“You would have been tempted to explore,” Tomas said. “And we are not testing that today.”

Allison nodded slowly.

“This session will be logged,” Tomas added. “As non-interventive recognition.”

“That sounds like a label,” Allison said.

“It is,” Tomas replied. “Labels help the Confluence predict risk.”

That word again.

Risk.

“Am I… risky?” Allison asked before she could stop herself.

Tomas considered her for a moment. “You are uncalibrated,” he said. “That is not the same thing.”

He gestured toward the door. “We will repeat sessions like this. Often. With variation. With limits.”

“And if I mess up?” Allison asked.

Tomas met her eyes. “Then we stop earlier.”

The answer was simple. Final.

As they left the room, Allison glanced back once at the empty space. It looked unchanged, inert, as if nothing at all had happened there.

But she knew better now.

The room had been watching.

And so had she.

# Chapter 7 — Orientation

The orientation room was warmer than the briefing spaces.

Not noticeably so at first. Allison only became aware of the difference after she had already sat down, when the tension in her shoulders failed to ease in the way she expected it to. The stone walls carried a softer hue than the rest of the Confluence—less gray, more pale amber—but the color was muted enough that it felt provisional, like a suggestion rather than a commitment. The lighting was angled to reduce shadow rather than eliminate it, leaving depth intact while discouraging focus.

The chairs were wider than the ones used in the briefing chambers. Their surfaces responded slowly to her weight, adjusting over several seconds instead of immediately, as if comfort were something to be negotiated rather than granted.

Allison noticed all of that without meaning to.

She also noticed that the room still had no windows.

“This space is designed to be supportive,” Lysa Merewen said, her voice calm and careful as she took the seat across from Allison. She placed her hands on the table, fingers loosely interlaced, posture open without being casual. “Orientation can feel overwhelming if it’s handled too clinically.”

Tomas stood near the wall to Allison’s left, hands folded behind his back. He did not sit. He did not lean. His posture was unchanged from the briefing spaces, as if the room’s softened parameters did not apply to him.

Allison nodded once. Supportive, she thought, did not mean permissive.

“This isn’t training,” Lysa continued, her cadence measured, as though she had practiced saying the sentence many times. “And it’s not assessment. Orientation is about context—about making sure you understand the framework you’re operating within.”

The word operating caught in Allison’s chest. Not sharply. Just enough to register.

“You are not expected to perform,” Lysa added quickly, as if anticipating the reaction. “You’re not here to demonstrate anything. What you experienced earlier was a controlled exposure, nothing more.”

Tomas shifted his weight almost imperceptibly.

“Within approved parameters,” Lysa amended, her eyes flicking briefly toward him before returning to Allison.

Allison folded her hands together in her lap, aligning her fingers carefully. “What parameters?” she asked.

Lysa smiled—not dismissively, but with the practiced patience of someone accustomed to incomplete answers. “Those will be introduced gradually. Orientation isn’t about detail. It’s about alignment.”

There it was again.

“The Confluence,” Lysa continued, “has a responsibility to ensure that individuals under its protection are not placed at unnecessary risk. That includes cognitive risk, emotional strain, and structural exposure.”

Tomas spoke without turning his head. “Clarify.”

Lysa inhaled, then nodded. “Structural exposure refers to prolonged contact with systems that operate on incompatible assumptions.”

“Incompatible with what?” Allison asked.

“With each other,” Lysa replied. “And, sometimes, with the individual.”

The room remained warm. The chairs remained supportive. Nothing about the environment reacted to the exchange, and that lack of response made Allison more aware of her own stillness.

“This is why orientation matters,” Lysa said. “We want to ensure that what you notice doesn’t become burdensome.”

“Noticing already feels burdensome,” Allison said, her voice quieter than she intended.

“That’s understandable,” Lysa replied gently. “But noticing doesn’t have to mean responsibility.”

“Noticing creates responsibility regardless of intent,” Tomas said.

The difference in emphasis lingered between them, unresolved.

“This orientation,” Lysa said after a moment, “is meant to help you remain grounded while we proceed carefully. You are not alone in this process.”

Allison absorbed the phrasing. Remain grounded. Proceed carefully.

“You’re helping us understand how recognition functions,” Lysa added, earnest now. “That’s valuable.”

“Allison is not here to help us,” Tomas said. “She is here to be protected.”

“I didn’t mean to confirm anything,” Allison said quietly.

“That’s part of what makes it useful,” Lysa replied.

Silence followed—not tense, but weighted. The room seemed to hold its temperature steady, neither warming nor cooling further, as if waiting to see whether the balance would shift.

“This concludes the first orientation segment,” Lysa said at last.

“That is sufficient,” Tomas agreed.

As Allison stepped back into the corridor, the warmth receded subtly, as if comfort were conditional on remaining seated. She felt, with quiet certainty, that she had been accounted for.

The second room was smaller.

Not cramped. Just arranged so that her attention had nowhere to wander. The distance between surfaces was reduced: the table narrower, the chairs closer together, the ceiling lower in a way that discouraged upward focus. The lighting was more direct here, eliminating the softer angles of the previous space.

“This portion of orientation is interactive,” Lysa said as they settled in. “Within limits.”

Allison waited, then asked, “How often will exposures happen?”

“At first, infrequently,” Lysa replied. “Later will depend on stability indicators.”

“What kind of indicators?” Allison asked.

“That’s determined collectively,” Lysa said. “Patterns, not moments.”

Tomas nodded once. “We do not evaluate isolated events.”

“So if nothing goes wrong,” Allison said slowly, “that still counts?”

“Yes,” Lysa said. “It still informs the model.”

“And if something does go wrong?”

“Exposure will be suspended,” Tomas said.

“How quickly?”

“As close to immediately as the system allows.”

Allison absorbed that phrasing.

“What happens if I notice something outside an exposure?” she asked.

“You disengage,” Tomas said.

“And report?”

“Yes,” he replied. “If and when you are asked.”

“We don’t want you monitoring yourself,” Lysa added. “That can create unnecessary strain.”

“So I shouldn’t think about it,” Allison said.

“You can think,” Lysa replied. “Just not toward action.”

The distinction felt impossibly thin.

Allison became aware, gradually, that each question she asked altered the room’s attention—not visibly, not dramatically, but in the same way pressure changes are felt before they are measured.

Her questions were data.

She nodded once. “All right.”

She would ask less.

The third space was not called a data room.

Allison knew that immediately, because everything about it suggested the term had been deliberately avoided. The walls were the same pale stone as the rest of the Confluence, but the air carried a faint sense of activity—processes running just beneath perception. Light fell in thin, precise bands across the far wall, illuminating sections that shifted slowly, as if responding to variables she could not see.

Lysa gestured for her to stand rather than sit.

“This won’t take long,” she said. “We just want to show you where things land.”

Tomas positioned himself slightly behind and to Allison’s right.

One of the illuminated sections sharpened, resolving into abstract lines and markers. No labels appeared.

“This is aggregated,” Lysa said. “Trend-focused.”

Allison took a step without realizing it.

“Do not step closer,” Tomas said sharply.

She stopped at once, her pulse quickening.

“This suggests that recognition—passive recognition—can alter outcomes,” Lysa continued. “Without escalation.”

“Allison does not need comparative baselines,” Tomas said.

The display dimmed slightly, as if withdrawing.

“You’re helping us see what’s possible,” Lysa said.

Allison stared at the shifting lines, understanding dawning slowly, uncomfortably.

She was not a participant.

She was a proof.

Her assigned room was exactly the size it needed to be.

# Chapter 10 — Quiet Prohibition


The room was built to look agreeable.

Allison felt the small, instinctive release in her chest—and immediately distrusted it.

Not *you*.
*Conditions*.

Allison nodded once.

Allison felt the familiar tightening. She did not look at Tomas.

The word *favorable* sounded strange in the room. Not celebratory. Just… catalogued.

“However,” the woman continued, “favorability is not the sole metric by which stability is assessed.”

There it was.

Stability.

Allison recognized the structure of the argument before it completed itself. Favorable outcomes increased scrutiny. Increased scrutiny narrowed space.

“We are not prepared,” the woman said, “to expand the scope of your exposure at this time.”

Allison waited. The phrasing was careful. *Not prepared*. *At this time*. She could hear the door in it—closed, but not sealed.

“This is not a prohibition,” the woman added smoothly. “It is a deferral.”

Allison nodded again. She had learned the difference. Deferral meant waiting without timeline.

Tomas spoke then, for the first time. “For clarity,” he said, his voice even, “this does not alter her current restrictions.”

“That’s correct,” the woman said. “Existing constraints remain appropriate.”

Appropriate. Another word that smoothed edges without removing them.

Allison’s breath remained steady.

“This request is made,” the woman added, “for stability.”

The phrase landed with quiet finality.

Allison understood exactly what was being asked of her. Not to stop noticing. Not to forget what she had already seen. Only not to go further.

She also understood—without anyone saying it—that going further was possible.

The recognition settled in her chest like weight.

He did not offer.

Facing a wall panel that wasn’t lit yet.

Appropriate.

Refine.

As if she had been doing this on purpose.

Precautionary was one of those words that sounded reasonable in any context. It made restriction feel like maturity.

Pursuit.

By narrowing.

When conditions support it.

Not when she was ready. Not when she asked. When conditions—external, institutional, undefined—shifted.

“Thank you,” the man said immediately, as if her nod had completed a contract. “That is the response we expected.”

The man glanced briefly toward him, then back to Allison. “Tomas will continue to supervise your sessions,” he said. “And he will continue to stop them early when necessary.”

As if ending her access to experience were a routine safety feature. As if Tomas’s caution were part of the design, not a personal intervention.

Tomas’s jaw tightened once—so small Allison almost missed it.

“Yes,” Tomas said.

All parties.

Maybe she wasn’t even the primary one.

The panel dimmed.

The meeting ended without ending. No dismissal. No closing words. Just the cessation of instruction.

As they left the room, the man spoke one last time.

“Allison,” he said, tone unchanged. “This is temporary.”

Allison turned her head just enough to acknowledge him.

Temporary was a word people used when they wanted you to stop asking how long.

She nodded again.

In the corridor, the hum felt louder. Allison realized she had been holding her breath.

Tomas did not speak until they were far enough away that no panel could activate without warning.

Then he said, very quietly, “Do not interpret ‘temporary’ as permission.”

Allison’s throat tightened.

“I won’t,” she said.

Tomas looked ahead, not at her. “Good,” he said. “Because they will.”

Presence.
