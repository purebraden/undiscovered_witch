# Chapter 1: The Weekend

The handoff was fast.

Too fast for small talk, too fast for second guesses. The girls climbed out of the car with their backpacks already on, jackets zipped, hair still damp from the rain. Their mother stayed in the driver’s seat, engine running, phone wedged between her shoulder and ear, doing that thing where a person is physically present but mentally halfway down the road.

“I’ll text when I’m home,” she said, distracted. “They ate already.”

Robert nodded. He always nodded. The nod had been perfected during the divorce—noncommittal, polite, useful as a paper towel. He took the bags, shut the door, and stood on the curb while her car pulled away and merged back into traffic.

That was the last normal thing that happened.

He was halfway to the front door when the air changed. A pressure drop. The kind that made his ears ring and his stomach tighten for no clear reason, like his body had gotten a text his phone couldn’t receive.

Alicia stopped walking. “Dad?”

Before he could answer, the sound hit—deep, tearing, wrong. Not thunder. Not an explosion. Something structural giving up somewhere nearby. A ripple went through the ground, subtle but undeniable, like the earth had flinched.

Car alarms started screaming up and down the street.

Robert grabbed Samantha’s shoulder and steered her inside. “Shoes off later,” he said, already locking the door behind them. The house lights flickered once, twice, then steadied.

For maybe five minutes, nothing else happened.

They stood in the living room, listening to distant sirens and closer shouting. Someone ran past the house. Then another. Phones buzzed uselessly in pockets, lighting up with *No Signal* and *Call Failed* messages.

Then the second sound came.

Closer this time. A sharp metallic shriek followed by a dull, concussive thud. The lights went out completely.

Samantha screamed.

Robert dropped to a knee, pulling both girls against him. He could smell something acrid through the closed windows. Not smoke. Something cleaner. Industrial. The kind of smell that belonged in a factory, not in your living room on a Friday night.

“Basement,” he said. “Now. Move your butts.”

They made it halfway down the stairs when the pounding started at the front door.

“Help!” a man shouted. “Please—my wife—”

Robert froze.

His hand went toward the deadbolt anyway, because that’s what decent people did. That’s what they were raised to do. Then he pictured a stranger’s hand on the other side of the door, and the picture didn’t come with a face. It came with teeth.

The pounding came again. Harder. The doorframe rattled.

Alicia’s grip tightened on his arm. She didn’t say anything.

Robert listened.

He counted breaths. One. Two. Three. Four—because he was the kind of guy who believed the extra breath might change the outcome.

Another sound cut through the shouting—glass breaking somewhere down the street. A crash, followed by a woman screaming.

The pounding stopped.

Footsteps ran past the house.

Robert exhaled slowly and kept moving, hating himself for it and doing it anyway.

They stayed in the basement until the noises thinned out, until the shouting turned into distant, directionless sound. When they finally came back upstairs, the house felt smaller. Like it had already learned something about them.

Outside, the street was dark. No streetlights. No passing cars. Just people moving in uneven clusters, flashlights bobbing, voices raised and urgent.

Robert locked the door again.

They ate peanut butter straight from the jar because it didn’t require cooking. Samantha complained about the taste. Alicia didn’t. Alicia watched the dark windows like she expected them to complain back.

Just before midnight, Robert dug the old radio out of the hall closet.

He had to move boxes to get to it—holiday decorations, a folded stroller he’d never thrown away. The radio was heavier than he remembered, metal casing scuffed, antenna bent at the joint.

He set it on the kitchen counter and cranked the dial.

Static.

He turned it slowly, careful not to skip past anything. The noise shifted in texture—hiss to crackle to low hum—but never resolved into words.

Samantha sat at the table with her knees pulled up, watching his hands.

“Is that the news?” she asked.

“Yeah,” he said, because it was easier than explaining why it wasn’t.

Alicia leaned against the doorway, arms crossed. She watched the radio, not him.

For a while, someone spoke.

Not clearly. Just fragments slipping through the static.

“…county…”
“…temporary…”
“…remain—”

Robert leaned closer, adjusting the antenna, heart pounding.

The signal collapsed back into noise.

He tried another band. Then another.

An emergency tone sounded once—sharp, electronic—and cut off mid-note.

No repeat. No follow-up.

Hours passed without anyone noticing until Robert realized his legs had gone numb from standing.

Outside, the street stayed dark. No headlights. No sirens. The shouting didn’t come back.

He kept the radio on anyway.

Around two in the morning, Alicia sat on the couch and pulled a blanket around Samantha without being asked. Robert noticed and looked away.

Sometime after that, the radio stopped making noise altogether.

Not silence—power. The dial still turned, but the static was gone. Dead air.

Robert didn’t turn it off.

He sat on the floor with his back against the cabinet and watched the window until the gray light crept in.

Morning didn’t bring answers.

Phones stayed dark. The radio stayed empty. The air outside looked the same as it had hours ago, heavy and unmoving.

When Robert opened the front door, the quiet pressed in immediately. Not peaceful. Expectant.

A man stood in the middle of the street turning in slow circles, like he was waiting for something to appear.

Alicia came up beside him. “Are we going somewhere?”

Robert watched the man stop, then sit down on the curb.

“No,” he said. “We’re staying.”

She nodded.

Samantha didn’t ask.

The town was quiet in a way that felt permanent.

Robert didn’t know yet that something had noticed them.

He only knew that whatever came next would not involve leaving.

# Chapter 2: No Signal

The radio stayed useless, but Robert kept turning the dial anyway—slow, stubborn, like you could guilt a machine into doing its job. Static filled the kitchen in a flat, even sheet—no rise, no fall—just sound without information, without shape. It sounded like winter television, like a snowstorm inside the speakers, like the world shrugging.

Samantha lay on her stomach on the living room floor, chin in her hands, staring at nothing in particular while the noise went on and on like it had always lived there.

“This is boring,” she said.

“I know,” Robert replied, still watching the radio like it might change if he looked hard enough.

Alicia sat at the table with a notebook open in front of her. She wasn’t writing. She was listening, head slightly tilted, as though she expected the static to slip and reveal something underneath.

“I want my Switch,” Samantha said after a while.

“It’s in the basement,” Robert answered automatically, already half elsewhere. “Be careful.”

She was halfway to the stairs before he realized he hadn’t actually watched her go.

The first few steps sounded normal—socks on wood, soft and familiar, the rhythm of a hundred earlier trips.

Then the rhythm broke.

A sharp crack.

A breathless sound that wasn’t quite a scream.

“Sam?”

The silence afterward landed heavier than noise ever had.

Robert reached the stairs just as Samantha started crying in earnest. She was twisted sideways on the steps, one leg folded under her, both hands gripping her ankle like it might come loose if she didn’t hold it in place.

“I slipped,” she said between sobs. “I didn’t mean to—”

He crouched and touched the ankle. It was already swelling, skin hot and tight under his fingers, the joint misshapen in a way that made his stomach drop.

“Don’t move,” he said.

She tried anyway and screamed.

Alicia stood at the top of the stairs, very still, watching his hands.

Robert lifted Samantha carefully. Her arms locked around his neck, her body shaking as he carried her back to the couch.

The radio hissed on, unchanged.

He didn’t wait to see if it would settle. He didn’t try to convince himself it was nothing. He grabbed his keys, lifted her again, and went.

Outside, the street looked worse than it had earlier—more abandoned cars, fewer people. Those who were still out moved quickly and didn’t look at one another, like stopping carried its own risk.

The clinic lights were on.

That felt like a promise.

Inside, it was chaos under fluorescent lighting. Too many people packed into too little space. A constant low roar of voices layered with crying, coughing, raised arguments that never quite turned into fights. It smelled like wet coats, disinfectant, and fear—fear always had a smell, if you paid attention.

A nurse at the desk didn’t look up when they approached. She looked like she’d been built out of coffee and irritation.

“Name.”

Robert gave it.

“Age.”

“Eight.”

“What happened?”

“Fall. Stairs. Her ankle.”

“Can she walk?”

“No.”

The nurse nodded once, already reaching for a clipboard. “Take a seat. If you pass out, try not to land on anybody.”

They waited.

Minutes stretched into something heavier. Samantha’s crying dulled into small, exhausted sounds as Robert shifted her weight again and again, arms burning, shoulders locking up. People were brought back ahead of them—bleeding, shaking, shouting—while others were sent away just as quickly.

Eventually, a different nurse waved them over without making eye contact.

“Shoes off,” she said, already pulling on gloves.

She pressed along Samantha’s ankle quickly, efficiently, without warning. Samantha cried out.

“Sprain,” the nurse said immediately. “Happening a lot tonight.” Like it was a sale item.

She wrapped the ankle briskly, the elastic bandage firm but imperfect, already loosening at the edges by the time she finished.

“Rest. Ice. Keep it elevated,” she said, turning away as she spoke. “If it’s still bad in a few days, follow up.”

“No X-ray?” Robert asked.

The nurse hesitated just long enough to be irritated. “Not unless she can’t feel her toes. Or the bone starts waving hello.”

She was already gone.

They were ushered back into the waiting area and then toward the door before Robert fully realized the visit was over.

He was still holding Samantha when the world shifted.

Not a flicker. Not a blur.

More like something snapping quietly into place behind his eyes.

Text hung in the air, sharp and wrong, layered over the scuffed wall by the exit.

**STATUS UPDATE RECORDED**  
**Trait Affected: Mobility**  
**Condition Applied: Slowed**

“What the fuck,” Robert whispered, because sometimes profanity was just accuracy.

He turned his head. The words stayed.

He shut his eyes hard and opened them again.

Still there.

“Sir?” someone said sharply.

The text vanished.

His arms felt heavier immediately—not weak, not tired, just weighted, like gravity had tilted slightly against him.

“Dad,” Samantha said quietly.

He froze.

“What’s wrong?” he asked.

She was staring past him, eyes unfocused. “There was… something else.”

“What kind of something?”

She hesitated. “It said I needed help. About not walking by myself.”

His stomach dropped.

“What exactly did it say?” he asked.

She shook her head. “It went away.”

Alicia was watching both of them now. Very carefully.

They were sent outside moments later, the door closing behind them with a hollow final sound.

On the sidewalk, Robert nearly stumbled. His foot caught on nothing, balance lagging just enough to matter.

A man passed them easily, moving faster than he should have been able to with the limp he’d had earlier.

Alicia noticed. Her eyes tracked his feet, then flicked back to Robert’s.

She frowned but said nothing.

By the time they reached the car, Robert’s legs were shaking.

The radio in the dashboard was dead.

No static. No signal.

Just silence.

# Chapter 3: The Ankle

Morning didn’t fix anything. Morning never fixed anything. It just turned the lights on so you could see what was broken.

Samantha tried to stand as soon as she woke up, as if sleeping on it might have been enough. She pushed herself off the couch, jaw set, hands braced, and made it almost upright before her knee buckled and she dropped back down with a sharp breath.

“I’m okay,” she said immediately.

Robert watched her ankle. The wrap from the clinic had loosened overnight, elastic sliding where the swelling pushed against it. The joint looked wrong now—too large, skin stretched tight and shiny, bruising dark and uneven along the side.

“Don’t,” he said. “You don’t have to.”

She nodded, embarrassed, and pulled her foot back onto the cushion.

The house felt exposed in daylight.

Not brighter—just uncovered, like somebody had peeled the wallpaper off reality. Every sound carried. The refrigerator cycling. Wind worrying at the siding. Voices outside that rose and fell without pattern, like the street was trying out arguments it hadn’t committed to yet.

Robert checked his phone.

No signal.

He turned the radio on again. The dial moved. Nothing came through. The world was full of devices doing their little dances without delivering.

“They said sprain,” he said, mostly to himself. “It just needs time.”

Samantha nodded because that was what she did when adults sounded certain. She shifted slightly, then froze as pain cut through the movement.

Robert fetched ice and pressed it gently against the ankle. She flinched but didn’t pull away.

Alicia leaned closer. “It’s worse,” she said.

“I know.”

“It wasn’t like that last night.”

“I know.”

They stayed that way for a while. Ice melting. The quiet stretching thin around them.

Eventually Samantha asked, “Can I go outside?”

Robert hesitated.

“Just the porch,” she said quickly.

He looked out the window. The sky was flat and colorless, distance compressed until the end of the street felt too close. A man limped past with a grocery bag dragging behind him. No one helped him.

“Okay,” Robert said. “But not far.”

Getting Samantha down the front steps took time. She gripped the railing with both hands, moving one stair at a time, breathing hard by the time they reached the sidewalk.

They didn’t make it past the mailbox.

Her foot slipped on loose gravel and she cried out, sharp and startled.

Robert caught her before she fell.

The sound carried.

A man two houses down stopped what he was doing. A woman across the street looked up. Someone closer said, “Hey—”

“She okay?” the woman asked.

“Yes,” Robert said. “Sprain.”

The woman nodded, already reaching into her pocket.

“They’re doing check-ins,” she said. “Just making sure kids are accounted for.”

“I didn’t—” Robert started.

“It’s fine,” she said, not waiting. “What’s her name?”

Robert hesitated for half a second too long.

Alicia answered. “Samantha.”

The woman glanced at the ankle, then at Alicia. “And you?”

“Alicia.”

She typed with her thumb, screen glowing briefly in the daylight.

“Okay,” she said. “They’ll expect you.”

“Expect us where?” Robert asked.

“At the school,” she replied, already turning away. “They’re tracking injuries.”

The word tracking lingered after she left.

Robert carried Samantha back inside.

The effort burned more than it should have. His grip slipped once, just enough that Alicia stepped in without thinking and steadied Samantha’s leg until he adjusted.

Inside, he set her down and stayed crouched, breathing harder than he wanted to.

“They already know,” Alicia said.

“Know what?”

“That she’s hurt,” Alicia said. “That we’re here.”

Robert looked at the door.

The microwave clock ticked over to the next minute.

The sound felt loud.

He waited for something to contradict this.

Nothing did.

They weren’t invisible anymore.

# Chapter 4: First Gathering

Cars were already moving when they pulled out of the driveway.

Not rushing. Not hesitating. Just going, all in the same direction—like the whole town had been nudged with a thumb and decided, sure, why not. At the first intersection, no one waited. The turn simply happened, and Robert found himself doing it too, because sometimes the easiest way to get scared is to let a crowd do your thinking.

The school parking lot was fuller than he expected. Parents stood outside their cars instead of heading straight in, adjusting backpacks, lifting children down carefully, doing that nervous-parent choreography like it was back-to-school night instead of whatever this was. Everyone kept glancing toward the doors like they were waiting for permission from the building itself.

Robert parked farther out than usual.

“I can walk,” Samantha said.

He didn’t answer. He lifted her before she could try. The wrap around her ankle pressed tight against swollen skin, the elastic already digging in. She made a small sound and then stopped herself.

Near the doors, a woman raised her hand.

Not a wave. A signal, like traffic control for the end of the world.

She stepped forward with a clipboard and glanced down at Samantha’s ankle before looking at Robert’s face.

“Okay,” she said, and made a mark.

She handed him a narrow strip of paper. Blue.

“Keep that visible.”

“Why?” Robert asked.

“It helps,” she said, already turning away, which made it sound like she’d said it a thousand times and never once believed it.

Inside, the gym looked wrong.

Not damaged. Organized—the kind of organized that made your shoulders tighten before your brain could explain why.

Colored tape divided the floor into wide paths that led toward different exits. It looked like a kid’s obstacle course, except nobody was laughing and the stakes weren’t a scraped knee. People clustered along the tape, uncertain but compliant, standing where the colors suggested they should stand. No one crossed from one color to another, like the tape might shock you.

A man stood near the bleachers with a megaphone, but his voice was calm.

“We’re just going to get everyone where they need to be,” he said. “This won’t take long.”

Parents shifted. Children leaned against legs. Someone started to ask a question and then stopped.

“Anyone needing help with movement,” the man continued, “please follow the blue line.”

Robert didn’t move.

A staff member appeared at his side.

“Blue,” she said, lightly, already guiding his shoulder.

“I can carry her,” Robert said.

“Yes,” she replied. “That’s fine.”

They stepped onto the blue tape.

It was shorter than the others. Quieter. People here stood closer together, watching instead of talking.

Across the gym, another group began to move.

Alicia watched them.

“Where are they going?” she asked.

“I don’t know,” Robert said.

The green line emptied quickly. Doors opened. People passed through them and didn’t come back.

Another staff member walked the length of the blue line, tablet in hand. She stopped briefly in front of each family, glancing, tapping, moving on.

When she reached them, her eyes flicked to Samantha’s ankle, then to Robert’s arms.

She tapped once and moved on.

“What did she write?” Alicia asked.

“I don’t know,” Robert said again.

The megaphone crackled.

“If your child can’t walk without help, please stay where you are.”

No one on the blue line moved.

The gym grew quieter as the other lines thinned.

Samantha shifted against Robert’s shoulder. “Why are we still here?”

“Because you’re hurt,” he said.

“But they’re hurt too,” she said, nodding toward a boy with a bandaged wrist being guided toward the doors.

Robert didn’t have an answer.

They were directed into the hallway next. Doors stood open, lights already on. Signs were taped beside them in neat block letters.

ASSISTANCE REQUIRED  
LIMITED MOBILITY

Robert slowed.

Samantha read the sign and frowned. “That’s not my name.”

“I know,” he said.

Inside the room, chairs lined the walls. Parents stood instead of sitting. A woman handed Robert another strip of paper, this one a different color.

“If you leave,” she said, “bring this back with you.”

“Leave to go where?” he asked.

She smiled. “Anywhere.”

The door closed.

Robert eased Samantha into a chair. His arms shook when he straightened.

Alicia stood near the doorway, watching people pass in the hall—people without blue paper, moving quickly, carrying nothing.

“Dad?” she asked.

“Yeah?”

“What’s happening?”

Robert opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

The bell rang.

It sounded the way it always had.

# Chapter 5: Faster

They waited in the classroom longer than Robert expected, long enough for the air to go stale and for the initial spike of panic to drain into something duller and meaner. Parents shifted positions without fully sitting down, like sitting all the way would make the waiting permanent. Every few minutes someone glanced at the door, then looked away again when nothing happened—because nothing happening was starting to feel like a decision.

Robert sat with Samantha across his lap, her injured leg carefully angled so the ankle didn’t press against anything. His arms still felt wrong, heavy in a way that didn’t fade, the ache settling deeper each time he adjusted his grip, like his muscles were being quietly fined. Alicia sat on the floor near his feet, knees drawn up, watching the narrow window in the door with the patient intensity of someone tracking movement even when there was none—because in a place like this, “none” didn’t mean safe.

People passed in the hallway beyond the glass, moving with purpose and speed, most of them carrying green slips of paper instead of blue, their voices low and relieved as they went. Once, someone laughed, and the sound carried just far enough into the room to feel misplaced before it disappeared again.

When the woman with the clipboard entered, the room stilled almost immediately.

“Okay,” she said, scanning the space without looking at anyone in particular. “We’re going to clear a few families through so we can keep things moving.”

Robert straightened slightly, instinctive and useless.

“If your situation has changed,” she continued, “or if you feel you were categorized incorrectly, now’s the time to tell us.”

The words settled into the room and stayed there.

Across from him, a man stood and gestured toward his son, who rose quickly and put his weight down with a visible wince that he immediately smoothed away. The boy took two careful steps, then a third, his face tightening before his father’s hand closed around his shoulder in encouragement.

“He twisted it earlier,” the man said easily, already smiling. “But he’s fine now. Walk it off, right?”

The woman watched for a moment longer than was comfortable, then nodded once.

“Green,” she said.

They were gone almost immediately, the door closing softly behind them.

The absence they left behind felt heavier than their presence had been.

Samantha shifted against Robert’s chest. “He was hurt too,” she said quietly.

“I know,” Robert replied, his eyes still on the door.

“But they let him go.”

“Yes.”

Alicia looked up at him, her brow drawn together. “Why did they get to leave?”

Robert didn’t answer because the answer had already begun to form, unwelcome and precise.

The clipboard woman returned to the doorway, her pen hovering.

“Last check,” she said. “After this, we’ll finalize groupings.” Like they were closing down a register and all the customers had to go somewhere.

Robert looked down at Samantha’s ankle. The swelling hadn’t gone down at all. If anything, it had crept higher, the wrap stretched tight and uneven, the skin beneath it flushed and angry. Samantha noticed his gaze and shook her head almost imperceptibly, her fingers tightening in his shirt.

“I can’t,” she whispered.

He knew that.

He also knew something else, ugly and immediate: nobody in this room got points for being honest.

He also knew that the question wasn’t whether she could walk.

It was whether he could say she could and live with himself afterward.

When the woman’s eyes met his, he heard himself speak before he could stop it.

“She can walk,” Robert said. “She just twisted it. She’s sore, but she can manage.”

The lie landed softly, shaped exactly like the truth would have, and he hated how easy that was.

The woman glanced down at Samantha’s ankle, then at Robert’s face, her expression unreadable.

“Can you walk for me, sweetheart?” she asked.

Samantha hesitated for half a second too long.

Robert tightened his arm around her, just slightly. “She can,” he said, firmer now. “She gets dramatic. But she can.”

The woman nodded.

“All right,” she said. “Let’s move you over.”

As Robert stood, the world shifted.

Text settled into place in the air in front of him, steady and indifferent.

**STATUS UPDATE RECORDED**  
**Trait Affected: Compliance**  
**Condition Applied: Efficient**

Robert’s breath caught.

The words didn’t flicker or glow. They simply existed.

“Dad?” Alicia said.

The text vanished.

They moved faster after that.

Doors opened sooner. Instructions shortened. Within minutes they were standing with families holding green slips, no one asking them to wait, no one looking twice at Samantha’s careful, uneven steps.

Outside, the air felt different.

Alicia looked up at Robert. “Why did that work?”

Robert didn’t answer.

He was still thinking about the word **Efficient**, and how easily it had appeared.

# Chapter 6: The Green Room

Robert expected “green” to mean _out_. Home. A couch. A door with a lock he controlled.

Instead, “green” meant another room.

They were funneled into it with the same soft voices and the same hands that never quite pushed and never quite let you forget they could. The room was bigger than the classroom—cafeteria, maybe, or a multipurpose room that smelled faintly of mop water and old pizza even when there was no pizza. Folding tables had been shoved to the walls. Chairs were set in neat little islands, like whoever arranged them had been trying to pretend this wasn’t a holding pen.

People talked in that brittle, whispery way people talked at wakes and in hospital waiting rooms. Too polite. Too careful. Like the room itself might take offense.

Samantha leaned into Robert, her ankle throbbing against his foot. Every time she adjusted, his arms flared with that wrong heaviness, as if the clinic had quietly added sand to his bones.

“We’re leaving now, right?” she asked, hopeful in the way that made him want to break something.

“Soon,” Robert lied, because he’d been doing a lot of that lately and it kept getting rewarded.

Alicia sat with her backpack upright between her feet, hands folded like she was in church. Her eyes kept ticking around the room, landing on staff, landing on doorways, landing on empty patches of air as if there were things there worth reading.

Robert tried not to watch her. He watched her anyway.

A man two chairs over muttered, “This is some bullshit,” and his wife hissed at him like profanity would summon a staff member the way saying Bloody Mary summoned a ghost.

Robert almost laughed. It came out as a cough.

Across the room, a staff member with a tablet paused beside an old man and murmured something. The old man nodded too quickly. When the staff member moved on, the old man’s shoulders dropped like he’d just been spared. Nobody congratulated him. Nobody asked why.

Robert looked at the doors. There were three. Only one was being used. The others might as well have been painted on.

His phone buzzed against his thigh—phantom vibration, maybe, or maybe not. He pulled it out and saw signal bars.

Not full. Not even good. But there. Present. A little green miracle.

His thumb hovered over the screen, the old habit rising like a prayer: call Anne. Text. Say _are you seeing this_.

Beside him, Alicia whispered without moving her mouth. “Don’t.”

The word was so quiet it could have been his own thought.

Robert stared at her.

Alicia didn’t look back. She kept her eyes on the aisle and swallowed once, like she was trying to swallow down a warning.

Robert slid the phone back into his pocket.

The room hummed. The lights hummed. The building hummed like it was digesting them.

Somewhere nearby, a child started crying and got shushed fast, like that sound was illegal now.

Then a voice—calm, practiced—called from the doorway, “We need everyone to remain seated until verification clears.”

Nobody asked what verification was.

Robert hated them all for that, including himself.

# Chapter 7: Verification Pending

Robert waited until the room settled.

It took longer than he expected. People kept shifting, standing and sitting again, moving bags closer to their feet, glancing at doors that didn’t open. Every few minutes someone would rise as if they’d missed an instruction, then sit back down when nothing happened. The noise never fully disappeared; it just softened into a constant, uneasy murmur.

When no one was looking directly at him, Robert eased his phone out of his pocket and turned slightly away from the others.

The screen lit up.

Signal bars were there. Fewer than usual, but present. Enough to pretend this should work.

He didn’t open his contacts. He didn’t need to. He dialed from memory, the sequence of numbers so familiar his thumb moved without hesitation. He had called it hundreds of times—standing in grocery store aisles, pacing hospital corridors, leaning against his car in parking lots while the girls slept in the back seat.

The phone rang.

Once.  
Twice.

He let himself breathe when it connected.

“Hello?” a woman said.

There was road noise behind her, the muted rush of tires on pavement, a turn signal clicking close to the receiver.

“Hey,” Robert said. “It’s Rob. It’s—” He swallowed. “It’s me.”

A pause.

“Who is this?”

He frowned. “Robert. Robert Hardy.”

Another pause. Longer this time.

“I think you’ve got the wrong number,” the woman said.

“No,” Robert said. “No, I don’t. Sorry—can you put me on with Anne?”

“I don’t know anyone named Robert,” she said. Not unkindly. Just certain. “Who are you trying to reach?”

His throat tightened. “This is Anne’s phone.”

Silence. Then a short laugh. “No, it isn’t.”

“Yes, it is,” he said, sharper than he meant to. “I’ve had this number for years. My kids have this number memorized.”

“Well,” she said, “you don’t have it now.”

Robert looked down at the screen. The number glowed there exactly as it always had. Same digits. Same spacing. No roaming indicator. No warning.

“Who am I talking to?” he asked, and heard how thin he sounded.

“Melissa,” she said. “And I don’t know an Anne. Or a Robert.”

A horn blared behind her.

“Look,” she continued, impatience creeping in, “I’m driving. You should check the number.”

“I did,” he said. “I did, okay?”

She exhaled. “Then I don’t know what to tell you.”

The line went dead.

Robert stood there for a moment longer than he meant to, phone still pressed to his ear. When he lowered it, the screen had already gone dark.

Alicia looked up at him from her chair.

“Did you get her?” she asked.

“No,” Robert said.

He didn’t try again.

He slid the phone back into his pocket and sat down, the chair creaking softly under his weight. His heart was still beating too fast, but there was no clean place for the panic to go. Nothing dramatic had happened. Nothing he could point to.

Just a call that had gone somewhere else, like the whole world had been forwarded and nobody had bothered to tell him.

Across the room, a man near the wall was arguing quietly with his phone, thumb jabbing at the screen. “I’m telling you,” he said to no one in particular, “it rang. Someone picked up. Said they’ve had the number for years.”

A woman beside him shook her head. “Same. Whoever answered mine asked how I even got it.”

Someone laughed softly, then stopped when no one joined in.

Robert watched the staff at the far end of the room. They weren’t panicking. They weren’t huddled together. They moved with the same practiced efficiency as before, checking slips, murmuring to one another, redirecting anyone who stood too long without purpose.

LIMIT 2 PER ITEM

The letters were thick and shaky, as if the person who’d written them had pressed too hard.

Alicia didn’t stop at the sign.

The sidewalk curved around the side of the building toward the loading dock. The air smelled different here—wet cardboard, old produce, something sour that had been left too long. A dumpster sat with its lid propped open. Flies hovered in a loose cloud.

A truck was backed up to the dock.

Its doors were shut.

Its engine was off.

It looked like it had arrived and then been forgotten.

Alicia stopped.

Robert stopped behind her.

Neither of them spoke.

A man came around the corner carrying a clipboard. He wore a reflective vest. He slowed when he saw the truck, flipped through the pages, frowned. He took out his phone and listened, turning in a slow circle as if the answer might appear behind him.

He lowered the phone and wrote something on the clipboard.

When he noticed them, his eyes went to Robert first. “Can I help you?” he asked.

Robert stepped forward. “My daughter—” He stopped and looked at Alicia. “We’re fine,” he said instead.

A soft metallic click came from the truck.

Robert’s head snapped toward it. He took a step closer to the dock.

Alicia said, “Dad.”

The word was quiet. It landed.

The sound didn’t come again.

Alicia waited one more beat and then turned away.

She walked past the dumpsters, past a woman digging through one with gloved hands, lifting bags and setting them aside. The woman didn’t look up. She moved like someone doing a chore.

Alicia kept walking.

Robert followed, still on foot, still close, still not shaping her direction.

By the time they reached the next street, Alicia noticed that her breathing felt easier. The space inside her chest didn’t tighten the way it had earlier. She blinked once and caught a brief shimmer at the edge of her vision, like the afterimage of looking at something bright.

She didn’t stop.

She didn’t turn.

She kept walking toward town, and the steps came more easily than they had a block ago.

# Chapter 34 — At the Intersection

Alicia was ahead of them, far enough that Robert had to keep his eyes on her to make sure she didn’t turn off somewhere without him noticing. She moved easily through the sidewalk traffic, slipping between people without stopping, her head angled forward as if she already knew which gaps would open. Robert stayed a few steps back with Samantha, his hand hovering near her shoulder when the crowd pressed too close, dropping again when it didn’t, and the street grew louder as they approached the intersection.

Engines idled. Someone leaned on a horn and then let off, embarrassed by it. A delivery van sat half into the crosswalk with its blinker ticking steadily, the sound cutting through the low murmur of voices and movement. People stood at the corners in loose clumps, some facing the street, some turned sideways, watching things that weren’t happening. Alicia slowed near the curb and Robert slowed too, the light changing without anyone moving right away.

That was when Robert heard the voice, not loud enough to rise above the noise, just threaded through it. “Hey,” it said, followed by a pause that made it hard to tell if the word had been meant to go anywhere else. “Can you—”

Robert turned his head toward the sound. It came from the far side of the intersection, near the curb where the pavement dipped and water sometimes collected after rain. A man sat on the ground there with his back against the pole of a street sign, one leg bent awkwardly beneath him, one hand resting on the asphalt as if he’d put it there to steady himself and then forgotten about it. Cars idled around him. People stood close enough that Robert could see their faces clearly, a woman with a stroller caught mid-step at the edge of the crosswalk, a man in a work jacket leaning against a parking meter with his arms folded, watching without expression.

“Hey,” the voice said again, softer this time. “I just— I can’t—”

Alicia stepped off the curb and Robert felt the shift immediately, moving after her with his attention narrowing. Samantha stayed close, her limp more pronounced now that they’d slowed. Alicia crossed halfway into the street and stopped, standing there with her weight settled evenly as she looked toward the man on the ground, waiting in a way that made Robert uneasy.

Robert stepped past her without meaning to. “Sir,” he said, raising his voice just enough to carry. “Are you hurt?” The man lifted his head with visible effort, his eyes focusing on Robert slowly. “I fell,” he said, then stopped, swallowing. “I just need a minute,” he added, though it sounded like a question.

Robert took another step into the street as a car crept forward and stopped again, the driver’s hands tight on the wheel. Someone farther back leaned on a horn, short and sharp, then cut it off. A woman near Robert shook her head without looking at him. “They already checked,” she said.
“Who did?” Robert asked.
She shrugged. “Someone.”

A man near the curb cleared his throat. “He said he was fine.”
The man on the ground didn’t say anything.

Robert turned back to him. “Can you stand?” he asked. “Do you want me to call an ambulance?” The man’s gaze drifted past Robert to the stopped cars and watching faces. “I don’t want trouble,” he said, and Robert felt heat rise in his chest.

“This isn’t trouble,” he said. “You fell in the street.”

Behind him, Alicia shifted her weight. Robert glanced back automatically, checking her position, and saw her standing just behind the crosswalk line now, her attention still on the man, her expression unchanged.

A woman near the stroller leaned toward Robert slightly. “You shouldn’t interfere,” she said quietly, as if offering advice. “It just makes things worse.”
“He’s sitting in traffic,” Robert said.
“It’s being handled,” she replied.
“By who?”
No one answered.

The light changed again. Engines revved and idled as drivers decided what to do, one car easing forward and stopping when the man didn’t move. Robert crouched in front of him, lowering his voice. “Sir, can you tell me your name?” The man blinked slowly and let his head tip back against the signpost. “I’m okay,” he said, the words thin.

Someone behind Robert sighed, loud and pointed. “Please don’t,” a voice said. “You’re making everyone late.” Robert stood and turned toward the sound. “This will take two minutes,” he said. “I just need someone to call—”
“I already did,” someone else said.
“When?”
There was a pause.
“Earlier.”

Faces slid away from his gaze. Someone checked a phone. Someone adjusted a bag strap. The stroller rocked gently as its owner shifted her weight. The man on the ground closed his eyes.

Traffic began to move, not all at once, but one car at a time, easing around the curb and giving the fallen man a wide berth without stopping. The delivery van lurched forward, its blinker still ticking. Robert stepped back instinctively as a car rolled past too close. “Wait,” he said. “Just wait.” No one did.

The intersection loosened and dissolved, cars clearing and pedestrians finding paths through the gaps, and the man on the ground grew smaller, partially obscured by movement. Robert turned, looking for Alicia.

She wasn’t at the curb.

He scanned the sidewalk, his eyes jumping ahead automatically, searching for the familiar outline of her shoulders, the way she moved through space. She wasn’t there either. “Alicia,” he said, louder now.

Samantha tugged at his sleeve. “Dad.”
He looked down at her. “Did you see where she went?”
She shook her head. “I thought she was with you.”

Robert turned back toward the intersection. The man on the ground was gone. He didn’t see him stand. He didn’t see anyone help him up. One moment the space had been occupied, the next it was just pavement and the darkened outline where someone had been sitting.

The light changed again. Cars moved. People crossed. The intersection returned to itself.

Robert stood in the middle of it with Samantha’s hand in his and no sense of when he’d lost the other one. No one looked at him. No one asked if everything was okay. Traffic flowed on.

# Chapter 35 — Still Ahead

Robert kept moving because stopping felt like a decision, and he wasn’t ready to make one yet.

Alicia had been ahead of them at the intersection, close enough that he could see her without looking hard, and even after the crowd thinned and the street opened up again, he still found himself tracking that space in front of him, the place where she should reappear once sightlines cleared. He walked with his eyes forward, not scanning the sidewalks so much as waiting for her to resolve out of the movement, the way people did when you caught up to them after a pause.

Samantha limped beside him, her steps shorter now, her weight favoring one side. Robert noticed it the way you noticed weather changing, vaguely at first, as something you planned to deal with later. He kept his pace steady, resisting the urge to slow, telling himself they’d adjust once they were past the busier blocks.

A few people passed them going the other direction. A man nodded at Robert and kept walking. A woman stepped aside to let them through, her eyes flicking to Samantha’s ankle and then away again. Robert took that as confirmation that things were still visible, still legible, that if Alicia had been nearby someone would have looked past him toward her.

At the next corner, he thought he saw her turn.

It was just a flash of movement at the edge of his vision, the angle of a shoulder disappearing down the side street, and he veered without thinking, pulling Samantha gently with him before checking himself. The side street was shorter than he’d expected, lined with parked cars and a row of trash bins pulled up against the curb. No one walked there. No one stood at the end waiting.

Robert stopped and looked back toward the main road.

Alicia wasn’t there either.

He felt a tightening in his chest, brief and sharp, and then he pushed it down, telling himself she’d crossed ahead while he’d been distracted, that she’d doubled back the way she sometimes did, impatient with his pace. He turned them back toward the main street, scanning now, counting steps without realizing he was doing it.

Samantha stumbled.

He caught her automatically, his hand closing around her arm before she could fall. She leaned into him, breathing harder than she had been, her face pale in a way that hadn’t been there earlier.

“Just a second,” she said.

Robert nodded, already looking ahead again. “We’re almost there,” he said, though he didn’t know what *there* meant anymore. He shifted his grip on her arm and took another step.

She didn’t follow.

Her foot came down wrong, her ankle turning inward slightly before she yanked it back, a sharp sound escaping her before she could stop it. She grabbed at his jacket and bent forward, her weight sagging.

“Okay,” Robert said quickly. “Okay. Stop. Stop.”

They stood there, half in the shadow of a building, half in the sun, people moving around them without slowing. Samantha pressed her hand against her ankle and shook her head.

“I can’t,” she said. “It won’t—”

Robert crouched in front of her, his focus narrowing now whether he wanted it to or not. Her ankle was swollen now, the skin tight enough that the shape of it looked wrong, and when he touched it she flinched hard enough that he pulled his hand back.

“We’ll get you to the car,” he said. “I’ve got you.”

He straightened and slid his arm under her knees, lifting her before she could argue. She wrapped her arms around his neck instinctively, her face pressing into his shoulder, and the sudden weight shifted everything at once. His balance adjusted. His breathing changed. The street seemed farther away, the edges of his vision dulling as he focused on keeping them upright.

As he took his first step, something flickered across his vision.

Not outside him. Inside.

A translucent block of text hovered briefly at the edge of his sight, pale and steady, like an afterimage that didn’t belong to any light source around him.

**LOAD EXCEEDS OPTIMAL THRESHOLD**  
**INJURY RISK: ELEVATED**

It vanished almost immediately.

Robert stopped short, his foot half raised, his heart thudding hard enough that he felt it in his throat. He shifted Samantha’s weight and looked around, expecting to see someone watching him, some sign that the moment had been visible.

No one was paying attention.

“Dad?” Samantha said quietly.

“I’m fine,” he said, too quickly. He adjusted his grip and kept walking, his jaw set, his attention fixed on the line of parked cars ahead. His back twinged as he stepped off the curb, a familiar warning he ignored.

The car was where he’d left it.

He crossed the last stretch of pavement with care, each step measured now, the earlier urgency replaced by the simple math of balance and distance. When he reached the driver’s side, he turned and lowered Samantha onto the seat, guiding her gently so her ankle didn’t knock against the door frame.

She leaned back, closing her eyes.

Robert straightened and looked up automatically.

The street behind them stretched back toward the intersection. A man crossed with his phone held up in front of his face. Someone bent to adjust a shopping bag that had split at the bottom, cans rolling just far enough that another person nudged one back with their shoe. A car pulled out from the curb and eased into traffic.

No one was looking toward him.

He waited a moment longer, scanning the sidewalk, the corners, the spaces between parked cars where Alicia should have appeared by now, where she always appeared.

She didn’t.

Robert rested his hands on the roof of the car and breathed until the tightness in his chest loosened enough to ignore. He told himself she was ahead, that she’d kept going, that she’d circle back once she realized he wasn’t behind her anymore.

He opened the driver’s door.

# Chapter 36 — What He Had

Robert carried Samantha inside without turning on any more lights than he needed. The house looked the way it always did at this hour, furniture in its places, the faint smell of whatever they’d last cooked lingering in the air. Nothing had shifted while they were gone. That felt stranger than it should have.

He set Samantha down on the couch and knelt in front of her, easing her shoe off carefully. The ankle was swollen, the skin stretched and shiny, and she sucked in a breath when he touched it, then let it out again, jaw set.

“I know,” he said. “I know.”

He fetched the ace bandage from the bag they’d brought back from the pharmacy and unrolled it slowly, checking the tension with his fingers before he started. He wrapped from the foot up, firm but not tight, the motion practiced enough that he didn’t have to think about it. Samantha watched his hands, her shoulders loosening as the pressure settled into something manageable.

“There,” he said when he was done.

She flexed her foot slightly and nodded. “That’s better.”

“Good,” he said, and meant more than the word covered.

He went into the kitchen and started pulling things out of the cabinets without stopping to take stock. Pasta, a jar of sauce that was already open, a can of vegetables he set aside and then put back. He filled a pot with water and turned on the burner, the sound of it grounding in a way he hadn’t realized he needed.

Samantha came in and sat at the table, her leg stretched out carefully in front of her. She rested her chin in her hand and watched him move.

“Is Alicia home?” she asked, like she was asking about something ordinary.

“Not yet,” Robert said. “She’s probably ahead of us. She does that.”

Samantha nodded, accepting it, and didn’t ask anything else.

The water boiled. He stirred the pasta and lowered the heat, moving on autopilot. When it was ready, he portioned it out onto two plates, adding sauce without measuring, just enough to cover. He carried the plates to the table and sat across from Samantha, the chair scraping softly against the floor.

They ate quietly. The food was hot and plain and filling enough. Robert noticed the empty space on the table where a third plate would usually go and looked away from it without making a thing of it.

Halfway through the meal, there was a knock at the door.

Not loud. Not tentative. Just a knock, the kind that assumed it would be answered.

Robert paused with his fork halfway to his mouth. He waited a beat, listening, then set the fork down and stood. He glanced at Samantha.

“Stay here,” he said.

She nodded, already focused back on her plate.

At the door, he hesitated long enough to know what that meant and then opened it anyway.

A man stood on the porch, about Robert’s age, hands shoved into the pockets of his jacket. He smiled when Robert opened the door, a quick, practiced thing that didn’t quite settle.

“Hey,” the man said. “Sorry to bother you.”

“That’s okay,” Robert said.

The man shifted his weight. “I saw you come in earlier,” he said. “Thought I’d ask.”

“Ask what?” Robert said, though he already knew.

The man cleared his throat. “We’re running low,” he said. “The store was pretty picked over, and they’re saying deliveries might not be back on schedule yet. I figured I’d see if you had anything to spare. Just for tonight.”

Robert looked past him, down the street. A porch light flicked on two houses down. Somewhere, a door closed.

“How many?” Robert asked.

“Just me and my wife,” the man said quickly. “We’re not asking for much.”

Robert nodded once and stepped back into the house, leaving the door open. He went into the kitchen and opened the pantry, taking in what was there without counting it. He grabbed the can he’d put back earlier and one of the unopened boxes of pasta, hesitated, then added the jar of sauce he hadn’t used.

When he returned, the man’s shoulders relaxed visibly.

“Thank you,” he said, taking the items with both hands. “Thank you so much. I’ll pay you back.”

“That’s fine,” Robert said.

The man nodded too many times. “People will remember this,” he said, like it was reassurance.

Robert didn’t answer.

He closed the door and leaned his forehead against it for a moment before turning back toward the kitchen.

Samantha was still at the table. She looked up at him as he came back in, then down at her plate, then back up again.

“Everything okay?” she asked.

“Yeah,” Robert said. “Eat.”

She did.

Robert sat down and finished what was left on his plate. The food tasted the same. The portions were smaller than they had been a few minutes ago, but no one commented on it. When they were done, he gathered the dishes and set them in the sink, rinsing them one at a time.

Outside, another porch light came on.

Robert turned off the kitchen light and went back to the living room, already planning what he’d do next, the shape of the evening narrowing to what he could manage.

He did not look at the door again.

# Chapter 37 — Directions

Anne left work on time.

She checked the clock in her car as she pulled onto the road and did the math without thinking. If traffic cooperated, she’d be early. She’d text Robert when she was ten minutes out, like she always did.

The radio was on low, the same station she listened to most evenings, the hosts talking about weather farther west than she cared about. The route was familiar enough that she didn’t need directions, but she entered the town name into her phone anyway and set it in the cup holder.

The line on the screen hesitated.

It recalculated once, then again, the blue dot sliding forward while the map shifted underneath it. The route it offered was longer than she expected, looping her onto a state road she didn’t usually take. She followed it, letting the car settle into the drive.

After a while, the landmarks didn’t line up.

A diner she remembered seeing on this stretch wasn’t there. The feed store on the corner had been replaced by an empty lot with tall grass pushing through the gravel. She slowed, glancing at the screen again, and watched the route redraw itself with quiet insistence.

She took the next turn and then the one after that, the road narrowing as it went, trees crowding closer than she liked. When the phone chimed to reroute again, she sighed and turned it off, setting it face down in the console.

She tried calling Robert.

The line clicked and then went straight to a busy signal, the old kind, sharp and steady. She frowned and hung up, waiting a few seconds before trying again. The same sound met her ear.

She slowed at a stop sign and checked the number, even though she knew it was right.

Busy again.

She drove another mile before pulling into a gas station set back from the road, its lights bright against the darkening sky. Inside, a man stood behind the counter scrolling on his phone. She waited until he looked up, then leaned forward slightly.

“Hi,” she said. “I’m trying to get to—” She named the town, saying it the way she always did, with the emphasis she’d learned from years of correcting people.

The man blinked. “Where?”

She repeated it.

He shook his head once. “Never heard of it.”

“It’s not far,” she said, already reaching for her phone. “It should be around here somewhere.”

He shrugged. “Could be unincorporated,” he said. “Lots of places don’t show up.”

She thanked him and stepped back outside, dialing Robert again as she walked. The busy signal answered her before the first ring.

She stood by her car for a moment, listening to it, then ended the call and got back in.

The road carried her farther than she meant to go. The light faded completely, the sky settling into a flat dark without stars. She passed another sign for the same highway she thought she’d already crossed and slowed, checking her mirrors, her speed, the clock.

Everything else behaved the way it should.

She turned around at the next wide shoulder and headed back the way she’d come.

The town did not appear.

By the time she found the hotel, the sign was already lit. She parked near the entrance and sat in the car for a moment before getting out, her phone in her hand. She tried Robert again. The line stayed busy.

At the front desk, she asked for a room and took the key without comment. Upstairs, she set her bag down and sat on the edge of the bed, scrolling through the map on her phone until it dimmed and went dark.

She set an alarm for early morning.

She would try again then.

# Chapter 38 — What People Do

There were voices outside, not raised exactly and not urgent either, just present in a way they hadn’t been earlier, the sound of people lingering and talking past the point where they normally would have gone back inside. Robert stood in the kitchen longer than he needed to, listening without quite meaning to, trying to decide whether it was anything or just one of those evenings when no one seemed in a hurry.

He crossed the living room and looked out through the front window. A couple of people stood near the curb, talking with their hands in their pockets. Someone else leaned against a car that was parked too close to the corner, the engine still running, the headlights washing the side of a house that didn’t need the light. No one seemed to be in charge of it, but no one was leaving either.

Samantha sat on the couch with her ankle propped up, the television on and muted. She watched him the way she had been watching him all evening, quietly, like she was keeping track of where he was in the room in case she needed him again.

The knock came while he was still standing there.

When he opened the door, it was a man from a few houses down, someone Robert recognized but had never really talked to beyond a wave or a comment about the weather. He smiled right away, easy and familiar, like this was a continuation of something Robert had missed.

“They’re getting everyone together,” the man said.

Robert stayed where he was. “About what?”

The man shifted his weight and glanced back toward the street. “Just talking things through,” he said, already sounding like he didn’t expect that answer to matter much.

Robert looked back into the house. Samantha had pushed herself a little more upright on the couch, one hand braced beside her, careful with her foot. She was watching them now.

“I’ve got my kid,” Robert said.

“Yeah,” the man said quickly. “Of course. She can stay here.” He hesitated, then added, almost as an afterthought, “People will expect you, though.”

Robert stepped out onto the porch and pulled the door mostly closed behind him, not locking it, just leaving it the way it fell. The man turned and started walking toward the street without checking to see if Robert was coming.

Robert followed.

Out there, it didn’t feel like a meeting yet, but it didn’t feel casual either. People stood closer together than they normally did, conversations bending inward as new voices joined them. Someone laughed and then stopped. A woman said something about keeping things fair, and another person nodded like they’d already been thinking the same thing.

No one asked Robert anything when he reached the edge of it. Someone shifted to make space for him without saying so. He stood there longer than he meant to, listening as ideas surfaced and drifted, never quite landing but never going away either.

“We’re just trying to get a sense of what everyone has,” a woman said from somewhere near the middle. She spoke calmly, like she was explaining a process she’d used before. “Nothing specific. Just generally.”

“Until things get sorted out,” someone said behind Robert.

“Deliveries will resume,” a man said, with a confidence that sounded practiced.

“For now,” another voice added, and then again a few seconds later from somewhere else, the words repeating until they started to sound like agreement.

People nodded. Someone murmured something about pooling. Someone else mentioned rotations, maybe lists, just so things stayed fair. No one pushed back. No one formally agreed either. The ideas seemed to settle on their own, as if they’d been waiting for the right moment to appear.

Robert cleared his throat, surprised at how loud it sounded.

“I don’t have much,” he said. “And my daughter’s hurt.” He paused there, the rest of the sentence not lining up the way he expected it to. “We’re managing.”

A woman near him nodded sympathetically. “Everyone’s got something.”

“That’s why this helps,” a man said. “So nobody has to carry it alone.”

Someone said Robert’s name, not loudly, but clearly enough that he knew it was meant for him. Someone else repeated it, like confirmation. A hand clapped his shoulder in passing, brief and familiar, as if the contact had already been earned.

He waited for someone to ask him a question, or for the conversation to turn toward an actual decision, but it didn’t. It kept moving instead, reshaping itself around what had already been said. By the time he realized that silence was being taken for agreement, it felt too late to interrupt without sounding like he was objecting to something everyone else had already accepted.

Eventually, someone brought their hands together lightly and said, “This is a good start.”

That seemed to be enough.

People began to drift away in small groups, conversations continuing as they walked back toward their houses, plans implied but not written down. Doors opened. Lights shifted. The street didn’t empty so much as rearrange itself, settling into a new shape that felt temporary and permanent at the same time.

Robert turned back toward his porch.

Samantha sat just inside the doorway now, the door cracked open, her shoulder leaning against the frame. She watched him approach, her expression careful.

“Everything okay?” she asked.

“I think so,” Robert said.

Behind him, the talking went on, quieter than before but steadier, threaded with the sound of relief. Someone laughed again, the way people did when they believed something difficult had been handled.

Robert stepped inside and closed the door.

The voices carried on through the walls.

# Chapter 39 — Home

Robert was rinsing a plate when he heard the door.

Not a knock this time, not the pause that usually came with it, just the sound of the handle turning and the door opening the way it always had. He shut off the water and stood there, listening as it closed again and footsteps crossed the entryway, familiar enough that his shoulders loosened before he realized they had been tight.

“Dad?”

Her voice sounded like it always had.

He dried his hands on the towel and stepped into the living room. Alicia stood just inside the doorway, her backpack still on one shoulder, her hand resting on the strap as if she hadn’t finished deciding what came next.

“You’re home,” he said, and felt stupid for how much relief there was in it.

“Yes,” Alicia said. “But I can eat again if you want me to.”

“You don’t have to,” he said quickly. “I just meant—” He waved it off. “I’ll make something later.”

Alicia looked toward the window. “I know.”

“They were earlier,” Alicia said. She shrugged, small and unbothered. “It was loud.”

Alicia thought about it. Not long. Just enough.

“Helping,” she said. “Mostly just staying where they needed me.”

She shrugged again. “I don’t know. Watching, I guess.”

Robert nodded, even though that didn’t explain anything. He told himself it didn’t have to yet. She was home. That was what mattered.

“Go get ready for bed,” he said. “We’ll talk more tomorrow.”

Samantha pulled the blanket higher around her leg.

“She’s different,” she said quietly.

Robert gathered the dishes from the table and carried them to the sink, rinsing them one at a time.

“She’s tired,” he said finally. “It’s been a long day.”

Samantha didn’t respond.

Later, when the house had settled and the lights were off, Robert lay on his back with his hands folded over his chest, staring up at the ceiling where the light from the street bent into a faint, uneven shape. He listened to the house settle, the small sounds it made when no one was moving through it, the familiar creaks and clicks that usually meant the night had taken over.

He waited for sleep to do what it always did.

When it didn’t, he rolled onto his side and pulled the blanket higher, then kicked it down again when it felt too heavy. He told himself it was just adrenaline, that the day had been long, that relief took time to burn off. Alicia was home. Samantha was resting. The doors were locked.

He pictured Alicia the way she’d looked standing in the doorway, backpack still on her shoulder, and then the way she’d set it down so carefully, like she’d been taught where it went. He tried to remember if she’d always done that.

He couldn’t be sure.

He turned onto his other side and listened again, counting breaths without meaning to. Down the hall, a floorboard creaked softly and he held his breath, waiting for footsteps that didn’t come. He thought of getting up, of checking on her, then imagined what that would look like if she woke and saw him standing there.

He stayed where he was.

His mind kept circling back to the same small details, the ones that hadn’t seemed important at the time. The way she’d washed her hands. The way she’d sat down and waited. The way she hadn’t asked anything at all.

He told himself that tired kids did that sometimes. That good kids did that.

When he finally closed his eyes, the house felt too quiet, like it was waiting for something else to settle before it would let him rest. He drifted for a few minutes at a time, never deep enough to lose track of where he was, waking again with the same thought unfinished.

Sometime later, he heard voices outside.

Not close. Not loud. Just present enough to remind him they were still there.

Robert lay awake until the light from the street shifted and the shape on the ceiling changed, and even then, when sleep finally took him, it felt temporary, like something he’d borrowed rather than earned.

# Chapter 40 — Morning

Robert woke to voices that didn’t resolve.

They weren’t shouting, not at first, but they were loud enough to overlap, loud enough that he couldn’t tell where one person stopped and another began. The sound pressed in through the walls, uneven and persistent, like it had already been going on for a while and had no intention of stopping just because he was awake now.

He lay still, listening.

The voices rose and fell, sometimes sharp, sometimes almost conversational, the same phrases surfacing again and again in different tones. He couldn’t make out what anyone was actually saying. It was the repetition that stuck with him, the sense that whatever decision was being argued had already passed the point where it could be undone.

He pushed himself up and sat on the edge of the bed.

From down the hall came the quiet sound of movement. Not hurried. Not surprised. Drawers opening. The soft rasp of fabric. A zipper pulled smooth and sure.

“Alicia,” he called, already standing.

She was in the hallway when he stepped out, jacket on, shoes in her hands. She looked awake in a way he wasn’t, her attention already oriented outward, toward the sound outside the house.

“What’s going on?” he asked.

“They’re talking,” she said.

“About what?”

She slipped one shoe on, then the other. “It keeps changing.”

“That doesn’t answer anything.”

She glanced at him, just briefly. “It doesn’t need to.”

The voices outside swelled, someone raising their voice enough that it cut through the rest before dissolving back into the noise.

“Stay here,” Robert said. “Just for a minute.”

She nodded. “Okay.”

And then she picked up her jacket.

He followed her toward the front of the house, irritation sharpening into something tighter as he tried to keep pace without turning it into a chase.

“You don’t have to go out there,” he said. “Whatever it is, it can wait.”

She stopped at the door and turned to face him fully for the first time.

“I’m needed,” she said.

The words weren’t defensive. They weren’t emotional. They landed the way a fact did, something she’d already accepted and didn’t expect him to argue with.

“No,” he said. “You’re not.”

She reached for the handle anyway.

Robert stepped forward and caught her arm.

She didn’t jerk away. She didn’t tense. She just continued moving, and his grip slid down from her sleeve to her wrist and then to nothing as she stepped out of reach.

“Dad,” she said, not unkindly. “You can’t.”

The door opened.

Cold air rushed in, carrying the sound of the street with it. The voices were louder now, closer, threaded with footsteps and the low rumble of engines idling where they shouldn’t have been.

From behind him came a small, sharp sound.

“Dad?”

He turned.

Samantha stood in the hallway doorway, one hand braced against the wall, her face drawn tight as she tried to put weight on her bad ankle. She took a step, faltered, and sucked in a breath as the pain caught her.

“I can’t,” she said. “It hurts.”

Robert moved toward her without thinking, one hand already out.

Behind him, the door creaked wider.

He steadied Samantha, feeling her weight lean into him, her fingers digging into his arm as she tried to stay upright.

“I’ve got you,” he said, though his eyes were already pulling back toward the door.

Alicia stood on the porch, her back to them now, the crowd in the street shifting subtly to make space. She was already walking, her pace unhurried, confident, as if the path had been cleared before she reached it.

“Wait,” Robert said.

He let go of Samantha long enough to take a step after her.

Just one.

Then Samantha made another small, involuntary sound behind him, her weight shifting again, unstable.

Robert stopped.

By the time he reached the porch, Alicia was halfway down the block. People moved around her without touching, parting and closing again as she passed. No one tried to stop her. No one needed to.

She didn’t look back.

Robert stood there barefoot on the cold concrete, the sound of the street filling his ears, the house open behind him and his daughter disappearing ahead of him, and felt the space between those two things stretch wider than he could cross.

He turned back inside.

# Chapter 41 — Procedures

The voices were still out there.

They hadn’t stopped after Alicia left. If anything they’d gotten louder, not in a steady climb, more in waves, as if people kept trying to calm down and failing, then remembering what they were upset about and starting again. Robert stood in the living room long enough to hear the same tone rise twice from different mouths, that particular edge people got when they weren’t yelling but were close to it.

Samantha shifted on the couch. Her ankle was propped on a pillow and the blanket was pulled up to her waist, but her eyes kept going to the window as if she could see sound.

“She’s gone,” Sam said.

“I know,” Robert said.

He didn’t add anything else, because he didn’t have anything that didn’t sound like a lie.

“I’m going to see,” he said, already moving.

Sam nodded, but she didn’t look convinced. She watched him the way she’d been watching him since the clinic, like she was counting how many times he left the room and came back.

“I’ll be right outside,” he told her. “If you need me, you yell.”

Sam made a face at that, the kind that said she didn’t want to yell for anything, but she nodded anyway.

Robert stepped onto the porch and stopped.

The street was full in a way it hadn’t been the day before. Not packed exactly. No one was shoulder-to-shoulder. But there were people everywhere you didn’t normally see people—standing in the street itself, half in driveways, gathered around open car doors with engines running. Someone had parked too close to the corner and nobody said anything about it. A dog barked from a yard and kept barking, like it had figured out that the usual rules didn’t apply.

Robert walked toward the clusters, trying to find the center of it, and realized there wasn’t one.

A folding table sat crooked in the street, dragged out at an angle that made it more of a boundary than a tool. A woman stood near it with a notebook open, her pen moving quickly, then stopping as she looked up and tried to track three conversations at once.

“Okay,” she said, too loud, not because she was trying to command attention, but because she’d been drowned out already and didn’t have another way to be heard. “One at a time.”

No one did one at a time.

Food came up first, or maybe it was the first thing Robert caught. He heard deliveries and trucks and two days in the same breath, and then someone saying, “If they don’t come today—”

“They will,” another voice said, immediately, like that was a prayer you said out loud to keep it true.

“Based on what?” someone else asked.

No one answered that part.

People started naming what they had, not because anyone demanded it but because the question kept floating back around. Freezers. Generators. How much formula. How many kids. Who had insulin. Who had a neighbor with a chest freezer full of meat that was going to go bad if the power stayed weird.

The notebook filled unevenly, the list already messy. A name would get written down and then someone would call out another detail and the woman would scratch it into the margin. Robert watched that and felt, stupidly, a small sense of relief that someone was writing anything at all, because writing it down made it feel like a problem you could solve.

Someone glanced at him and then said his name, and it took him a second to realize it was a question.

“What about you, Robert?” the man said. “You’ve got kids.”

“Two,” Robert said. “One’s injured.”

There was a pause, the kind that wasn’t sympathy but calculation.

The woman with the notebook didn’t ask what happened. She just nodded and wrote something down like she already knew where to put him on the page.

“Okay,” she said. “So limited mobility.”

That wasn’t what he’d meant to offer. He’d meant I can’t do what you think I can do, but that wasn’t how it landed. The list moved on without waiting for him to fix it.

Someone said, “We need to decide what’s fair.”

Someone else said, “Fair doesn’t feed people.”

That got a laugh from the wrong person and then it wasn’t funny anymore.

Robert shifted his weight and looked down the street for Alicia without thinking. He didn’t see her. He didn’t even see the shape of her. Just people, moving around each other, talking, and a few faces turned toward the school like they could see it from here.

The heart-attack man showed up at the edge of the crowd like he’d come out to complain about the noise and ended up walking into it.

“What the hell is this?” he said.

A few people went quiet the way you do when you realize you’re looking at someone who shouldn’t be standing there.

“You okay?” a woman asked.

He frowned at her. “Why wouldn’t I be?”

“You collapsed,” someone said, and then another voice added, “Yesterday.”

“No, I didn’t,” he said immediately, like it was ridiculous.

“They took you to the hospital,” a man said.

The heart-attack man laughed once, sharp and offended. “No, they didn’t.”

“You don’t remember the ambulance?” the woman asked.

“I remember going to work,” he said. “Which I’m late for.” He looked around, squinting. “Why’s my truck on Maple?”

“That shop closed,” someone said. “Years ago.”

The heart-attack man stared at him as if he’d just spoken nonsense. “That’s not true.”

A different kind of quiet settled, one that wasn’t agreement but unease.

The notebook stopped moving. The woman holding it looked up and then down again, like writing it down would make it fit.

Someone tried to steer it back. “Okay, okay, food. We’re talking about food.”

“Maybe he should sit,” another person said.

“I don’t need to sit,” the heart-attack man snapped. “I need to get to work.”

“You can’t,” someone said, and then realized how that sounded and softened it. “I mean— you should… just… stay put.”

The man’s face tightened. “Stay put for what? What’s wrong with you people?”

It turned into argument the way a spark turns into flame when the air is dry. Not one argument, several. People trying to solve different problems at the same time, and none of the solutions fitting. Someone said doctor. Someone said there was no doctor. Someone said the clinic had been useless. Someone else said maybe the school had supplies. Someone said the school wasn’t talking to anyone.

Robert stood there watching it fragment, the planning already collapsing into noise, the list on the table suddenly pointless because nobody could agree on what the list was for.

“We need somebody official,” a woman said finally, her voice cutting through because she sounded exhausted. “This can’t just be us.”

“Like who?” someone asked.

“The sheriff,” she said.

That landed, not like an answer, more like a place to throw the problem.

“He won’t do anything,” a man said.

“He has to,” another voice replied. “It’s literally his job.”

People started shifting, some toward the sidewalk, some toward their cars, still arguing as they moved.

Robert felt the change in momentum and had the sudden, clear thought that this wasn’t going to be quick, and Samantha was inside alone on the couch with an ankle that looked worse than it should.

He turned and headed back toward his house.

By the time he got inside, Sam was staring at the door like she’d been waiting for it to open.

“They’re going to the sheriff,” Robert said, already moving for her shoes. “You’re coming with me.”

Sam blinked. “What?”

“I’m not leaving you here,” he said. “Can you get up?”

She tried. Her face tightened immediately. She put weight on the ankle and then snatched it back, breath hissing through her teeth.

“Okay,” Robert said, and didn’t pretend it was okay. He came around her and helped her up, slow and careful. She leaned into him, trying not to, and he could feel how much she was relying on him even when she didn’t want to.

Outside, engines were starting now, one after another, unevenly. Doors slamming. Voices shouting across the street asking who had room, who was going, who was driving.

Robert got Sam down the front steps and into the passenger seat. It took longer than it should have because everything took longer when you were trying not to hurt someone. He shut her door and hurried around to his side, heart thudding in that stupid way it did when you were late for something you hadn’t agreed to.

He started the engine and pulled out as two cars passed in front of him, people inside gesturing wildly as they talked.

Sam watched the movement through the window. “Where are we going?” she asked.

“The sheriff’s,” Robert said.

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, “What happens if he says no?”

Robert didn’t answer.

They arrived in waves, not together. Cars pulled in wherever they could. People spilled out onto the sidewalk already mid-argument, voices carrying into the building before the door even finished closing behind them.

Inside the sheriff’s office it was too small for this many bodies. People crowded the counter, then crowded behind the people crowding the counter. Someone tried to talk about food and got cut off by someone talking about the heart-attack man and then someone else talking about the school. It was all the same tone—urgent without clarity, angry without a target.

The sheriff was there. He stood behind the counter with his hands braced on it, watching the room like he was trying to identify a single problem he could address.

He let them talk.

He let them burn through themselves, the way storms did when there was nowhere for the wind to go.

When the noise finally thinned enough for his voice to fit into it, he said, “Nothing’s been declared.”

People stared at him.

“I don’t have authorization for coordinated action,” he continued. “Procedures don’t say it’s time yet.”

For a second no one spoke, as if they were waiting for the next part, the part where he offered something else.

There was no next part.

“So we just wait?” someone asked.

The sheriff hesitated. “For now? Keep things calm. Stick to what you know.”

# Chapter 42 — Handling It

The sheriff’s office didn’t empty after the sheriff said to keep calm.

People stayed because leaving felt like admitting something had already been decided without them, and no one wanted to be the first person to do that. The arguments thinned but didn’t stop. They dropped into lower registers, repeated themselves, circled. Someone near the counter kept saying, “So we just wait,” like if the sentence got said enough times it might turn into instructions.

Robert stood with Samantha near the doorway, not fully inside, not fully out. He’d put her there on purpose, close enough to leave quickly if they needed to, far enough back that she wasn’t getting jostled every time someone gestured too hard. She leaned into him more than she probably meant to, her ankle forcing her weight to one side. He adjusted without looking, the movement automatic now.

She was watching faces. Not the sheriff. The faces.

The door opened again.

At first, nothing happened. Someone finished a sentence. Someone else started another. The noise didn’t stop so much as tilt, like a radio losing signal for a half second and then settling on a different station.

Robert felt it before he saw anything change. A small, unpleasant drop in his stomach, like missing a step you didn’t know was there.

Then he noticed the three people standing just inside the doorway.

They weren’t deputies. They weren’t teachers. They weren’t anyone Robert recognized, and that bothered him more than if they had been. Their clothes were plain in a way that didn’t call attention to itself—clean, practical, unremarkable. No badges visible. No bags. No papers. They didn’t push forward or raise their voices. They just stood there, waiting, like the room would eventually catch up to them.

The sheriff looked at them.

He didn’t straighten. He didn’t greet them. His hands came off the counter and rested at his sides, like he’d been interrupted in the middle of something that no longer mattered.

Someone near Robert whispered, “Who are they?”

Another voice, uncertain, answered, “They’re… with the lab. I think.”

The word lab didn’t ripple the way school would have. It didn’t bring up faces or names or familiarity. It landed heavy instead, undefined, and stayed there.

One of the women stepped forward just enough that she didn’t have to raise her voice.

“We’re aware of what’s happening,” she said.

The room quieted, not fully, but enough that Robert realized how loud it had been before.

“This is being handled,” she continued. “There’s no need for a public gathering.”

Someone tried to jump in immediately, and another voice cut across it, sharper, frustrated. The woman waited. She didn’t hurry them. She didn’t look annoyed. She let the words trip over each other until they collapsed into their own silence.

“I understand people are concerned,” she said. “But uncoordinated groups create confusion. They interfere with assessment.”

Assessment.

Robert found himself thinking about the notebook out on the folding table in the street. The half-finished list. The way the woman writing it had stopped once no one could agree what the list was even for. Assessment sounded like something that didn’t care whether the list made sense.

A man near the front scoffed. “So what, we go home and pretend this is normal?”

“No one is asking you to pretend,” the woman said. “We’re asking you to return home and allow the process to continue.”

The sheriff cleared his throat.

For a second Robert thought he was going to say something—anything—but instead he glanced at the woman, nodded once, and fell quiet. It wasn’t dramatic. It was worse than that. It was the sound of a decision being accepted before anyone had admitted it was being made.

Robert felt something tighten in his chest, sharp and unpleasant. He’d come here because this was where you came when things didn’t make sense. Watching the sheriff step back without being told made that instinct feel naïve.

Another of the three—the man—checked his phone. The motion was small, almost careless, but it pulled Robert’s attention anyway.

“If action is required,” the man said, still looking at the screen, “you’ll be notified through established channels.”

“By who?” someone asked.

The man looked up briefly, as if the question had arrived from a slightly different angle than expected. “Appropriate parties,” he said, and looked back down.

People began to leave.

Not all at once. One woman near the wall first, then a man by the door. A couple followed, not meeting anyone’s eyes as they slipped past. No one told them to go. Staying just began to feel like the wrong choice.

Sam’s fingers tightened in Robert’s sleeve. He shifted instinctively, giving her more of his weight to lean on.

Someone near the doorway muttered, “So that’s it.”

“For now,” the woman said, polite and final.

The room thinned. Voices faded into the hallway and then outside. The pressure eased without resolving, like something being deflated before it was finished taking shape.

The three of them stayed where they were.

The sheriff stepped back from the counter, not hurried, not reluctant, just done.

In the quiet that followed, Robert became aware of the ticking clock on the wall, louder now that there was nothing else competing with it. He glanced up without thinking.

Then the man spoke again.

“It’s ten forty.”

The words landed oddly. Not wrong exactly. Just misplaced.

Robert looked back at the clock. The numbers didn’t line up with what the man had said. He stared at them for a second longer than necessary, waiting for something to correct itself—his sense of time, the clock, the moment. Nothing did.

The man slipped his phone back into his pocket, unconcerned, as if the room had already adjusted around the time he was carrying rather than the other way around.

Robert stayed where he was, Sam warm and unsteady against his side, and tried to trace the moment when the room had stopped belonging to the people in it.

# Chapter 43 — Already Handled

Robert drove to the clinic the same way he had the day before.

He kept both hands on the wheel and braked early, more out of habit than care, because Sam was sitting sideways again with her leg pulled up and he didn’t want to feel her tense when the car slowed. She stared out the window instead of at the road. When the car hit a seam in the pavement she sucked in a breath and then let it out slowly, like she’d practiced.

“You okay?” he asked.

“I’m okay,” Sam said.

He waited.

“It hurts,” she added.

The parking lot wasn’t full, but it wasn’t empty either. A truck idled near the curb with no one inside it. Someone sat in the driver’s seat of a sedan scrolling on their phone, thumb moving fast. No one looked up when Robert parked.

Inside, the smell was the same.

The waiting room wasn’t.

The chairs were lined up instead of angled, spaced farther apart than they’d been before. The magazines were gone. A laminated sign was taped to the counter, the edges curling just slightly. Robert glanced at it and then looked away before he finished reading.

He helped Sam inside and guided her to a chair near the wall. She lowered herself carefully, gripping the seat with both hands. He stood in front of her for a second, then moved toward the counter because standing there felt like blocking traffic.

The woman behind the counter was writing something. She didn’t look up right away. Robert waited, not sure if he was supposed to speak yet or not.

She finished the line she was on and raised her head.

“Name?”

He told her.

“Date of birth?”

He answered.

“What’s changed?”

“It’s—” He stopped. Started again. “It’s worse. The swelling’s higher. She can’t put weight on it now.”

The woman nodded once and wrote something down.

“We’ll call you,” she said.

Robert stayed where he was, waiting for the rest of it. When nothing came, he stepped away and went back to Sam.

People moved through the room in uneven bursts. A couple came in carrying a toddler who was crying hard enough that his face had gone red, and they were taken through a door almost immediately. A man with his hand wrapped in a towel stood near the wall for a while, then was waved forward without anyone saying his name. Two teenagers came in together, whispered, then turned around and left.

Sam sat very still. Her foot didn’t touch the floor.

After a while, Robert stood up again.

He went back to the counter.

“Sorry,” he said, because that word came out automatically. “We’ve been waiting.”

The woman looked up.

“Name?”

He told her again. His voice sounded thinner than he expected.

She glanced down, pen following a line on the page.

“She’s already been handled,” she said.

Robert stood there.

“We were here yesterday,” he said.

“Yes,” she said.

“And it’s worse,” he said. “Her ankle’s worse. It’s bigger. She can’t stand on it. She couldn’t really stand on it before, but now she—”

“That’s consistent,” the woman said.

“With what,” he said, and the words came out too fast.

She didn’t answer that. She just looked at him, pen resting against the paper.

“There’s no further action required at this time,” she said. “You can continue monitoring at home.”

Monitoring.

“Monitoring,” he repeated.

“Yes.”

“So if it gets worse,” he said. “If it keeps getting worse.”

“If there’s a change,” she said, “you’ll be notified.”

“By who,” he asked, sharper than he meant to be.

She paused. Not long. Just long enough.

“You can take a seat,” she said.

He didn’t move.

“She’s right there,” he said, gesturing without turning around. “She’s sitting right there.”

The woman’s eyes flicked past him and back.

“She’s already been assessed,” she said.

Handled. Assessed. The words kept coming back around, never landing on anything he could grab.

“Can we talk to someone else,” he said. “A doctor. A nurse.”

“If further intervention is required,” she said, “you’ll be notified.”

That again.

Robert opened his mouth to say something else and couldn’t find where to start. Everything he thought of sounded wrong before it reached his tongue. He stood there long enough that the space between them started to feel noticeable.

Behind him, Sam shifted. Just a small sound.

He turned.

Her face had gone pale. The ankle was bigger than it had been when they came in. He didn’t need to crouch to see it. He didn’t need to touch it.

He looked back at the counter.

The woman was already writing again.

He waited for something to happen. For someone to say something. For the pen to stop.

It didn’t.

He went back to Sam instead of standing there any longer.

“We’re going home,” he said.

Sam frowned. “But—”

“I know,” he said, and bent before she could argue. He slid one arm under her knees and the other behind her back and lifted her. She hissed softly and clutched his shoulder.

As he carried her out, the room kept doing what it had been doing before he spoke—waiting, shifting, breathing—like it didn’t belong to anyone in it.

Outside, the light was too bright.

Robert opened the car door one-handed and lowered her into the seat, careful with her ankle, then shut the door and stood there for a moment before walking around to the driver’s side.

# Chapter 44

Robert parked at the curb because the driveway was full. The garage door was open and the light was on, and Mark was inside moving things around without much purpose. Plastic bins stacked near the wall, lids half on. Masking tape labels peeling. A radio murmured somewhere toward the back, mostly static, the voice coming and going.

Mark looked up when Robert shut the car door. His eyes went past him to the back seat.

“Hey,” he said. “Sam okay?”

“She twisted her ankle,” Robert said. He stayed where he was instead of coming closer. “She’s fine. I just didn’t want to haul her out again.”

“Yeah,” Mark said. “Yeah, that makes sense.”

He turned back to the shelf, slid one of the bins over, then slid it back again. Robert followed him into the garage and leaned against the workbench. The concrete felt cool through his shoes. From the car came a faint thump as Sam shifted, then nothing.

“They wouldn’t see her,” Robert said.

Mark paused. “Who wouldn’t.”

“The clinic,” Robert said. “Today.”

Mark turned a little, waiting.

“They said she was already handled,” Robert said. He didn’t add anything to it. “That was the phrase.”

“Handled,” Mark said.

“Yeah.”

Mark frowned slightly. “Handled how.”

“That’s the thing,” Robert said. “They didn’t say. Just told me to keep an eye on it. Like it was finished.”

Mark picked up a rag and wiped his hands. They weren’t dirty. He wiped them anyway.

“You tell them it was worse.”

“More than once.”

“And they still sent you home.”

“Yeah.”

Mark nodded. Not agreeing. Just acknowledging the shape of it. “That’s not great.”

Robert let out a breath. “Right.”

The radio clicked. The voice dropped out, came back thinner. Mark reached for a box, turned it, set it down again.

“There was a woman at the counter,” Robert said. He hadn’t meant to say that part. “I don’t know why, but she felt familiar.”

Mark looked at him. “Like you knew her.”

“Like I should,” Robert said. “But I don’t.”

Mark nodded once. “Yeah.”

They stood there. The garage felt caught between inside and outside, like it couldn’t decide.

“People have been talking,” Mark said. Then, “About that research lab.”

Robert looked at him. “What about it.”

Mark shrugged. “Frank was saying he saw a guy over at the hardware store. Swore it was the same guy he’d seen out by the lab a few weeks back. Badge and everything.”

“And,” Robert said.

“And nothing,” Mark said. “Probably helping out. Everybody’s short-handed. That’s what he said, anyway.”

“At the lab,” Robert said.

“Yeah,” Mark said. “I mean. That’s what Frank said. You know how Frank is.”

Robert didn’t answer.

“I wouldn’t put too much weight on it,” Mark said. “People see one thing and start stacking stories.”

“Yeah,” Robert said.

“And Alicia,” he said, before he’d decided to.

Mark looked up. “What about her.”

“She’s fine,” Robert said right away. “I think. She’s helping out a lot. At the school. She’s just… calmer.”

“Teenagers do that,” Mark said. “When they’re tired.”

“Maybe.”

“She’s probably just stepping up,” Mark said. “Trying to be useful.”

“Yeah,” Robert said again. The word didn’t sit right, but he let it stay.

They stood there a little longer. A car passed slow enough that Robert noticed it. Down the street, a lawnmower started and stopped almost immediately.

“I should get her home,” Robert said.

“Yeah,” Mark said. “Let me know if—”

“Sure.”

Robert went back to the car. Sam looked up when he opened the door.

“Everything okay?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

He closed the door and stood there a second, listening to the radio through the open garage, to the sound of Mark shifting things that didn’t need shifting.

On the drive home, the words came back the way they’d been said.

*Already handled.*

Not loud. Not sharp. Just finished.

He’d heard it at the clinic. And before that, something close enough at the school that it slid when he tried to separate the two.

It wasn’t the words. It was the way they closed things.

By the time he pulled into his driveway, the research lab had a place in his head it hadn’t had before.

# Chapter 45

Sam slept longer than she should have.

Robert noticed because she didn’t argue when he touched her shoulder, didn’t pull the blanket up or roll away, just opened her eyes and closed them again like the effort had cost something. When he helped her sit, she leaned into him without comment, her weight heavier than usual, and the warmth of her skin came through the sweatshirt in a way that made him pause before he decided not to think about it yet.

“You okay?” he asked, even though the answer was already there.

“I’m tired,” she said, after a moment. Then, quieter, “My ankle hurts.”

“I know,” he said. “We’re going to the hospital.”

That got her eyes open again, slow but focused enough. “The big one?”

“Yeah,” he said. “Outside town.”

She nodded and let him do the rest—help her dress, slide the shoe on carefully, lift her without protest—things she usually insisted on doing herself. She didn’t complain, and the lack of it stayed with him longer than it should have.

He called the hospital while she sat on the edge of the bed.

The automated system answered, then another, and he followed the prompts with the patience of someone who could see the end of it now that he’d finally started. A real voice picked up after the second transfer, calm and professional in a way that steadied him more than he wanted to admit.

He explained. Twisted ankle. Swelling. Fever.

“Bring her in,” the woman said. “We’ll take a look.”

Relief came fast enough that he had to sit down.

“Okay,” he said. “We’re on our way.”

“Drive safe,” she said, and the line went dead.

He wrote the hospital’s name on a scrap of paper even though he knew it, folded it once, and put it in his pocket like it mattered.

Sam dozed in the passenger seat as he drove, her head tipped toward the window, breath shallow and uneven. He followed the road out of town without thinking about it at first, because this was a route he knew and knowing something felt important right now, and he let himself believe that familiarity would carry them the rest of the way if he didn’t interfere with it.

Sam is already dressed, sitting cross-legged on the bed. Alicia stands near the door with Anne’s keys in her hand.

“They were on the dresser,” Alicia says.

Anne nods.

They check out. The clerk barely looks up. Outside, the morning smells like asphalt warming and coffee from somewhere nearby.

Anne drives.

The road stretches out ahead of them, long and familiar. She keeps both hands on the wheel and glances at the rearview mirror.

Both girls sit in the backseat.

Sam leans against the window, watching the world pass. Alicia sits upright beside her, seatbelt fastened, eyes forward.

The light changes.

Anne presses the gas.

In the mirror, her daughters watch the road unfold behind them.
