# Chapter 1: The Weekend

The handoff was fast.

Too fast for small talk, too fast for second guesses. The girls climbed out of the car with their backpacks already on, jackets zipped, hair still damp from the rain. Their mother stayed in the driver’s seat, engine running, phone wedged between her shoulder and ear, doing that thing where a person is physically present but mentally halfway down the road.

“I’ll text when I’m home,” she said, distracted. “They ate already.”

Robert nodded. He always nodded. The nod had been perfected during the divorce—noncommittal, polite, useful as a paper towel. He took the bags, shut the door, and stood on the curb while her car pulled away and merged back into traffic.

That was the last normal thing that happened.

He was halfway to the front door when the air changed. A pressure drop. The kind that made his ears ring and his stomach tighten for no clear reason, like his body had gotten a text his phone couldn’t receive.

Alicia stopped walking. “Dad?”

Before he could answer, the sound hit—deep, tearing, wrong. Not thunder. Not an explosion. Something structural giving up somewhere nearby. A ripple went through the ground, subtle but undeniable, like the earth had flinched.

Car alarms started screaming up and down the street.

Robert grabbed Samantha’s shoulder and steered her inside. “Shoes off later,” he said, already locking the door behind them. The house lights flickered once, twice, then steadied.

For maybe five minutes, nothing else happened.

They stood in the living room, listening to distant sirens and closer shouting. Someone ran past the house. Then another. Phones buzzed uselessly in pockets, lighting up with *No Signal* and *Call Failed* messages.

Then the second sound came.

Closer this time. A sharp metallic shriek followed by a dull, concussive thud. The lights went out completely.

Samantha screamed.

Robert dropped to a knee, pulling both girls against him. He could smell something acrid through the closed windows. Not smoke. Something cleaner. Industrial. The kind of smell that belonged in a factory, not in your living room on a Friday night.

“Basement,” he said. “Now. Move your butts.”

They made it halfway down the stairs when the pounding started at the front door.

“Help!” a man shouted. “Please—my wife—”

Robert froze.

His hand went toward the deadbolt anyway, because that’s what decent people did. That’s what they were raised to do. Then he pictured a stranger’s hand on the other side of the door, and the picture didn’t come with a face. It came with teeth.

The pounding came again. Harder. The doorframe rattled.

Alicia’s grip tightened on his arm. She didn’t say anything.

Robert listened.

He counted breaths. One. Two. Three. Four—because he was the kind of guy who believed the extra breath might change the outcome.

Another sound cut through the shouting—glass breaking somewhere down the street. A crash, followed by a woman screaming.

The pounding stopped.

Footsteps ran past the house.

Robert exhaled slowly and kept moving, hating himself for it and doing it anyway.

They stayed in the basement until the noises thinned out, until the shouting turned into distant, directionless sound. When they finally came back upstairs, the house felt smaller. Like it had already learned something about them.

Outside, the street was dark. No streetlights. No passing cars. Just people moving in uneven clusters, flashlights bobbing, voices raised and urgent.

Robert locked the door again.

They ate peanut butter straight from the jar because it didn’t require cooking. Samantha complained about the taste. Alicia didn’t. Alicia watched the dark windows like she expected them to complain back.

Just before midnight, Robert dug the old radio out of the hall closet.

He had to move boxes to get to it—holiday decorations, a folded stroller he’d never thrown away. The radio was heavier than he remembered, metal casing scuffed, antenna bent at the joint.

He set it on the kitchen counter and cranked the dial.

Static.

He turned it slowly, careful not to skip past anything. The noise shifted in texture—hiss to crackle to low hum—but never resolved into words.

Samantha sat at the table with her knees pulled up, watching his hands.

“Is that the news?” she asked.

“Yeah,” he said, because it was easier than explaining why it wasn’t.

Alicia leaned against the doorway, arms crossed. She watched the radio, not him.

For a while, someone spoke.

Not clearly. Just fragments slipping through the static.

“…county…”
“…temporary…”
“…remain—”

Robert leaned closer, adjusting the antenna, heart pounding.

The signal collapsed back into noise.

He tried another band. Then another.

An emergency tone sounded once—sharp, electronic—and cut off mid-note.

No repeat. No follow-up.

Hours passed without anyone noticing until Robert realized his legs had gone numb from standing.

Outside, the street stayed dark. No headlights. No sirens. The shouting didn’t come back.

He kept the radio on anyway.

Around two in the morning, Alicia sat on the couch and pulled a blanket around Samantha without being asked. Robert noticed and looked away.

Sometime after that, the radio stopped making noise altogether.

Not silence—power. The dial still turned, but the static was gone. Dead air.

Robert didn’t turn it off.

He sat on the floor with his back against the cabinet and watched the window until the gray light crept in.

Morning didn’t bring answers.

Phones stayed dark. The radio stayed empty. The air outside looked the same as it had hours ago, heavy and unmoving.

When Robert opened the front door, the quiet pressed in immediately. Not peaceful. Expectant.

A man stood in the middle of the street turning in slow circles, like he was waiting for something to appear.

Alicia came up beside him. “Are we going somewhere?”

Robert watched the man stop, then sit down on the curb.

“No,” he said. “We’re staying.”

She nodded.

Samantha didn’t ask.

The town was quiet in a way that felt permanent.

Robert didn’t know yet that something had noticed them.

He only knew that whatever came next would not involve leaving.

# Chapter 2: No Signal

The radio stayed useless, but Robert kept turning the dial anyway, slower now, as if patience might coax something out of it. Static filled the kitchen in a flat, even sheet—no rise, no fall—just sound without information, without shape.

Samantha lay on her stomach on the living room floor, chin in her hands, staring at nothing in particular while the noise went on and on.

“This is boring,” she said.

“I know,” Robert replied, still watching the radio like it might change if he looked hard enough.

Alicia sat at the table with a notebook open in front of her. She wasn’t writing. She was listening, head slightly tilted, as though she expected the static to slip and reveal something underneath.

“I want my Switch,” Samantha said after a while.

“It’s in the basement,” Robert answered automatically, already half elsewhere. “Be careful.”

She was halfway to the stairs before he realized he hadn’t actually watched her go.

The first few steps sounded normal—socks on wood, soft and familiar, the rhythm of a hundred earlier trips.

Then the rhythm broke.

A sharp crack.

A breathless sound that wasn’t quite a scream.

“Sam?”

The silence afterward landed heavier than noise ever had.

Robert reached the stairs just as Samantha started crying in earnest. She was twisted sideways on the steps, one leg folded under her, both hands gripping her ankle like it might come loose if she didn’t hold it in place.

“I slipped,” she said between sobs. “I didn’t mean to—”

He crouched and touched the ankle. It was already swelling, skin hot and tight under his fingers, the joint misshapen in a way that made his stomach drop.

“Don’t move,” he said.

She tried anyway and screamed.

Alicia stood at the top of the stairs, very still, watching his hands.

Robert lifted Samantha carefully. Her arms locked around his neck, her body shaking as he carried her back to the couch.

The radio hissed on, unchanged.

He didn’t wait to see if it would settle. He didn’t try to convince himself it was nothing. He grabbed his keys, lifted her again, and went.

Outside, the street looked worse than it had earlier—more abandoned cars, fewer people. Those who were still out moved quickly and didn’t look at one another, like stopping carried its own risk.

The clinic lights were on.

That felt like a promise.

Inside, it was chaos under fluorescent lighting. Too many people packed into too little space. A constant low roar of voices layered with crying, coughing, raised arguments that never quite turned into fights. It smelled like wet coats, disinfectant, and fear—fear always had a smell, if you paid attention.

A nurse at the desk didn’t look up when they approached. She looked like she’d been built out of coffee and irritation.

“Name.”

Robert gave it.

“Age.”

“Eight.”

“What happened?”

“Fall. Stairs. Her ankle.”

“Can she walk?”

“No.”

The nurse nodded once, already reaching for a clipboard. “Take a seat. If you pass out, try not to land on anybody.”

They waited.

Minutes stretched into something heavier. Samantha’s crying dulled into small, exhausted sounds as Robert shifted her weight again and again, arms burning, shoulders locking up. People were brought back ahead of them—bleeding, shaking, shouting—while others were sent away just as quickly.

Eventually, a different nurse waved them over without making eye contact.

“Shoes off,” she said, already pulling on gloves.

She pressed along Samantha’s ankle quickly, efficiently, without warning. Samantha cried out.

“Sprain,” the nurse said immediately. “Happening a lot tonight.” Like it was a sale item.

She wrapped the ankle briskly, the elastic bandage firm but imperfect, already loosening at the edges by the time she finished.

“Rest. Ice. Keep it elevated,” she said, turning away as she spoke. “If it’s still bad in a few days, follow up.”

“No X-ray?” Robert asked.

The nurse hesitated just long enough to be irritated. “Not unless she can’t feel her toes. Or the bone starts waving hello.”

She was already gone.

They were ushered back into the waiting area and then toward the door before Robert fully realized the visit was over.

He was still holding Samantha when the world shifted.

Not a flicker. Not a blur.

More like something snapping quietly into place behind his eyes.

Text hung in the air, sharp and wrong, layered over the scuffed wall by the exit.

**STATUS UPDATE RECORDED**  
**Trait Affected: Mobility**  
**Condition Applied: Slowed**

“What the fuck,” Robert whispered, because sometimes profanity was just accuracy.

He turned his head. The words stayed.

He shut his eyes hard and opened them again.

Still there.

“Sir?” someone said sharply.

The text vanished.

His arms felt heavier immediately—not weak, not tired, just weighted, like gravity had tilted slightly against him.

“Dad,” Samantha said quietly.

He froze.

“What’s wrong?” he asked.

She was staring past him, eyes unfocused. “There was… something else.”

“What kind of something?”

She hesitated. “It said I needed help. About not walking by myself.”

His stomach dropped.

“What exactly did it say?” he asked.

She shook her head. “It went away.”

Alicia was watching both of them now. Very carefully.

They were sent outside moments later, the door closing behind them with a hollow final sound.

On the sidewalk, Robert nearly stumbled. His foot caught on nothing, balance lagging just enough to matter.

A man passed them easily, moving faster than he should have been able to with the limp he’d had earlier.

Alicia noticed. Her eyes tracked his feet, then flicked back to Robert’s.

She frowned but said nothing.

By the time they reached the car, Robert’s legs were shaking.

The radio in the dashboard was dead.

No static. No signal.

Just silence.

# Chapter 3: The Ankle

Morning didn’t fix anything.

Samantha tried to stand as soon as she woke up, as if sleeping on it might have been enough. She pushed herself off the couch, jaw set, hands braced, and made it almost upright before her knee buckled and she dropped back down with a sharp breath.

“I’m okay,” she said immediately.

Robert watched her ankle. The wrap from the clinic had loosened overnight, elastic sliding where the swelling pushed against it. The joint looked wrong now—too large, skin stretched tight and shiny, bruising dark and uneven along the side.

“Don’t,” he said. “You don’t have to.”

She nodded, embarrassed, and pulled her foot back onto the cushion.

The house felt exposed in daylight.

Not brighter—just uncovered. Every sound carried. The refrigerator cycling. Wind pushing against the siding. Voices outside that rose and fell without pattern.

Robert checked his phone.

No signal.

He turned the radio on again. The dial moved. Nothing came through.

“They said sprain,” he said, mostly to himself. “It just needs time.”

Samantha nodded because that was what she did when adults sounded certain. She shifted slightly, then froze as pain cut through the movement.

Robert fetched ice and pressed it gently against the ankle. She flinched but didn’t pull away.

Alicia leaned closer. “It’s worse,” she said.

“I know.”

“It wasn’t like that last night.”

“I know.”

They stayed that way for a while. Ice melting. The quiet stretching thin around them.

Eventually Samantha asked, “Can I go outside?”

Robert hesitated.

“Just the porch,” she said quickly.

He looked out the window. The sky was flat and colorless, distance compressed until the end of the street felt too close. A man limped past with a grocery bag dragging behind him. No one helped him.

“Okay,” Robert said. “But not far.”

Getting Samantha down the front steps took time. She gripped the railing with both hands, moving one stair at a time, breathing hard by the time they reached the sidewalk.

They didn’t make it past the mailbox.

Her foot slipped on loose gravel and she cried out, sharp and startled.

Robert caught her before she fell.

The sound carried.

A man two houses down stopped what he was doing. A woman across the street looked up. Someone closer said, “Hey—”

“She okay?” the woman asked.

“Yes,” Robert said. “Sprain.”

The woman nodded, already reaching into her pocket.

“They’re doing check-ins,” she said. “Just making sure kids are accounted for.”

“I didn’t—” Robert started.

“It’s fine,” she said, not waiting. “What’s her name?”

Robert hesitated for half a second too long.

Alicia answered. “Samantha.”

The woman glanced at the ankle, then at Alicia. “And you?”

“Alicia.”

She typed with her thumb, screen glowing briefly in the daylight.

“Okay,” she said. “They’ll expect you.”

“Expect us where?” Robert asked.

“At the school,” she replied, already turning away. “They’re tracking injuries.”

The word tracking lingered after she left.

Robert carried Samantha back inside.

The effort burned more than it should have. His grip slipped once, just enough that Alicia stepped in without thinking and steadied Samantha’s leg until he adjusted.

Inside, he set her down and stayed crouched, breathing harder than he wanted to.

“They already know,” Alicia said.

“Know what?”

“That she’s hurt,” Alicia said. “That we’re here.”

Robert looked at the door.

The microwave clock ticked over to the next minute.

The sound felt loud.

He waited for something to contradict this.

Nothing did.

They weren’t invisible anymore.

# Chapter 4: First Gathering

Cars were already moving when they pulled out of the driveway.

Not rushing. Not hesitating. Just going, all in the same direction, steady enough that Robert fell in without thinking. At the first intersection, no one waited. The turn simply happened.

The school parking lot was fuller than he expected. Parents stood outside their cars instead of heading straight in, adjusting backpacks, lifting children down carefully, looking toward the doors like they were waiting for something to happen first.

Robert parked farther out than usual.

“I can walk,” Samantha said.

He didn’t answer. He lifted her before she could try. The wrap around her ankle pressed tight against swollen skin, the elastic already digging in. She made a small sound and then stopped herself.

Near the doors, a woman raised her hand.

Not a wave. A signal.

She stepped forward with a clipboard and glanced down at Samantha’s ankle before looking at Robert’s face.

“Okay,” she said, and made a mark.

She handed him a narrow strip of paper. Blue.

“Keep that visible.”

“Why?” Robert asked.

“It helps,” she said, already turning away.

Inside, the gym looked wrong.

Not damaged. Organized.

Colored tape divided the floor into wide paths that led toward different exits. People clustered along them, uncertain but compliant, standing where the tape suggested they should stand. No one crossed from one color to another.

A man stood near the bleachers with a megaphone, but his voice was calm.

“We’re just going to get everyone where they need to be,” he said. “This won’t take long.”

Parents shifted. Children leaned against legs. Someone started to ask a question and then stopped.

“Anyone needing help with movement,” the man continued, “please follow the blue line.”

Robert didn’t move.

A staff member appeared at his side.

“Blue,” she said, lightly, already guiding his shoulder.

“I can carry her,” Robert said.

“Yes,” she replied. “That’s fine.”

They stepped onto the blue tape.

It was shorter than the others. Quieter. People here stood closer together, watching instead of talking.

Across the gym, another group began to move.

Alicia watched them.

“Where are they going?” she asked.

“I don’t know,” Robert said.

The green line emptied quickly. Doors opened. People passed through them and didn’t come back.

Another staff member walked the length of the blue line, tablet in hand. She stopped briefly in front of each family, glancing, tapping, moving on.

When she reached them, her eyes flicked to Samantha’s ankle, then to Robert’s arms.

She tapped once and moved on.

“What did she write?” Alicia asked.

“I don’t know,” Robert said again.

The megaphone crackled.

“If your child can’t walk without help, please stay where you are.”

No one on the blue line moved.

The gym grew quieter as the other lines thinned.

Samantha shifted against Robert’s shoulder. “Why are we still here?”

“Because you’re hurt,” he said.

“But they’re hurt too,” she said, nodding toward a boy with a bandaged wrist being guided toward the doors.

Robert didn’t have an answer.

They were directed into the hallway next. Doors stood open, lights already on. Signs were taped beside them in neat block letters.

ASSISTANCE REQUIRED  
LIMITED MOBILITY

Robert slowed.

Samantha read the sign and frowned. “That’s not my name.”

“I know,” he said.

Inside the room, chairs lined the walls. Parents stood instead of sitting. A woman handed Robert another strip of paper, this one a different color.

“If you leave,” she said, “bring this back with you.”

“Leave to go where?” he asked.

She smiled. “Anywhere.”

The door closed.

Robert eased Samantha into a chair. His arms shook when he straightened.

Alicia stood near the doorway, watching people pass in the hall—people without blue paper, moving quickly, carrying nothing.

“Dad?” she asked.

“Yeah?”

“What’s happening?”

Robert opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

The bell rang.

It sounded the way it always had.

# Chapter 5: Faster

They waited in the classroom longer than Robert expected, long enough for the air to grow stale and for the initial tension to drain into something duller and harder to ignore. Parents shifted positions without fully sitting down, as if committing to a chair might make the waiting permanent, and every few minutes someone glanced at the door, then looked away again when nothing happened.

Robert sat with Samantha across his lap, her injured leg carefully angled so the ankle didn’t press against anything. His arms still felt wrong, heavy in a way that didn’t fade, the ache settling deeper each time he adjusted his grip. Alicia sat on the floor near his feet, knees drawn up, watching the narrow window in the door with the patient intensity of someone tracking movement even when there was none.

People passed in the hallway beyond the glass, moving with purpose and speed, most of them carrying green slips of paper instead of blue, their voices low and relieved as they went. Once, someone laughed, and the sound carried just far enough into the room to feel misplaced before it disappeared again.

When the woman with the clipboard entered, the room stilled almost immediately.

“Okay,” she said, scanning the space without looking at anyone in particular. “We’re going to clear a few families through so we can keep things moving.”

Robert straightened slightly, instinctive and useless.

“If your situation has changed,” she continued, “or if you feel you were categorized incorrectly, now’s the time to tell us.”

The words settled into the room and stayed there.

Across from him, a man stood and gestured toward his son, who rose quickly and put his weight down with a visible wince that he immediately smoothed away. The boy took two careful steps, then a third, his face tightening before his father’s hand closed around his shoulder in encouragement.

“He twisted it earlier,” the man said easily, already smiling. “But he’s fine now. Walk it off, right?”

The woman watched for a moment longer than was comfortable, then nodded once.

“Green,” she said.

They were gone almost immediately, the door closing softly behind them.

The absence they left behind felt heavier than their presence had been.

Samantha shifted against Robert’s chest. “He was hurt too,” she said quietly.

“I know,” Robert replied, his eyes still on the door.

“But they let him go.”

“Yes.”

Alicia looked up at him, her brow drawn together. “Why did they get to leave?”

Robert didn’t answer because the answer had already begun to form, unwelcome and precise.

The clipboard woman returned to the doorway, her pen hovering.

“Last check,” she said. “After this, we’ll finalize groupings.” Like they were closing down a register and all the customers had to go somewhere.

Robert looked down at Samantha’s ankle. The swelling hadn’t gone down at all. If anything, it had crept higher, the wrap stretched tight and uneven, the skin beneath it flushed and angry. Samantha noticed his gaze and shook her head almost imperceptibly, her fingers tightening in his shirt.

“I can’t,” she whispered.

He knew that.

He also knew something else, ugly and immediate: nobody in this room got points for being honest.

He also knew that the question wasn’t whether she could walk.

It was whether he could say she could and live with himself afterward.

When the woman’s eyes met his, he heard himself speak before he could stop it.

“She can walk,” Robert said. “She just twisted it. She’s sore, but she can manage.”

The lie landed softly, shaped exactly like the truth would have, and he hated how easy that was.

The woman glanced down at Samantha’s ankle, then at Robert’s face, her expression unreadable.

“Can you walk for me, sweetheart?” she asked.

Samantha hesitated for half a second too long.

Robert tightened his arm around her, just slightly. “She can,” he said, firmer now. “She gets dramatic. But she can.”

The woman nodded.

“All right,” she said. “Let’s move you over.”

As Robert stood, the world shifted.

Text settled into place in the air in front of him, steady and indifferent.

**STATUS UPDATE RECORDED**  
**Trait Affected: Compliance**  
**Condition Applied: Efficient**

Robert’s breath caught.

The words didn’t flicker or glow. They simply existed.

“Dad?” Alicia said.

The text vanished.

They moved faster after that.

Doors opened sooner. Instructions shortened. Within minutes they were standing with families holding green slips, no one asking them to wait, no one looking twice at Samantha’s careful, uneven steps.

Outside, the air felt different.

Alicia looked up at Robert. “Why did that work?”

Robert didn’t answer.

He was still thinking about the word **Efficient**, and how easily it had appeared.

# Chapter 6: The Green Room

Robert expected “green” to mean _out_. Home. A couch. A door with a lock he controlled.

Instead, “green” meant another room.

They were funneled into it with the same soft voices and the same hands that never quite pushed and never quite let you forget they could. The room was bigger than the classroom—cafeteria, maybe, or a multipurpose room that smelled faintly of mop water and old pizza even when there was no pizza. Folding tables had been shoved to the walls. Chairs were set in neat little islands, like whoever arranged them had been trying to pretend this wasn’t a holding pen.

People talked in that brittle, whispery way people talked at wakes and in hospital waiting rooms. Too polite. Too careful. Like the room itself might take offense.

Samantha leaned into Robert, her ankle throbbing against his foot. Every time she adjusted, his arms flared with that wrong heaviness, as if the clinic had quietly added sand to his bones.

“We’re leaving now, right?” she asked, hopeful in the way that made him want to break something.

“Soon,” Robert lied, because he’d been doing a lot of that lately and it kept getting rewarded.

Alicia sat with her backpack upright between her feet, hands folded like she was in church. Her eyes kept ticking around the room, landing on staff, landing on doorways, landing on empty patches of air as if there were things there worth reading.

Robert tried not to watch her. He watched her anyway.

A man two chairs over muttered, “This is some bullshit,” and his wife hissed at him like profanity would summon a staff member the way saying Bloody Mary summoned a ghost.

Robert almost laughed. It came out as a cough.

Across the room, a staff member with a tablet paused beside an old man and murmured something. The old man nodded too quickly. When the staff member moved on, the old man’s shoulders dropped like he’d just been spared. Nobody congratulated him. Nobody asked why.

Robert looked at the doors. There were three. Only one was being used. The others might as well have been painted on.

His phone buzzed against his thigh—phantom vibration, maybe, or maybe not. He pulled it out and saw signal bars.

Not full. Not even good. But there. Present. A little green miracle.

His thumb hovered over the screen, the old habit rising like a prayer: call Anne. Text. Say _are you seeing this_.

Beside him, Alicia whispered without moving her mouth. “Don’t.”

The word was so quiet it could have been his own thought.

Robert stared at her.

Alicia didn’t look back. She kept her eyes on the aisle and swallowed once, like she was trying to swallow down a warning.

Robert slid the phone back into his pocket.

The room hummed. The lights hummed. The building hummed like it was digesting them.

Somewhere nearby, a child started crying and got shushed fast, like that sound was illegal now.

Then a voice—calm, practiced—called from the doorway, “We need everyone to remain seated until verification clears.”

Nobody asked what verification was.

Robert hated them all for that, including himself.

# Chapter 7: Verification Pending

Robert waited until the room settled.

It took longer than he expected. People kept shifting, standing and sitting again, moving bags closer to their feet, glancing at doors that didn’t open. Every few minutes someone would rise as if they’d missed an instruction, then sit back down when nothing happened. The noise never fully disappeared; it just softened into a constant, uneasy murmur.

When no one was looking directly at him, Robert eased his phone out of his pocket and turned slightly away from the others.

The screen lit up.

Signal bars were there. Fewer than usual, but present. Enough to pretend this should work.

He didn’t open his contacts. He didn’t need to. He dialed from memory, the sequence of numbers so familiar his thumb moved without hesitation. He had called it hundreds of times—standing in grocery store aisles, pacing hospital corridors, leaning against his car in parking lots while the girls slept in the back seat.

The phone rang.

Once.  
Twice.

He let himself breathe when it connected.

“Hello?” a woman said.

There was road noise behind her, the muted rush of tires on pavement, a turn signal clicking close to the receiver.

“Hey,” Robert said. “It’s Rob. It’s—” He swallowed. “It’s me.”

A pause.

“Who is this?”

He frowned. “Robert. Robert Hardy.”

Another pause. Longer this time.

“I think you’ve got the wrong number,” the woman said.

“No,” Robert said. “No, I don’t. Sorry—can you put me on with Anne?”

“I don’t know anyone named Robert,” she said. Not unkindly. Just certain. “Who are you trying to reach?”

His throat tightened. “This is Anne’s phone.”

Silence. Then a short laugh. “No, it isn’t.”

“Yes, it is,” he said, sharper than he meant to. “I’ve had this number for years. My kids have this number memorized.”

“Well,” she said, “you don’t have it now.”

Robert looked down at the screen. The number glowed there exactly as it always had. Same digits. Same spacing. No roaming indicator. No warning.

“Who am I talking to?” he asked, and heard how thin he sounded.

“Melissa,” she said. “And I don’t know an Anne. Or a Robert.”

A horn blared behind her.

“Look,” she continued, impatience creeping in, “I’m driving. You should check the number.”

“I did,” he said. “I did, okay?”

She exhaled. “Then I don’t know what to tell you.”

The line went dead.

Robert stood there for a moment longer than he meant to, phone still pressed to his ear. When he lowered it, the screen had already gone dark.

Alicia looked up at him from her chair.

“Did you get her?” she asked.

“No,” Robert said.

He didn’t try again.

He slid the phone back into his pocket and sat down, the chair creaking softly under his weight. His heart was still beating too fast, but there was no clean place for the panic to go. Nothing dramatic had happened. Nothing he could point to.

Just a call that had gone somewhere else, like the whole world had been forwarded and nobody had bothered to tell him.

Across the room, a man near the wall was arguing quietly with his phone, thumb jabbing at the screen. “I’m telling you,” he said to no one in particular, “it rang. Someone picked up. Said they’ve had the number for years.”

A woman beside him shook her head. “Same. Whoever answered mine asked how I even got it.”

Someone laughed softly, then stopped when no one joined in.

Robert watched the staff at the far end of the room. They weren’t panicking. They weren’t huddled together. They moved with the same practiced efficiency as before, checking slips, murmuring to one another, redirecting anyone who stood too long without purpose.

A woman approached a staff member and held up her phone. “I can’t reach my husband,” she said. “He’s outside town.”

The staff member glanced at the screen, then back at the woman. “We’re aware of some connectivity issues,” she said. “It should resolve once verification clears.”

“Verification of what?”

The staff member’s smile tightened. “We’ll let you know.”

The woman opened her mouth again, then closed it. She stepped back into the flow of chairs without being told.

Alicia shifted beside Samantha, pulling her backpack closer, adjusting the strap so it didn’t stick out into the aisle. When a child nearby started crying, Alicia watched the staff member nearest them, gauging how close they were before the woman moved in to quiet him.

No one announced anything.

Instead, the doors at the back of the room opened and another group was guided in, their expressions tight with the same half-formed questions. The room filled incrementally, each new arrival slotting into the spaces that had been left open on purpose.

“Green slips remain seated,” a voice called out. “Blue slips, please gather your belongings.”

The murmur swelled again, sharper this time.

A man in the blue group stood too quickly. “Where are we going?”

# Chapter 9: Keep Things Moving

Robert tried again, changing angle. “We’re taking her home.”

The woman’s voice stayed even. “That won’t help her right now.”

“You don’t know that,” Robert said.

The woman nodded as if he were right in principle. “Parents always feel that way,” she said. “It’s part of it.”

Robert looked past her toward the hallways beyond the gym, the doors that led deeper into the school. People moved in and out of those doors with purpose, carrying papers, walking at a pace that suggested the day had been scheduled.

“How long?” Robert asked.

The woman’s smile thinned. Not anger. Just firmness entering the softness. “Within tolerance,” she said.

Robert blinked. “What does that mean? Tolerance like… what, a math class?”

“It means she hasn’t been delayed,” the woman said, and her tone made it sound like good news.

“Is that supposed to make me feel better?” Robert asked.

“That’s not what I asked,” Robert said.

The woman’s eyes stayed on his. “I know,” she said gently. “But it’s the part you need.”

Robert took a step forward before he remembered to stop himself. The woman didn’t move back. She didn’t have to.

“Where is she?” he asked again.

The woman’s voice lowered slightly, like she was sharing something private. “She’s where she needs to be,” she said. “And you can stop worrying about whether you’re doing the right thing. You already did it.”

The sentence landed wrong. It closed the space where his worry lived and tried to replace it with permission. You already did it—like he’d clicked _I agree_ on a screen without reading the terms.

Robert didn’t take the permission.

He shifted tactics again, grasping for something official. “Who’s in charge here?” he asked.

The woman smiled as if he’d asked a question she got all the time. “I can help you,” she said.

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know,” she said again, and it was the same gentle tone, the same steady loop back to the point she wanted him to stand on. “But you don’t need to escalate this.”

Robert heard himself breathe.

Sam squeezed his hand once, small and quick. Not comfort. A reminder.

Robert forced himself to look down at her. “You okay?” he asked.

Sam nodded, but her eyes flicked toward the hallway doors again.

Robert looked back at the woman. “Can I get my car?” he asked, and hated that he was asking it like permission.

The woman’s expression brightened, almost relieved. “Yes,” she said. “Of course.”

The ease of that answer made Robert’s stomach turn.

She stepped aside, clearing the path the way someone did when they were being accommodating. “You’re free to go,” she added, still gentle. “You don’t need to stay here.”

Robert stared at her. “And she does.”

The woman’s smile didn’t change. “For now,” she said, like she was offering him something to hold.

Robert turned away before he could say something that would become a mistake.

He and Sam walked back out into the parking lot. The air outside felt harsher, colder than it should have. Robert reached his car and stood with his hand on the door for a moment, as if touching it could make it mean what it used to mean.

He unlocked it.

The interior smelled like stale coffee and the cheap vanilla air freshener he’d hung months ago and forgotten about. He slid into the driver’s seat and gripped the steering wheel, knuckles whitening without him deciding to.

Sam climbed in carefully, moving like her ankle was a fragile thing that might shatter if she trusted it too much. She shut the door and sat with her hands in her lap.

Robert started the car.

The engine turned over on the first try, as if this was still a normal day with normal rules.

He sat there with it running, staring at the school doors.

Sam said, softly, “Are we coming back?”

Robert didn’t answer right away.

“Yes,” he said finally.

He put the car in reverse, backed out of the space, and drove out of the lot without looking at the building again until it was already behind them.
