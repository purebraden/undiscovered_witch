# Chapter 30 — Selective Non-Intervention

Alicia was sitting at a table she didn’t remember choosing, which wasn’t unusual on its own, except that the missing moment didn’t slide away the way it normally did. It stayed with her, faint but present, like she’d paused mid-step and only realized it afterward.

The room was smaller than her classroom, though it tried to feel the same, with the same chairs and the same scuffed floor and a clock on the wall that ticked loud enough to notice once you stopped ignoring it. A woman stood near the door holding a clipboard flat against her chest, not writing anything, just holding it there, while another adult sat behind a desk with a computer that hadn’t been turned on yet.

Alicia’s hands were folded on the table.

She noticed that because they hadn’t moved in a while, fingers lined up neatly, thumbs touching the way teachers liked without ever asking for it, and she considered unfolding them before the thought wandered off and left the decision unfinished.

The computer screen came on without a sound. Not bright. Just enough that her eyes adjusted. A set of boxes appeared and shifted slightly, as if they were being arranged by something patient, and Alicia leaned forward a fraction before stopping when the chair pressed against the backs of her legs, a quiet reminder that she was already where she needed to be.

No one told her not to move.

The woman with the clipboard shifted her weight, the paper making a soft noise that reminded Alicia of test days, the kind where everyone went quiet without being told.

“We’ll let this run,” the woman said, not looking at Alicia when she spoke. “She’s already close.”

The other adult didn’t answer right away. He watched the screen for a moment longer than necessary, then nodded once.

Something loosened behind Alicia’s eyes.

Not pain. Not relief. Just a sense of space, like when you realize you’ve been squinting and stop without remembering when you started.

The boxes on the screen changed. Some faded. Others stacked. A thin line appeared along the edge, pale and steady.

BUFFER ALIGNMENT: PARTIAL  
PRIORITY ROUTING: ACTIVE

Alicia read the words without trying to understand them. They didn’t feel like instructions. They felt like something that had already happened.

The woman glanced at the screen, then at the door, as if checking a list that didn’t need to be written down.

“That should reduce noise,” she said.

No one asked Alicia how she felt.

The space outside the room was wider than a hallway and louder in a way that never settled, folding chairs set in uneven rows, coats draped over backs, bags gathered at people’s feet as if everyone had planned to move again soon and kept postponing it. Adults sat with children angled close, conversations looping, the same phrases repeated with small variations and no endings.

Alicia stepped into it and waited.

A staff member passed her and slowed just long enough to glance at a tablet. The woman’s thumb moved once. Nothing else happened. She continued on without looking back.

Another staff member adjusted a stanchion near the doors, unhooking the strap and letting it retract with a soft snap, opening a narrow channel that hadn’t been there before. People near it didn’t move.

Alicia walked toward it.

As she passed, a man rubbing his knee paused, his hand hovering where it had been moving. A woman murmuring to her daughter lowered her voice until the words flattened into sound.

Near the doors, a woman with a lanyard glanced down at Alicia, then stepped half a pace aside. Her hand touched the door bar only long enough to release it.

The door swung open on its own.

Alicia stepped through.

Outside, the air pressed differently. The building settled behind her. She crossed the edge of the lot and waited because there was space to do so.

The words appeared after that, a single pale line.

OBSERVATION WINDOW ACTIVE

She read it once and kept moving.

Alicia crossed the street where the paint had worn thin and followed the sidewalk until it narrowed and widened again. Halfway down the block, a woman stood in her driveway with a phone pressed to her ear.

“I know,” the woman said. “I know, but they said—”

She stopped when she saw Alicia.

“You shouldn’t be out here by yourself,” the woman said, forcing a smile.

Alicia nodded.

Behind her, the garage door stood open. Boxes were stacked unevenly. A freezer hummed too loudly. A bin of canned food lay tipped on its side.

Alicia knew how long it would last. She knew which houses still had generators. She could have said something.

She didn’t.

The woman waited a moment, then turned back to the phone.

Alicia walked on.

The words appeared later, after the corner, clean and unadorned.

ACHIEVEMENT LOGGED: SELECTIVE NON-INTERVENTION  
TRAIT ADJUSTMENT: PERCEPTION (MINOR)

There was no feeling attached to it.

Alicia adjusted her pace and felt the path ahead clarify.

She didn’t look back.

# Chapter 31 — The Long Way Around

Robert didn’t realize how fast he was walking until Samantha took two shorter steps to stay beside him.

The school doors closed behind them without comment, glass settling into frame, and the noise from inside dropped away all at once. He kept his eyes on the parking lot, keys already in his hand, the metal pressing into his palm until he shifted them and didn’t.

“They said later,” Samantha said.

“I know,” he said, and heard how quickly it came out.

The lot was half full. Cars sat angled across their spaces, some too far forward, some not far enough, as if people had stopped caring about straight lines. A trunk stood open a few rows over. No one was near it. A scrap of paper slid across the asphalt and caught under a tire, tearing when the wind pulled it free again.

Robert unlocked the car before they reached it. The sound carried farther than it should have.

Samantha went around to the passenger side, climbing in on her own, bracing a hand on the door frame as she swung her legs inside. Robert watched long enough to make sure she settled, then got in and shut his door.

He started the engine and pulled out of the lot, the road bending the way it always did, his hands following without instruction. Samantha leaned her head against the window and traced a shape in the fog with her finger, then erased it with the side of her hand and started again.

Robert adjusted his grip on the steering wheel. His jaw ached, and when he forced it loose the movement sent a tight line of sensation down into his neck.

“This isn’t—” he said, and stopped.

The sentence didn’t finish itself.

He took the turn toward the sheriff’s office, corrected halfway through, and felt the irritation spike without anywhere to settle. The building came into view sooner than he expected, low and plain, the flag out front hanging slack against the pole.

He parked near the side and shut the engine off.

“Come on,” he said, opening his door.

Inside, the building smelled faintly of cleaner and old paper. A radio murmured from somewhere out of sight, not loud enough to make out words. The front desk was empty, the chair pushed back at an angle that suggested it hadn’t been meant to stay that way.

They waited.

Samantha leaned against the counter, careful with her ankle. Robert rested his hands on the edge of the laminate, feeling the raised seam where two pieces met and didn’t quite line up.

Footsteps sounded from the hallway. The sheriff appeared with a folder tucked under one arm. He slowed when he saw them, recognition settling in without needing to be named.

“Back again,” he said.

Robert nodded. “I just came from the school.”

The sheriff set the folder down without opening it.

“They wouldn’t release my daughter,” Robert said. “They told me to come back later. Again.”

The sheriff’s mouth tightened slightly, not quite a frown. “I haven’t gotten any updates from them.”

“So you don’t know what’s going on,” Robert said.

The sheriff spread his hands a fraction, then let them fall back to his sides. “I know they’re still sorting things out.”

“She’s been there all day,” Robert said. “They won’t tell me why.”

The radio cut out briefly, then returned with a soft burst of static.

“I can try to get someone on the phone,” the sheriff said. “But I can’t make them release her.”

Robert leaned forward, the edge of the counter pressing into his palms. “I’m not asking you to make them do anything. I’m asking you to tell me why I can’t take my kid home.”

The sheriff didn’t answer right away.

“If they told you she was safe,” he said finally, “then she probably is.”

“Probably,” Robert said.

The sheriff nodded, as if that settled the matter enough to move past it. “They’ve got protocols. Sometimes they don’t communicate well while those are in motion.”

“How long,” Robert said.

“I don’t have that information.”

Robert waited.

Samantha shifted beside him, straightening from the counter. She looked toward the door, then back at Robert, her foot angled slightly off the floor.

The sheriff picked the folder up again. “My advice is to give it some time,” he said. “Check back later.”

Robert nodded once.

The sheriff was already turning away.

Outside, the door closed behind them.

The air pressed flatter against them. Robert let the door swing shut and waited for it to latch before moving.

They walked back toward the car.

Samantha drifted half a step ahead, her pace uneven in a way she didn’t correct. Robert watched her feet, the way her right shoe landed clean and the left hesitated, the ankle stiff enough to change the rhythm without stopping it.

Robert unlocked the car.

Samantha opened the passenger door and braced herself as she climbed in. Her foot slipped slightly on the edge of the seat and she caught herself, quick and practiced.

“Hold on,” he said.

She froze.

He leaned closer and saw it.

The ace wrap from the clinic wasn’t there.

Not loose. Not bunched. Gone entirely, the ankle bare above the sock, a faint indentation still visible where the fabric had pressed earlier, the skin lighter in a thin band that hadn’t had time to fade.

“Where’s the wrap?” he asked.

Samantha looked down, then back at him. “I thought it was still on.”

Robert closed the door carefully. He stood there for a moment longer than necessary, keys still in his hand.

“We need to get another one,” he said.

She nodded and settled into her seat.

They pulled back onto the road and merged into traffic without the radio on. Samantha held her foot slightly off the floor, ankle hovering where it wouldn’t brush against anything.

Cars hesitated at intersections. A delivery truck idled outside a closed restaurant, its back doors shut, the driver nowhere in sight.

Robert stopped at a light and drummed his fingers once against the wheel, then forced his hand still. He thought about turning toward home and didn’t. The pharmacy sign was already visible ahead, lit and steady.

“Does it hurt?” he asked.

“Not really,” Samantha said.

The light changed. He turned into the lot.

Only a few cars sat near the entrance, spaced farther apart than usual. The doors slid open as he slowed, reacting before he fully stopped.

Inside, the lights were on. The air was cool and steady. A basket lay tipped on its side near the entrance. A cart stood in the first aisle with one wheel turned wrong.

Robert stepped in and stopped.

Nothing else moved.

The shelves near the front were thinned unevenly. Pain relievers gone in patches. Cough syrup missing completely. First aid picked over until only the oversized rolls remained, plastic torn at the corners.

He reached for one and turned it in his hand, then put it back and took the next. Samantha leaned against the endcap, her foot lifted slightly off the tile.

At the register, the screen glowed blue. The drawer sat half open, coins scattered where bills had been taken.

Robert moved past it.

The cabinet aisle was farther back.

The glass door stood ajar, the lock twisted sideways. Long stretches of shelf were bare. A few bottles remained, pushed to the back, labels turned away.

Robert stopped.

His back tightened, the ache rising slower this time. He rested his hand on the shelf and breathed once, then again.

He noticed how many were gone.

Not the names.  
The amounts.

The thought slid in before he could stop it, half-formed, already moving. His fingers curled against the metal. He realized he was counting.

He stopped.

Samantha scuffed her shoe lightly against the tile.

Robert straightened. He turned away from the cabinet, picked up the last wrap that would work, and carried it to the register. He waited.

Nothing happened.

He closed the drawer and left the money on the counter.

As they walked back out, the music cut off mid-note.

The doors slid shut behind them.

# Chapter 32 — The Long Way Around

Robert drove like the day was still normal.

Not fast. Not careful. Just the usual speed he’d used a thousand times on these streets, the same turns, the same lights, the same stretch where the road dipped slightly and the trees leaned in and then let go again. Samantha sat in the passenger seat with her foot lifted a little off the floor, ankle hovering so it wouldn’t brush against anything. She watched out the window and didn’t touch the radio.

The neighborhood took longer to arrive than it should have.

Not in miles. In the way the intersections seemed to hold cars for an extra beat, the way people waited at stop signs even when they had the right-of-way, the way a delivery truck sat at the curb outside a closed restaurant with its back doors shut and nobody in the driver’s seat. Robert saw these things and didn’t stop on any of them. His mind moved past them in the same way it moved past construction cones you’d been seeing for weeks.

He thought about home in pieces.

The kitchen light. The bowl by the door. The sound of the garage door when it stuck halfway and then jerked free. The idea of walking in and having Alicia there, the way she would be if this were a day that behaved. He didn’t say it aloud. He didn’t ask Samantha what she thought. The thought sat in him anyway, settling into place as if it belonged there.

A car rolled through a stop sign ahead of him without committing to it, a slow drift that made Robert tap his brake and then let off again. A man stood in his front yard holding a trash bag open and staring down into it as if he was waiting for it to tell him something. Two kids rode bikes in circles at the end of a driveway, looping and looping without changing direction, their tires making the same sound over and over on the pavement.

Robert loosened his grip on the steering wheel and didn’t realize he had been holding it so tight.

Up ahead, the street opened into the familiar split where he usually turned right to take the quicker route home. The light was green. He was already moving through it when something in his peripheral vision made him look left.

A figure.

Down the side street, half blocked by a parked SUV, someone walking along the curb with the steady, unhurried pace of a teenager who wasn’t trying to get anywhere on time.

Robert’s eyes snagged on it and slid away.

He turned his head forward again, hands steady, and told himself it wasn’t her because it didn’t make sense for it to be her. The school had said later. The sheriff had said check back. Later had become the only word anyone seemed willing to offer.

Then the figure stepped past the parked SUV and the light caught the side of her face.

Robert’s foot hit the brake hard enough that Samantha’s shoulder bumped the door.

“What—” she started.

Robert didn’t answer. He turned the wheel left, too late for the turn to feel smooth, the car cutting into the side street at an angle that would have annoyed him on any other day. A horn sounded behind them. He didn’t look.

He pulled over badly, tires too close to the curb on one side and too far from it on the other. The car rocked once as he shifted into park, the motion barely finished before he had the door open.

“Alicia,” he said, already stepping out.

The street smelled like damp leaves and exhaust and something sweet from a yard he didn’t recognize. The sound of his car’s engine idling behind him filled the gap between his words and the next breath.

He crossed the space between them too quickly, the way you moved when you’d been holding your body back for hours and suddenly didn’t have to. The relief hit him first, hot and immediate, bigger than it should have been.

“Alicia,” he said again, softer this time, as if saying it differently would change the shape of what was happening.

She turned.

Not startled. Not flinching. Just the motion of someone responding to a sound that had become relevant. Her eyes settled on him and stayed there.

“Hi,” she said.

The word came out even. Polite. Like she’d run into a neighbor on a walk.

Robert stopped in front of her and realized his hands were empty and didn’t know what to do with them. He wanted to grab her and didn’t. He wanted to look her over for bruises and didn’t. The moment was too narrow for everything he had been carrying.

“Where have you been,” he said, and immediately knew it was the wrong first question.

Alicia’s gaze shifted past him for a second, toward the car, toward the open door.

“With them,” she said.

“With who,” Robert said.

Alicia blinked once, slow. “At the school.”

Robert let out a breath that didn’t feel like relief anymore. “They wouldn’t release you,” he said. He heard himself and tried again. “They wouldn’t release you to me.”

Alicia nodded as if he’d said something factual and finished. “They said later.”

“I went back,” Robert said. The words came out too fast now, the sentence trying to run ahead of itself. “I went back and they said later again. I went to the sheriff. I—”

He stopped because Alicia’s expression didn’t change.

She wasn’t confused. She wasn’t surprised. She didn’t look like she’d been waiting to be rescued.

Behind him, the passenger door creaked. Samantha climbed out slowly, using the frame to steady herself. She shut the door and limped a few steps forward, then stopped, watching.

Alicia’s eyes went to her.

“Hey,” Alicia said. She didn’t move toward her. She didn’t ask what was wrong.

Samantha lifted a hand halfway and let it drop again.

Robert glanced back at Samantha, then back to Alicia, as if he could line the three of them up correctly if he looked fast enough. The street stayed the same. A dog barked once from somewhere behind a fence and then went quiet.

“Get in the car,” Robert said, reaching for the old rhythm like it was a handle he could pull and have things return to place. “We’re going home.”

Alicia didn’t move.

She looked down the street instead, not avoiding him exactly, just tracking something farther away that Robert couldn’t see. Her posture was relaxed in a way that made Robert feel too loud standing there.

“I need you to get in the car,” he said again, hearing the strain now and hating it.

Alicia’s eyes came back to him.

“I’m supposed to—” she said.

The sentence stopped.

Not like she’d forgotten the next word. Like the next word didn’t belong to her. She held his gaze for a moment longer than necessary and then looked past him again, toward the intersection at the end of the street.

Robert waited for the rest of it.

It didn’t arrive.

“What are you supposed to do,” he said.

Alicia’s mouth opened slightly, then closed. She didn’t shrug. She didn’t apologize. She shifted her weight and stepped back once, a small movement that created space where there hadn’t been any.

Robert took a step forward without meaning to. “Alicia—”

She held up one hand.

Not a stop sign. Not a refusal. Just a gesture that made the air between them feel measured.

“I’ll come,” she said, and the words sounded like a compromise that wasn’t for him. “Just—”

She looked down the street again.

Robert followed her gaze and saw nothing that explained it. Just the empty stretch of road, a parked car, a mailbox leaning slightly to one side, a flicker of movement behind a curtain.

Samantha shifted behind him, the scuff of her shoe on the pavement sharp in the quiet.

Robert stood there with the car idling behind him and his older daughter in front of him and no clear place to put either of them.

Alicia stepped around him instead of past him, close enough that he could smell shampoo in her hair and something faint and clean on her clothes, and then she was moving down the sidewalk toward the intersection as if the route had been chosen before she’d spoken.

“Where are you going,” Robert called after her.

Alicia didn’t answer.

She didn’t look back.

He took one step toward Samantha, then stopped.

The engine kept running.

Alicia kept walking.

# Chapter 33 — Down the Block

Alicia walked past the corner without slowing.

The light had just changed. Cars were already moving through the intersection, not fast, not careful, the way people drove when they weren’t sure who was supposed to go first. She stepped off the curb anyway and crossed, her foot landing on the cracked white paint of the crosswalk, then the darker asphalt beyond it. A car rolled through behind her without stopping all the way. The driver looked at her, then past her, his attention already somewhere else.

She reached the other side and kept going.

Behind her, Robert crossed too.

She didn’t look back. She didn’t need to. His footsteps stayed where they’d been since she’d started down the block—close enough that she could hear them when the pavement changed, far enough that she didn’t feel crowded. He wasn’t calling her name now. That was new.

The houses here were closer together. Smaller porches. Fences that leaned in places they hadn’t leaned before. Someone had dragged a couch out onto a front lawn and left it there, half turned, the cushions still wrapped together in cloudy plastic. A wind chime hung from the porch railing and kept starting and stopping, as if it couldn’t remember how the rest of the sound went.

Alicia walked past without looking long enough to decide whether it was new.

At the end of the block, a woman stood in her driveway with a grocery bag open at her feet. The bag was empty. She kept checking inside it anyway, reaching in and pulling her hand back out, like she expected something to appear if she did it enough times. When she noticed Alicia watching, she straightened and folded the bag carefully, smoothing the creases with her palm.

Alicia kept walking.

Robert slowed for a moment behind her. She felt it in the pause of his steps, the slight drag before he caught up again. He didn’t say anything.

The street sloped down toward town. The sidewalks were uneven here, patched and repatched, the concrete darker where it had been poured more recently. Alicia stepped over a storm drain where leaves had collected in a wet, pressed layer. The smell of them lingered in the air, sharp and sweet at the same time.

More people were outside than usual.

A man leaned against his mailbox with his arms crossed, watching the street without looking at anyone in particular. Two women stood at the end of a driveway, their heads close together, voices low enough that Alicia couldn’t catch words, only the shape of them. Someone farther down the block laughed once and then stopped.

Alicia passed them all.

She reached the convenience store and didn’t go in.

The door was propped open with a cinder block. Inside, the lights were on. A man stood in the aisle with his hands on his hips, staring at a shelf that had been picked over twice and then abandoned. Near the front, a little girl sat on the floor with a bag of chips in her lap, eating them one at a time and dropping the empty wrappers back into the bag as if she planned to keep them.

Alicia didn’t slow.

Robert’s footsteps stayed steady behind her. He wasn’t close enough to touch her. He was close enough that any stranger would assume he was there to make sure she didn’t wander off.

The next block opened onto a strip of small businesses. A nail salon. A dry cleaner. A pizza place with the chairs stacked in the window. A hardware store with the metal gate pulled halfway down and then stopped, the bottom edge floating above the sidewalk like someone had been interrupted.

Alicia walked past the hardware store and saw someone inside.

A man moved between the aisles, his shape broken up by shelves. He wasn’t carrying anything. He wasn’t pushing a cart. He walked slowly, stopping now and then to look at the spaces where things should have been.

The gate wasn’t fully closed.

Alicia slowed, just enough to register the gap, and then kept walking.

Behind her, Robert stopped.

She felt it immediately. The absence of sound where his steps had been. The space opening behind her without her asking for it. She didn’t turn around.

Metal scraped softly from inside the store.

Alicia kept going.

After a moment, Robert’s footsteps started again, the rhythm slightly off now, like he’d had to think about it.

They reached the small square with the dry fountain. The basin was empty, leaves gathered in the bottom in a loose spiral. A cluster of people stood near it, not close together, not spread out enough to look casual. A woman in a puffer vest was talking, her hands moving in small, careful gestures.

“If you have extra,” she was saying, “share.”

A man shook his head too quickly. “We don’t have extra.”

Someone laughed and stopped.

Alicia moved around them, skirting the edge of the group. She didn’t join. She didn’t slow enough to be invited.

The street beyond the square led toward the grocery store.

The parking lot in front of it was full and not full at the same time. Cars sat in spaces with too much empty pavement between them, angled slightly wrong. A shopping cart lay on its side near the curb, one wheel turning lazily when the wind caught it.

The automatic doors opened and closed.

Opened. Closed. Opened again.

People moved in and out without rhythm. Some carried bags. Some carried nothing. Some stood just inside the entrance with their hands on cart handles, looking at the shelves visible through the glass.

Alicia stopped short of the line where the doors would have opened for her.

Robert stopped behind her.

“Alicia,” he said quietly.

She watched the entrance.

A man came out carrying two cases of bottled water stacked against his chest, his arms trembling. A woman followed with a cart half full of paper goods, her mouth set tight. A teenage boy held a bag of oranges and stared down into it as if he couldn’t remember why he’d picked it up.

Inside, through the glass, a cereal shelf stood bare except for two boxes pushed to the back.

Robert shifted behind her. “Come on,” he said. “Let’s go home.”

Alicia didn’t answer.

A child came out of the store holding a bag of candy too big for her arms. The bag slipped at the curb and spilled open, bright wrappers scattering across the concrete. The child froze.

Her mother took three more steps before realizing she wasn’t beside her. She turned, bent halfway, reached for one piece of candy, then straightened again. She took the child by the wrist and walked away.

The candy stayed where it was.

Robert drew in a breath behind Alicia, sharp enough that she noticed it even over the sound of the doors. He stepped forward half a pace and stopped.

Alicia didn’t move.

She stayed where she was until the doors slid shut again, until the moment passed, until there was nothing left to wait for.

Then she turned and walked along the front of the building instead of into it.

Robert followed.

They passed the row of carts stacked near the entrance, the line uneven where several were missing. They passed a handwritten sign taped to the window.

NO DELIVERIES TODAY  
LIMIT 2 PER ITEM

The letters were thick and shaky, as if the person who’d written them had pressed too hard.

Alicia didn’t stop at the sign.

The sidewalk curved around the side of the building toward the loading dock. The air smelled different here—wet cardboard, old produce, something sour that had been left too long. A dumpster sat with its lid propped open. Flies hovered in a loose cloud.

A truck was backed up to the dock.

Its doors were shut.

Its engine was off.

It looked like it had arrived and then been forgotten.

Alicia stopped.

Robert stopped behind her.

Neither of them spoke.

A man came around the corner carrying a clipboard. He wore a reflective vest. He slowed when he saw the truck, flipped through the pages, frowned. He took out his phone and listened, turning in a slow circle as if the answer might appear behind him.

He lowered the phone and wrote something on the clipboard.

When he noticed them, his eyes went to Robert first. “Can I help you?” he asked.

Robert stepped forward. “My daughter—” He stopped and looked at Alicia. “We’re fine,” he said instead.

The man nodded too quickly. “Delayed,” he said. “Just delayed.”

He walked away.

A soft metallic click came from the truck.

Robert’s head snapped toward it. He took a step closer to the dock.

Alicia said, “Dad.”

The word was quiet. It landed.

Robert froze with his hand half raised. He lowered it and stepped back.

The sound didn’t come again.

Alicia waited one more beat and then turned away.

She walked past the dumpsters, past a woman digging through one with gloved hands, lifting bags and setting them aside. The woman didn’t look up. She moved like someone doing a chore.

Alicia kept walking.

Robert followed, still on foot, still close, still not shaping her direction.

By the time they reached the next street, Alicia noticed that her breathing felt easier. The space inside her chest didn’t tighten the way it had earlier. She blinked once and caught a brief shimmer at the edge of her vision, like the afterimage of looking at something bright.

She didn’t stop.

She didn’t turn.

She kept walking toward town, and the steps came more easily than they had a block ago.

# Chapter 34 — At the Intersection

Alicia was ahead of them, far enough that Robert had to keep his eyes on her to make sure she didn’t turn off somewhere without him noticing. She moved easily through the sidewalk traffic, slipping between people without stopping, her head angled forward as if she already knew which gaps would open. Robert stayed a few steps back with Samantha, his hand hovering near her shoulder when the crowd pressed too close, dropping again when it didn’t, and the street grew louder as they approached the intersection.

Engines idled. Someone leaned on a horn and then let off, embarrassed by it. A delivery van sat half into the crosswalk with its blinker ticking steadily, the sound cutting through the low murmur of voices and movement. People stood at the corners in loose clumps, some facing the street, some turned sideways, watching things that weren’t happening. Alicia slowed near the curb and Robert slowed too, the light changing without anyone moving right away.

That was when Robert heard the voice, not loud enough to rise above the noise, just threaded through it. “Hey,” it said, followed by a pause that made it hard to tell if the word had been meant to go anywhere else. “Can you—”

Robert turned his head toward the sound. It came from the far side of the intersection, near the curb where the pavement dipped and water sometimes collected after rain. A man sat on the ground there with his back against the pole of a street sign, one leg bent awkwardly beneath him, one hand resting on the asphalt as if he’d put it there to steady himself and then forgotten about it. Cars idled around him. People stood close enough that Robert could see their faces clearly, a woman with a stroller caught mid-step at the edge of the crosswalk, a man in a work jacket leaning against a parking meter with his arms folded, watching without expression.

“Hey,” the voice said again, softer this time. “I just— I can’t—”

Alicia stepped off the curb and Robert felt the shift immediately, moving after her with his attention narrowing. Samantha stayed close, her limp more pronounced now that they’d slowed. Alicia crossed halfway into the street and stopped, standing there with her weight settled evenly as she looked toward the man on the ground, waiting in a way that made Robert uneasy.

Robert stepped past her without meaning to. “Sir,” he said, raising his voice just enough to carry. “Are you hurt?” The man lifted his head with visible effort, his eyes focusing on Robert slowly. “I fell,” he said, then stopped, swallowing. “I just need a minute,” he added, though it sounded like a question.

Robert took another step into the street as a car crept forward and stopped again, the driver’s hands tight on the wheel. Someone farther back leaned on a horn, short and sharp, then cut it off. A woman near Robert shook her head without looking at him. “They already checked,” she said.
“Who did?” Robert asked.
She shrugged. “Someone.”

A man near the curb cleared his throat. “He said he was fine.”
The man on the ground didn’t say anything.

Robert turned back to him. “Can you stand?” he asked. “Do you want me to call an ambulance?” The man’s gaze drifted past Robert to the stopped cars and watching faces. “I don’t want trouble,” he said, and Robert felt heat rise in his chest.

“This isn’t trouble,” he said. “You fell in the street.”

# Chapter 37 — Directions

# Chapter 38 — What People Do

Sam made a face at that, the kind that said she didn’t want to yell for anything, but she nodded anyway.

The street was full in a way it hadn’t been the day before. Not packed exactly. No one was shoulder-to-shoulder. Someone had parked too close to the corner and nobody said anything about it. A dog barked from a yard and kept barking, like it had figured out that the usual rules didn’t apply.

“Okay,” she said, too loud, not because she was trying to command attention, but because she’d been drowned out already and didn’t have another way to be heard. “One at a time.”

No one did one at a time.

Food came up first, or maybe it was the first thing Robert caught. He heard deliveries and trucks and two days in the same breath, and then someone saying, “If they don’t come today—”

“They will,” another voice said, immediately, like that was a prayer you said out loud to keep it true.

“Based on what?” someone else asked.

No one answered that part.

“Two,” Robert said. “One’s injured.”

Someone else said, “Fair doesn’t feed people.”

“The sheriff,” she said.

That landed, not like an answer, more like a place to throw the problem.

“He won’t do anything,” a man said.

“He has to,” another voice replied. “It’s literally his job.”

Robert got Sam down the front steps and into the passenger seat. It took longer than it should have because everything took longer when you were trying not to hurt someone. He shut her door and hurried around to his side, heart thudding in that stupid way it did when you were late for something you hadn’t agreed to.

He started the engine and pulled out as two cars passed in front of him, people inside gesturing wildly as they talked.

Sam watched the movement through the window. “Where are we going?” she asked.

“The sheriff’s,” Robert said.

Someone tried to talk about food and got cut off by someone talking about the heart-attack man and then someone else talking about the school. It was all the same tone—urgent without clarity, angry without a target.

The sheriff was there. He stood behind the counter with his hands braced on it, watching the room like he was trying to identify a single problem he could address.

He let them talk.

He let them burn through themselves, the way storms did when there was nowhere for the wind to go.

When the noise finally thinned enough for his voice to fit into it, he said, “Nothing’s been declared.”

People stared at him.

“I don’t have authorization for coordinated action,” he continued. “Procedures don’t say it’s time yet.”

For a second no one spoke, as if they were waiting for the next part, the part where he offered something else.

There was no next part.

“So we just wait?” someone asked.

# Chapter 42 — Handling It

The sheriff’s office didn’t empty after the sheriff said to keep calm.

People stayed because leaving felt like admitting something had already been decided without them, and no one wanted to be the first person to do that. The arguments thinned but didn’t stop. They dropped into lower registers, repeated themselves, circled. Someone near the counter kept saying, “So we just wait,” like if the sentence got said enough times it might turn into instructions.

Robert stood with Samantha near the doorway, not fully inside, not fully out. He’d put her there on purpose, close enough to leave quickly if they needed to, far enough back that she wasn’t getting jostled every time someone gestured too hard. She leaned into him more than she probably meant to, her ankle forcing her weight to one side. He adjusted without looking, the movement automatic now.

She was watching faces. Not the sheriff. The faces.

The door opened again.

At first, nothing happened. Someone finished a sentence. Someone else started another. The noise didn’t stop so much as tilt, like a radio losing signal for a half second and then settling on a different station.

Robert felt it before he saw anything change. A small, unpleasant drop in his stomach, like missing a step you didn’t know was there.

Then he noticed the three people standing just inside the doorway.

They weren’t deputies. They weren’t teachers. They weren’t anyone Robert recognized, and that bothered him more than if they had been. Their clothes were plain in a way that didn’t call attention to itself—clean, practical, unremarkable. No badges visible. No bags. No papers. They didn’t push forward or raise their voices. They just stood there, waiting, like the room would eventually catch up to them.

The sheriff looked at them.

He didn’t straighten. He didn’t greet them. His hands came off the counter and rested at his sides, like he’d been interrupted in the middle of something that no longer mattered.

Someone near Robert whispered, “Who are they?”

Another voice, uncertain, answered, “They’re… with the lab. I think.”

The word lab didn’t ripple the way school would have. It didn’t bring up faces or names or familiarity. It landed heavy instead, undefined, and stayed there.

One of the women stepped forward just enough that she didn’t have to raise her voice.

“We’re aware of what’s happening,” she said.

The room quieted, not fully, but enough that Robert realized how loud it had been before.

“This is being handled,” she continued. “There’s no need for a public gathering.”

Someone tried to jump in immediately, and another voice cut across it, sharper, frustrated. The woman waited. She didn’t hurry them. She didn’t look annoyed. She let the words trip over each other until they collapsed into their own silence.

“I understand people are concerned,” she said. “But uncoordinated groups create confusion. They interfere with assessment.”

Assessment.

Robert found himself thinking about the notebook out on the folding table in the street. The half-finished list. The way the woman writing it had stopped once no one could agree what the list was even for. Assessment sounded like something that didn’t care whether the list made sense.

A man near the front scoffed. “So what, we go home and pretend this is normal?”

“No one is asking you to pretend,” the woman said. “We’re asking you to return home and allow the process to continue.”

The sheriff cleared his throat.

For a second Robert thought he was going to say something—anything—but instead he glanced at the woman, nodded once, and fell quiet. It wasn’t dramatic. It was worse than that. It was the sound of a decision being accepted before anyone had admitted it was being made.

Robert felt something tighten in his chest, sharp and unpleasant. He’d come here because this was where you came when things didn’t make sense. Watching the sheriff step back without being told made that instinct feel naïve.

Another of the three—the man—checked his phone. The motion was small, almost careless, but it pulled Robert’s attention anyway.

“If action is required,” the man said, still looking at the screen, “you’ll be notified through established channels.”

“By who?” someone asked.

The man looked up briefly, as if the question had arrived from a slightly different angle than expected. “Appropriate parties,” he said, and looked back down.

People began to leave.

Not all at once. One woman near the wall first, then a man by the door. A couple followed, not meeting anyone’s eyes as they slipped past. No one told them to go. Staying just began to feel like the wrong choice.

Sam’s fingers tightened in Robert’s sleeve. He shifted instinctively, giving her more of his weight to lean on.

Someone near the doorway muttered, “So that’s it.”

“For now,” the woman said, polite and final.

The room thinned. Voices faded into the hallway and then outside. The pressure eased without resolving, like something being deflated before it was finished taking shape.

The three of them stayed where they were.

The sheriff stepped back from the counter, not hurried, not reluctant, just done.

In the quiet that followed, Robert became aware of the ticking clock on the wall, louder now that there was nothing else competing with it. He glanced up without thinking.

Then the man spoke again.

“It’s ten forty.”

The words landed oddly. Not wrong exactly. Just misplaced.

Robert looked back at the clock. The numbers didn’t line up with what the man had said. He stared at them for a second longer than necessary, waiting for something to correct itself—his sense of time, the clock, the moment. Nothing did.

The man slipped his phone back into his pocket, unconcerned, as if the room had already adjusted around the time he was carrying rather than the other way around.

Robert stayed where he was, Sam warm and unsteady against his side, and tried to trace the moment when the room had stopped belonging to the people in it.

# Chapter 43 — Already Handled

Robert drove to the clinic the same way he had the day before.

He kept both hands on the wheel and braked early, more out of habit than care, because Sam was sitting sideways again with her leg pulled up and he didn’t want to feel her tense when the car slowed. She stared out the window instead of at the road. When the car hit a seam in the pavement she sucked in a breath and then let it out slowly, like she’d practiced.

“You okay?” he asked.

“I’m okay,” Sam said.

He waited.

“It hurts,” she added.

The parking lot wasn’t full, but it wasn’t empty either. A truck idled near the curb with no one inside it. Someone sat in the driver’s seat of a sedan scrolling on their phone, thumb moving fast. No one looked up when Robert parked.

The waiting room wasn’t.

“Name?”

He told her.

He answered.

People moved through the room in uneven bursts. A couple came in carrying a toddler who was crying hard enough that his face had gone red, and they were taken through a door almost immediately. A man with his hand wrapped in a towel stood near the wall for a while, then was waved forward without anyone saying his name. Two teenagers came in together, whispered, then turned around and left.

He went back to the counter.

“Sorry,” he said, because that word came out automatically. “We’ve been waiting.”

“Name?”

He told her again. His voice sounded thinner than he expected.

She glanced down, pen following a line on the page.

“She’s already been handled,” she said.

“We were here yesterday,” he said.

“Yes,” she said.

“With what,” he said, and the words came out too fast.

Monitoring.

“Monitoring,” he repeated.

“Yes.”

“If there’s a change,” she said, “you’ll be notified.”

“By who,” he asked, sharper than he meant to be.

She paused. Not long. Just long enough.

“You can take a seat,” she said.

He didn’t move.

“She’s right there,” he said, gesturing without turning around. “She’s sitting right there.”

The woman’s eyes flicked past him and back.

“She’s already been assessed,” she said.

Handled. Assessed. The words kept coming back around, never landing on anything he could grab.

“Can we talk to someone else,” he said. “A doctor. A nurse.”

“If further intervention is required,” she said, “you’ll be notified.”

That again.

Robert opened his mouth to say something else and couldn’t find where to start. Everything he thought of sounded wrong before it reached his tongue. He stood there long enough that the space between them started to feel noticeable.

Behind him, Sam shifted. Just a small sound.

He turned.

Her face had gone pale. The ankle was bigger than it had been when they came in. He didn’t need to crouch to see it. He didn’t need to touch it.

He looked back at the counter.

The woman was already writing again.

He waited for something to happen. For someone to say something. For the pen to stop.

“We’re going home,” he said.

Outside, the light was too bright.

Robert opened the car door one-handed and lowered her into the seat, careful with her ankle, then shut the door and stood there for a moment before walking around to the driver’s side.
